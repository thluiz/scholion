**Walter Bendix Schönflies Benjamin** ( [_BEN\-yə-min_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); German: [\[ˈvaltɐ ˈbɛnjamiːn\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/7/7d/De-Walter_Benjamin.ogg/De-Walter_Benjamin.ogg.mp3 "Play audio")<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Walter_Benjamin.ogg" title="File:De-Walter Benjamin.ogg">ⓘ</a></sup>;<sup id="cite_ref-6"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-6"><span>[</span>6<span>]</span></a></sup> 15 July 1892 – 26 September 1940)<sup id="cite_ref-Witte_7-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Witte-7"><span>[</span>7<span>]</span></a></sup> was a German-Jewish [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher "Philosopher"), [cultural critic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_critic "Cultural critic"), [media theorist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_theorist "Media theorist"), and [essayist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essayist "Essayist"). An eclectic thinker who combined elements of [German idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_idealism "German idealism"), [Jewish mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_mysticism "Jewish mysticism"), [Western Marxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Marxism "Western Marxism"), and [post-Kantianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Kantianism "Neo-Kantianism"), he made contributions to the [philosophy of history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_history "Philosophy of history"), [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics"), [historical materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_materialism "Historical materialism"), [criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criticism "Criticism"), [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics "Aesthetics") and had an oblique but overwhelmingly influential impact on the resurrection of the [Kabbalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabbalah "Kabbalah") by virtue of his life-long epistolary relationship with [Gershom Scholem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gershom_Scholem "Gershom Scholem").<sup id="cite_ref-:15_8-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:15-8"><span>[</span>8<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-9"><span>[</span>9<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-10"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-10"><span>[</span>10<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-11"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-11"><span>[</span>11<span>]</span></a></sup>

In popular culture and left journalism he often appears as an exemplar whose experience is representative of the tragedy of German-Jewish intellectuals under the Third Reich.<sup id="cite_ref-12"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-12"><span>[</span>12<span>]</span></a></sup>

Of the hidden principle organizing Walter Benjamin's thought [Scholem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gershom_Scholem "Gershom Scholem") wrote unequivocally that "Benjamin was a philosopher",<sup id="cite_ref-:8_13-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:8-13"><span>[</span>13<span>]</span></a></sup> while his younger colleagues Arendt<sup id="cite_ref-:9_14-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:9-14"><span>[</span>14<span>]</span></a></sup> and Adorno<sup id="cite_ref-:10_15-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:10-15"><span>[</span>15<span>]</span></a></sup> contend that he was "not a philosopher".<sup id="cite_ref-:9_14-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:9-14"><span>[</span>14<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:10_15-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:10-15"><span>[</span>15<span>]</span></a></sup> Scholem remarked "The peculiar aura of authority emanating from his work tended to incite contradiction".<sup id="cite_ref-:8_13-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:8-13"><span>[</span>13<span>]</span></a></sup> Benjamin himself considered his research to be [theological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theological "Theological"),<sup id="cite_ref-:11_16-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:11-16"><span>[</span>16<span>]</span></a></sup> though he eschewed all recourse to traditionally metaphysical sources of transcendentally revealed authority.<sup id="cite_ref-:9_14-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:9-14"><span>[</span>14<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:11_16-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:11-16"><span>[</span>16<span>]</span></a></sup>

He was associated with the [Frankfurt School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_School "Frankfurt School") and also maintained formative relationships with thinkers and cultural figures such as [playwright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playwright "Playwright") [Bertolt Brecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht "Bertolt Brecht") (friend), [Martin Buber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Buber "Martin Buber") (an early impresario in his career), Nazi constitutionalist [Carl Schmitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Schmitt "Carl Schmitt") (a rival), and many others.<sup id="cite_ref-17"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-17"><span>[</span>17<span>]</span></a></sup> He was related to German [political theorist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_theory "Political theory") and philosopher [Hannah Arendt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_Arendt "Hannah Arendt") through her first marriage, though his friendship with her flowered in Paris after she separated from her husband (Benjamin’s cousin [Günther Anders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%BCnther_Anders "Günther Anders")). Both Arendt and Anders were students of [Martin Heidegger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Heidegger "Martin Heidegger"), whom Benjamin considered a nemesis.<sup id="cite_ref-18"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-18"><span>[</span>18<span>]</span></a></sup>

Among Benjamin's best known works are the essays "[The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Work_of_Art_in_the_Age_of_Mechanical_Reproduction "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction")" (1935), and "[Theses on the Philosophy of History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theses_on_the_Philosophy_of_History "Theses on the Philosophy of History")" (1940). His major work as a critic included essays on [Kafka,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka "Franz Kafka") [Baudelaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Baudelaire "Charles Baudelaire"), [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe "Johann Wolfgang von Goethe"), [Kraus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Kraus_(writer) "Karl Kraus (writer)"), [Leskov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Leskov "Nikolai Leskov"), [Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Proust "Marcel Proust"), [Walser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Walser_(writer) "Robert Walser (writer)"), [Trauerspiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trauerspiel "Trauerspiel") and [translation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_theory "Translation theory"). He translated the _Tableaux Parisiens_ section of Baudelaire's _[Les Fleurs du mal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Fleurs_du_mal "Les Fleurs du mal")_ and parts of Proust's _[À la recherche du temps perdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Search_of_Lost_Time "In Search of Lost Time")_.

In 1940, at the age of 48, Benjamin died during his flight into exile on the French–Spanish border while attempting to escape the advance of the [Third Reich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany").<sup id="cite_ref-19"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-19"><span>[</span>19<span>]</span></a></sup> Having remained in Europe until it was too late, as [Cynthia Ozick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynthia_Ozick "Cynthia Ozick") puts it, Benjamin took his own life to avoid being murdered as a Jew.<sup id="cite_ref-:15_8-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:15-8"><span>[</span>8<span>]</span></a></sup> "Impressed and shaken by his death, the Spanish authorities allowed Benjamin's companions to continue their travel" into Spain by which route they were able to escape the Third Reich.<sup id="cite_ref-20"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-20"><span>[</span>20<span>]</span></a></sup>

Though popular acclaim eluded him during his life, the decades following his death won his work posthumous renown.<sup id="cite_ref-21"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-21"><span>[</span>21<span>]</span></a></sup> Some German readers and academics encountered Benjamin after his Complete Works began to be released by [Suhrkamp Verlag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suhrkamp_Verlag "Suhrkamp Verlag") in 1955, but global acclaim came to him when his works were translated into English and introduced to a reading public in the [Anglosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglosphere "Anglosphere") by Hannah Arendt in 1968.<sup id="cite_ref-22"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-22"><span>[</span>22<span>]</span></a></sup>

### Early life and education

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Walter_Benjamin&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Early life and education")\]

Walter Benjamin and his younger siblings, Georg (1895–1942) and [Dora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dora_Benjamin "Dora Benjamin") (1901–1946), were born to a wealthy business family of assimilated [Ashkenazi Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jews "Ashkenazi Jews") in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin"), then the capital of the [German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire "German Empire"). Walter's father, Emil Benjamin, was a banker in Paris who had relocated from France to Germany,<sup id="cite_ref-:5_23-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:5-23"><span>[</span>23<span>]</span></a></sup> where he worked as an antiques trader and art dealer; marrying Pauline Schönflies.<sup id="cite_ref-:5_23-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:5-23"><span>[</span>23<span>]</span></a></sup> He owned a number of investments in Berlin, including ice skating rinks.<sup id="cite_ref-:5_23-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:5-23"><span>[</span>23<span>]</span></a></sup>

Walter's uncle, [William Stern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Stern_(psychologist) "William Stern (psychologist)"), was a prominent German [child psychologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_psychology "Child psychology") who developed the concept of the [intelligence quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_quotient "Intelligence quotient") (IQ).<sup id="cite_ref-:5_23-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:5-23"><span>[</span>23<span>]</span></a></sup> He also had a cousin, [Günther Anders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%BCnther_Anders "Günther Anders"),<sup id="cite_ref-:5_23-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:5-23"><span>[</span>23<span>]</span></a></sup> a German philosopher and anti-nuclear activist who studied under [Edmund Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl "Edmund Husserl") and [Martin Heidegger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Heidegger "Martin Heidegger"). Through his mother, Walter's great-uncle was the classical archaeologist [Gustav Hirschfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Hirschfeld "Gustav Hirschfeld").<sup id="cite_ref-:5_23-5"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:5-23"><span>[</span>23<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-24"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-24"><span>[</span>24<span>]</span></a></sup>

In 1901, eight-year-old Walter was enrolled at the Kaiser Friedrich School in [Charlottenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlottenburg "Charlottenburg"); he completed his secondary school studies ten years later. In his youth, Walter was of fragile health and so in 1905 the family sent him to Hermann-Lietz-Schule Haubinda,<sup id="cite_ref-25"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-25"><span>[</span>25<span>]</span></a></sup> part of the [German rural boarding school movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_rural_boarding_school_movement "German rural boarding school movement") in the [Thuringian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thuringia "Thuringia") countryside, for two years; in 1907, having returned to Berlin, he resumed his schooling at the Kaiser Friedrich School.<sup id="cite_ref-Witte_7-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Witte-7"><span>[</span>7<span>]</span></a></sup>

In 1912, at the age of 20, he enrolled at the [University of Freiburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Freiburg "University of Freiburg"), but at the summer semester's end, he returned to Berlin and matriculated at the [University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humboldt_University_of_Berlin "Humboldt University of Berlin") to continue studying philosophy. There, Benjamin had his first exposure to [Zionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zionism "Zionism"), which had not been part of his liberal upbringing. This gave him occasion to formulate his own ideas about the meaning of Judaism. Benjamin distanced himself from political and nationalist Zionism, instead developing in his own thinking what he called a kind of "[cultural Zionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Zionism "Cultural Zionism")"—an attitude that recognized and promoted [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism "Judaism") and [Jewish ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_ethics "Jewish ethics"). In Benjamin's formulation, his Jewishness meant a commitment to the furtherance of European culture. He wrote: "My life experience led me to this insight: the Jews represent an elite in the ranks of the spiritually active ... For Judaism is to me in no sense an end in itself, but the most distinguished bearer and representative of the spiritual."<sup id="cite_ref-26"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-26"><span>[</span>26<span>]</span></a></sup> This was a position Benjamin largely held lifelong.<sup id="cite_ref-27"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-27"><span>[</span>27<span>]</span></a></sup>

It was as a speaker and debater in the milieu of the [Gustav Wyneken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Wyneken "Gustav Wyneken")'s [German Youth Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Youth_Movement "German Youth Movement") that Benjamin was first encountered by [Gershom Scholem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gershom_Scholem "Gershom Scholem") and later [Martin Buber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Buber "Martin Buber") although he had parted ways with the youth group before they had become properly acquainted.<sup id="cite_ref-28"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-28"><span>[</span>28<span>]</span></a></sup> Elected president of the _Freie Studentenschaft_ (Free Students Association), Benjamin wrote essays arguing for educational and general cultural change while working alongside Wyneken at the legendary and controversial youth magazine _Der Anfang_ (The beginning), that was banned in all schools in Bavaria. Wyneken's thesis that a new youth must pave the way for revolutionary cultural change became the main theme of all of Benjamin's publications at that time.<sup id="cite_ref-29"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-29"><span>[</span>29<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-30"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-30"><span>[</span>30<span>]</span></a></sup> When not reelected as student association president, he returned to Freiburg to study, with particular attention to the lectures of [Heinrich Rickert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Rickert "Heinrich Rickert"); at that time he traveled to France and Italy.

Benjamin's attempt to volunteer for service at the outbreak of [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") in August 1914 was rejected by the army.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_31-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:1-31"><span>[</span>31<span>]</span></a></sup> Benjamin later feigned illnesses to avoid conscription,<sup id="cite_ref-:1_31-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:1-31"><span>[</span>31<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Eberg2018_32-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Eberg2018-32"><span>[</span>32<span>]</span></a></sup> allowing him to continue his studies and his translations of works by French poet [Charles Baudelaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Baudelaire "Charles Baudelaire"). His conspicuous refuge in Switzerland on dubious medical grounds was a likely factor in his ongoing challenges in obtaining academic employment after the war.<sup id="cite_ref-Eberg2018_32-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Eberg2018-32"><span>[</span>32<span>]</span></a></sup>

The next year, 1915, Benjamin moved to Munich, and continued his schooling at the [University of Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Munich "University of Munich"), where he met [Rainer Maria Rilke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainer_Maria_Rilke "Rainer Maria Rilke")<sup id="cite_ref-Faber_and_Faber_33-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Faber_and_Faber-33"><span>[</span>33<span>]</span></a></sup> and Scholem; the latter became a friend. Intensive discussions with Scholem about Judaism and Jewish mysticism gave the impetus for the 1916 text (surviving as a manuscript) _Über Sprache überhaupt und über die Sprache des Menschen_ ("[On Language as Such and on the Language of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Language_as_Such_and_on_the_Language_of_Man "On Language as Such and on the Language of Man")"), which, as Benjamin said to Scholem , "has an immanent relationship to Judaism and to the first chapter of the [Genesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Genesis "Book of Genesis")".<sup id="cite_ref-34"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-34"><span>[</span>34<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-35"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-35"><span>[</span>35<span>]</span></a></sup> In that period, Benjamin wrote about the 18th-century [Romantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism "Romanticism") German poet [Friedrich Hölderlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_H%C3%B6lderlin "Friedrich Hölderlin").<sup id="cite_ref-36"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-36"><span>[</span>36<span>]</span></a></sup>

In 1917 Benjamin transferred to the [University of Bern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bern "University of Bern"); there he met [Ernst Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Bloch "Ernst Bloch"), and Dora Sophie Pollak<sup id="cite_ref-37"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-37"><span>[</span>37<span>]</span></a></sup> (née Kellner), whom he married. They had a son, Stefan Rafael, in 1918. In 1919 Benjamin earned his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") _[summa cum laude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_cum_laude "Summa cum laude")_ with the dissertation _Der Begriff der Kunstkritik in der deutschen Romantik_ (_The Concept of Art Criticism in German Romanticism_).<sup id="cite_ref-38"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-38"><span>[</span>38<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-39"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-39"><span>[</span>39<span>]</span></a></sup>

For his [postdoctoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral "Postdoctoral") thesis in 1920, Benjamin hit upon an idea very similar to the thesis proposed by [Martin Heidegger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Heidegger "Martin Heidegger") in the latter's own postdoctoral project (_Duns Scotus: Theory of Categories and Meaning_).<sup id="cite_ref-40"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-40"><span>[</span>40<span>]</span></a></sup> Wolfram Eilenberger writes that Benjamin's plan was "to legitimize \[his theory of language\] with reference to a largely forgotten tradition \[found in the archaic writings of [Duns Scotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duns_Scotus "Duns Scotus")\], and to strike the sparks of systematization from the apparent disjunct among modern, logical, and analytical linguistic philosophy and medieval speculations on language that fell under the heading of theology".<sup id="cite_ref-Eberg2018_32-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Eberg2018-32"><span>[</span>32<span>]</span></a></sup> After Scholem sympathetically informed his friend that his interest in the concept had been pre-empted by Heidegger's earlier publication,<sup id="cite_ref-41"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-41"><span>[</span>41<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-42"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-42"><span>[</span>42<span>]</span></a></sup> Benjamin seemed to have derived a lifelong antagonism toward the rival philosopher whose major insights, over the course of both of their careers, sometimes overlapped and sometimes conflicted with Benjamin's.<sup id="cite_ref-Eberg2018_32-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Eberg2018-32"><span>[</span>32<span>]</span></a></sup>

Later, unable to support himself and family, Benjamin returned to Berlin and resided with his parents. In 1921 he published the essay "[Zur Kritik der Gewalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critique_of_Violence "Critique of Violence")" ("Toward the Critique of Violence"). At this time Benjamin first became socially acquainted with [Leo Strauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Strauss "Leo Strauss"), and he remained an admirer of Strauss and his work throughout his life.<sup id="cite_ref-43"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-43"><span>[</span>43<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:3_44-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:3-44"><span>[</span>44<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-45"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-45"><span>[</span>45<span>]</span></a></sup>

Starting in adolescence, in a trend of episodic behavior that was to remain true throughout his life, Benjamin was a maven within an important community during a critically important historical period: the left-[intelligentsia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligentsia "Intelligentsia") of [interwar Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interwar_period "Interwar period") and [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interwar_France "Interwar France"). Acquaintance with Walter Benjamin was a connecting thread for a variety of major figures in metaphysics, philosophy, theology, the visual arts, theater, literature, radio, politics and various other domains. Benjamin happened to be present on the outskirts of many of the most important events within the intellectual ferment of the [interwar-period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interwar_period "Interwar period") in [Weimar Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Republic "Weimar Republic") and interpreted those events in his writing.

He was in the crowd at [the conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Conference_on_the_Epistemology_of_the_Exact_Sciences "Second Conference on the Epistemology of the Exact Sciences") where [Kurt Gödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_G%C3%B6del "Kurt Gödel") first described the [incompleteness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del%27s_incompleteness_theorems "Gödel's incompleteness theorems").<sup id="cite_ref-46"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-46"><span>[</span>46<span>]</span></a></sup> He once took a class on the [Ancient Mayans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization "Maya civilization") from [Rainer Maria Rilke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainer_Maria_Rilke "Rainer Maria Rilke").<sup id="cite_ref-Faber_and_Faber_33-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Faber_and_Faber-33"><span>[</span>33<span>]</span></a></sup> He attended the same seminar as Heidegger at Freiburg in the summer of 1913 when both men were still university students: concepts first encountered there influenced their thought for the remainder of their careers. He was an early draft script reader, comrade, favorable critic and promoter as well as a frequent house-guest of the [Berlin cabaret theater scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_culture "Weimar culture") writer and director [Bertolt Brecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht "Bertolt Brecht"). [Martin Buber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Buber "Martin Buber") took an interest in Benjamin, but Benjamin declined to contribute to Buber's journal because it was too esoteric.<sup id="cite_ref-47"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-47"><span>[</span>47<span>]</span></a></sup> Nevertheless, Buber financed Benjamin's trip to Moscow and promoted his career in other ways. Buber commissioned Benjamin to write an article Moscow for his _Die Kreatur,_ though Benjamin missed his deadline for the delivery of this piece by several years.

Benjamin was a close colleague of [Ernst Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Bloch "Ernst Bloch") while Bloch was writing the _Spirit of Utopia_ and maintained a relationship with him until the late 20's that Bloch later described as "almost too close."<sup id="cite_ref-48"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-48"><span>[</span>48<span>]</span></a></sup> An untitled scrap omitted from Benjamin's book review of Bloch's _Spirit of Utopia_ which remained unpublished during Benjamin's lifetime (later anthologized under the title "Theologico-Political Fragment") is now perhaps better remembered than the larger work it cites as an authority for its mystical reflections.<sup id="cite_ref-49"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-49"><span>[</span>49<span>]</span></a></sup> It was Bloch's commission that inspired Benjamin's work on [the theory of categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_being "Category of being"), according to Scholem.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_44-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:3-44"><span>[</span>44<span>]</span></a></sup> This was to be a consequential theme throughout his career.

One of Benjamin's high-school best friends (also a German Jew) killed himself using gas at the outbreak of the first World War; another was one of the Jewish Liaisons who took Nazi diplomats on a tour of Palestine. This happened while the Third Reich was preparing the European Zionists to believe that Europe's Jews would be forcibly emigrated from the Reich, to deflect attention from the looming possibility of the strategy that was ultimately adopted: [mass extermination in the death camps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust "The Holocaust").<sup id="cite_ref-50"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-50"><span>[</span>50<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-51"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-51"><span>[</span>51<span>]</span></a></sup> Scholem, Benjamin's oldest friend, and the sole executor of his literary estate, would resurrect the canonical books of the Kabbalah from private libraries and ancient document dumps called [Genizah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genizah "Genizah").<sup id="cite_ref-:6_52-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:6-52"><span>[</span>52<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:7_53-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:7-53"><span>[</span>53<span>]</span></a></sup> These were created when the books flooded into [Mandatory Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandatory_Palestine "Mandatory Palestine") during the period leading up to, coinciding with, and immediately following the [Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust "The Holocaust").<sup id="cite_ref-54"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-54"><span>[</span>54<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:6_52-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:6-52"><span>[</span>52<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:7_53-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:7-53"><span>[</span>53<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-55"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-55"><span>[</span>55<span>]</span></a></sup>

In 1923, when the [Institute for Social Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Frankfurt_Institute_for_Social_Research "University of Frankfurt Institute for Social Research") was founded, later to become home to the [Frankfurt School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_School "Frankfurt School"), Benjamin published _Charles Baudelaire, Tableaux Parisiens_. At this time he became acquainted with [Theodor Adorno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Adorno "Theodor Adorno") and befriended [Georg Lukács](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Luk%C3%A1cs "Georg Lukács"), whose _The Theory of the Novel_ (1920) influenced him. Meanwhile, [inflation in the Weimar Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_in_the_Weimar_Republic "Inflation in the Weimar Republic") after the war made it difficult for Emil Benjamin to continue supporting his son's family. At the end of 1923 Scholem emigrated to Palestine, then under a British mandate; despite repeated invitations, he failed to persuade Benjamin (and family) to leave the continent for the Middle East.

In 1924 [Hugo von Hofmannsthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_von_Hofmannsthal "Hugo von Hofmannsthal"), in the _Neue Deutsche Beiträge_ magazine, published Benjamin's "Goethes Wahlverwandtschaften" ("[Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe "Goethe")'s [Elective Affinities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elective_Affinities "Elective Affinities")"), about Goethe's third novel, _[Die Wahlverwandtschaften](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elective_Affinities "Elective Affinities")_ (1809). According to literary critic Burkhardt Lindner, the essay forms the "third major philosophical-aesthetic treatise of the early work" alongside the PhD dissertation and the [habilitation thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation_thesis "Habilitation thesis"). It has often been linked to the breakup of his marriage. The dedication to Julia Cohn, whom he had courted in vain at the time, suggests this.<sup id="cite_ref-56"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-56"><span>[</span>56<span>]</span></a></sup>

Likewise, according to [Hannah Arendt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_Arendt "Hannah Arendt"), it was his essay on Goethe that ruined Benjamin's only chance of a university career.<sup id="cite_ref-:14_57-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:14-57"><span>[</span>57<span>]</span></a></sup> Benjamin's Goethe monograph is partly a meditation on the form 'free-love' that the Benjamins were experimenting with in their marriage at this time, amongst other things. But this was only tangential to the issue that led to the controversy to which Arendt refers. His mistake (per Arendt) was killing a sacred cow from amongst the academic establishment.<sup id="cite_ref-:14_57-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:14-57"><span>[</span>57<span>]</span></a></sup> As so often in Benjamin's writings, his study of Goethe's _Elective Affinities_ was marked by polemics and the theme of his assault in this work concerned [Friedrich Gundolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Gundolf "Friedrich Gundolf")'s Goethe book. Gundolf was the most prominent and able academic member of the [(Stefan) George-Kreis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George-Kreis "George-Kreis")\--a cult of post-symbolist, romantic nationalist poets with a mystically conservative, medievalist bent.<sup id="cite_ref-:14_57-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:14-57"><span>[</span>57<span>]</span></a></sup> Elsewhere, in the anonymity of his private epistolary writings, Benjamin explicitly points out how (regardless of the ultimate horror, withdrawal and rejection with which members of the circle greeted [the Nazi regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany")) this group's commitment to particular archaic styles anticipated the aesthetics of [fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascism "Fascism").<sup id="cite_ref-58"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-58"><span>[</span>58<span>]</span></a></sup>

Later that year Benjamin and Bloch resided on the Italian island of [Capri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capri "Capri"); Benjamin wrote _Ursprung des deutschen Trauerspiels_ (_[The Origin of German Tragic Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Origin_of_German_Tragic_Drama "The Origin of German Tragic Drama")_) as a habilitation thesis meant to qualify him as a tenured university professor in Germany.

At Bloch's suggestion, he read Lukács's _[History and Class Consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_and_Class_Consciousness "History and Class Consciousness")_ (1923). He also met the Latvian Bolshevik and actress [Asja Lācis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asja_L%C4%81cis "Asja Lācis"), then residing in Moscow; he became her lover and she was a lasting intellectual influence on him.<sup id="cite_ref-59"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-59"><span>[</span>59<span>]</span></a></sup>

A year later, in 1925, Benjamin withdrew _The Origin of German Tragic Drama_ as his possible qualification for the habilitation teaching credential at the [University of Frankfurt am Main](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Frankfurt_am_Main "University of Frankfurt am Main") at Frankfurt am Main, fearing its possible rejection.<sup id="cite_ref-BL_60-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-BL-60"><span>[</span>60<span>]</span></a></sup> The work was a study in which he sought to "save" the category of [allegory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory "Allegory"). It proved too unorthodox and abstruse for its examiners, who included prominent members of the humanities faculty, such as [Hans Cornelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Cornelius "Hans Cornelius").<sup id="cite_ref-Baroque_61-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Baroque-61"><span>[</span>61<span>]</span></a></sup> [Max Horkheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Horkheimer "Max Horkheimer") also sat on the panel of examiners who rejected Benjamin's thesis. Horkheimer later serves as both patron and promoter of Benjamin's work at the [Institute for Social Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Frankfurt_Institute_for_Social_Research "University of Frankfurt Institute for Social Research") and is best remembered as the co-author of Benjamin's closest disciple [Theodor Adorno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_W._Adorno "Theodor W. Adorno")'s magnum opus, the _[Dialectic of the Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectic_of_Enlightenment "Dialectic of Enlightenment")_ (a book which cribs heavily from Benjamin's unpublished, esoteric writings in many of its most important passages).<sup id="cite_ref-:12_62-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:12-62"><span>[</span>62<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:13_63-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:13-63"><span>[</span>63<span>]</span></a></sup> In the case of Benjamin's habilitation, however, Horkheimer presents a united front with Cornelius and Professor Schultz in asking Benjamin to withdraw his application for the habilitation to avoid disgrace on the occasion of the examination.<sup id="cite_ref-:12_62-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:12-62"><span>[</span>62<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:13_63-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:13-63"><span>[</span>63<span>]</span></a></sup> That is to say: His committee informed him that he will not be accepted as an academic instructor in the German university system.<sup id="cite_ref-:12_62-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:12-62"><span>[</span>62<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:13_63-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:13-63"><span>[</span>63<span>]</span></a></sup>

A diagram of the internecine dynamics of Benjamin's _habilitation_ committee's rejection of his work bear recollection here, as they determine something of the character of his later career and ultimate legacy.<sup id="cite_ref-:12_62-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:12-62"><span>[</span>62<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:13_63-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:13-63"><span>[</span>63<span>]</span></a></sup> [Hans Cornelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Cornelius "Hans Cornelius") had been Adorno's mentor in the institutional context of the university, whereas once Adorno started actually teaching as a professor at the University of Frankfurt, he devoted his seminars to Benjamin's rejected work.<sup id="cite_ref-:12_62-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:12-62"><span>[</span>62<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:13_63-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:13-63"><span>[</span>63<span>]</span></a></sup> Adorno's 1931 and 1932 seminars, delivered at Frankfurt University, devoted themselves to a close reading of the _[Origins of German Tragic Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Origin_of_German_Tragic_Drama "The Origin of German Tragic Drama")_. Adorno was still teaching this class on the _[Origins of German Tragic Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Origin_of_German_Tragic_Drama "The Origin of German Tragic Drama")_ during the winter semester that [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler") came to power, although at that time it was not listed in the course catalog--whereas Adorno's academic mentor Cornelius, who had rejected this thesis, is today remembered primarily because of his rejection of Benjamin's habilitation.<sup id="cite_ref-:12_62-5"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:12-62"><span>[</span>62<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:13_63-5"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:13-63"><span>[</span>63<span>]</span></a></sup> [Horkheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Horkheimer "Max Horkheimer") becomes a footnote to the career of Benjamin's apprentice. Schultz--the other member of Benjamin's committee who seems to have directed him to the subject of Baroque drama in the first place, only to reject the thesis that derived from this recommendation--is virtually altogether forgotten. The episode in the history of the German academy is immortalized in the _bon mot_, "One cannot habilitate intellect."<sup id="cite_ref-:12_62-6"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:12-62"><span>[</span>62<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:13_63-6"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:13-63"><span>[</span>63<span>]</span></a></sup>

This failure resulted in his father's refusal to continue to support him financially, so that Benjamin was forced to make ends meet as a professional critic and occasional translator.<sup id="cite_ref-Baroque_61-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Baroque-61"><span>[</span>61<span>]</span></a></sup> Working with [Franz Hessel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Hessel "Franz Hessel") he translated the first volumes of [Marcel Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Proust "Marcel Proust")'s _À la Recherche du Temps Perdu_ (_In Search of Lost Time_). The next year, 1926, he began writing for the German newspapers _[Frankfurter Zeitung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurter_Zeitung "Frankfurter Zeitung")_ and _Die Literarische Welt_ (The Literary World); that paid enough for him to reside in Paris for some months. In December 1926, the year his father died, Benjamin went to Moscow<sup id="cite_ref-64"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-64"><span>[</span>64<span>]</span></a></sup> to meet Lācis and found her ill in a sanatorium.<sup id="cite_ref-65"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-65"><span>[</span>65<span>]</span></a></sup>

During his stay in Moscow, he was asked by the editorial board of the [Great Soviet Encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Soviet_Encyclopedia "Great Soviet Encyclopedia") to write an article on Goethe for the first edition of the encyclopedia. Benjamin's article was ultimately rejected, with reviewer [Anatoly Lunacharsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Lunacharsky "Anatoly Lunacharsky") (then the [People's Commissar of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People%27s_Commissariat_for_Education "People's Commissariat for Education")) characterizing it as "non-encyclopedic",<sup id="cite_ref-66"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-66"><span>[</span>66<span>]</span></a></sup> and only a small part of the text prepared by Benjamin was included in the encyclopedia. During Benjamin's lifetime, the article was not published in its entirety. A Russian translation of the article was published in the Russian edition of "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" in 1996.<sup id="cite_ref-67"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-67"><span>[</span>67<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-68"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-68"><span>[</span>68<span>]</span></a></sup>

In 1927, he began _[Das Passagen-Werk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Arcades_Project "The Arcades Project")_ (_The Arcades Project_), his uncompleted _magnum opus_, a study of 19th-century Parisian life. The same year, he saw Scholem in Berlin, for the last time, and considered emigrating from Germany to Palestine. In 1928, he and Dora separated (they divorced two years later, in 1930); in the same year he published _Einbahnstraße_ (_One-Way Street_), and a revision of his habilitation thesis _Ursprung des Deutschen Trauerspiels_ (_The Origin of German Tragic Drama_). In 1929 Berlin, Lācis, then an assistant to [Bertolt Brecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht "Bertolt Brecht"), socially presented the intellectuals to each other. In that time, Benjamin also briefly embarked upon an academic career, as an instructor at the [University of Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Heidelberg "University of Heidelberg").

[![Walter Benjamin's membership card for the Bibliothèque nationale de France (1940).](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/ce/BenjaminBnF.jpg/330px-BenjaminBnF.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:BenjaminBnF.jpg)

Walter Benjamin's membership card for the [Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblioth%C3%A8que_nationale_de_France "Bibliothèque nationale de France") (1940)

In 1932, during the turmoil preceding [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler")'s assumption of the office of [Chancellor of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_Germany "Chancellor of Germany"), Benjamin left Germany temporarily for the Spanish island of [Ibiza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibiza "Ibiza") where he stayed for some months; he then moved to [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice "Nice"), where he considered killing himself. Perceiving the sociopolitical and cultural significance of the [Reichstag fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichstag_fire "Reichstag fire") (27 February 1933) as the _de facto_ Nazi assumption of full power in Germany, then manifest with the subsequent [persecution of the Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_policy_of_Nazi_Germany "Racial policy of Nazi Germany"), he permanently left Berlin and Germany in September. He moved to Paris, but before doing so he sought shelter in [Svendborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svendborg "Svendborg"), at Bertolt Brecht's house, and at [Sanremo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanremo "Sanremo"), where his ex-wife Dora lived.

As he ran out of money, Benjamin collaborated with [Max Horkheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Horkheimer "Max Horkheimer"), and received funds from the Institute for Social Research, later going permanently into exile. In Paris, he met other refugee German artists and intellectuals; he befriended [Hannah Arendt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_Arendt "Hannah Arendt"), novelist [Hermann Hesse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Hesse "Hermann Hesse"), and composer [Kurt Weill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Weill "Kurt Weill"). In 1936, a first version of "[The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Work_of_Art_in_the_Age_of_Mechanical_Reproduction "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction")" (originally written in German in 1935) was published in French ("L'œuvre d'art à l'époque de sa reproduction méchanisée") by Max Horkheimer in the _Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung_ journal of the Institute for Social Research.<sup id="cite_ref-69"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-69"><span>[</span>69<span>]</span></a></sup> It was a critique of the authenticity of mass-produced art; he wrote that a mechanically produced copy of an artwork can be taken somewhere the original could never have gone, arguing that the presence of the original is "prerequisite to the concept of authenticity".<sup id="cite_ref-70"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-70"><span>[</span>70<span>]</span></a></sup>

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b4/Benjamin%27s_apartment_in_Paris_%28fot._Mateusz_Palka%29.jpg/330px-Benjamin%27s_apartment_in_Paris_%28fot._Mateusz_Palka%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Benjamin%27s_apartment_in_Paris_(fot._Mateusz_Palka).jpg)

Walter Benjamin's Paris apartment at 10 [rue Dombasle](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Rue_Dombasle_(Paris)&action=edit&redlink=1 "Rue Dombasle (Paris) (page does not exist)") \[[fr](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rue_Dombasle_(Paris) "fr:Rue Dombasle (Paris)")\] (1938–1940)

In 1937 Benjamin worked on "Das Paris des Second Empire bei Baudelaire" ("The Paris of the Second Empire in Baudelaire"), met [Georges Bataille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Bataille "Georges Bataille") (to whom he later entrusted the _Arcades Project_ manuscript), and joined the [College of Sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_of_Sociology "College of Sociology") (which he would criticize for its "pre-fascist aestheticism.")<sup id="cite_ref-71"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-71"><span>[</span>71<span>]</span></a></sup> In 1938 he paid a last visit to Brecht, who was exiled to Denmark.<sup id="cite_ref-72"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-72"><span>[</span>72<span>]</span></a></sup> Meanwhile, the Nazi régime stripped German Jews of their German citizenship; now a stateless man, Benjamin was arrested by the French government and incarcerated for three months in a prison camp near [Nevers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevers "Nevers"), in central [Burgundy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burgundy_(French_region) "Burgundy (French region)").<sup id="cite_ref-73"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-73"><span>[</span>73<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-74"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-74"><span>[</span>74<span>]</span></a></sup>

Returning to Paris in January 1940, he drafted "Über den Begriff der Geschichte" ("On the Concept of History", later published as "[Theses on the Philosophy of History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theses_on_the_Philosophy_of_History "Theses on the Philosophy of History")"). While the [Wehrmacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wehrmacht "Wehrmacht") was pushing back the [French Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Army "French Army"), on 13 June Benjamin and his sister fled Paris to the town of [Lourdes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lourdes "Lourdes"), just a day before the Germans entered the capital with orders to arrest him at his flat. In August, he obtained a travel visa to the U.S. that Horkheimer had negotiated for him. In eluding the [Gestapo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestapo "Gestapo"), Benjamin planned to travel to the U.S. from neutral Portugal, which he expected to reach via [Francoist Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francoist_Spain "Francoist Spain"), then ostensibly a neutral country.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/af/Grab_Walter_Benjamin.jpg/250px-Grab_Walter_Benjamin.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Grab_Walter_Benjamin.jpg)

Walter Benjamin's grave in Portbou. The epitaph in German, repeated in Catalan, quotes from Section 7 of "Theses on the Philosophy of History": "There is no document of culture which is not at the same time a document of barbarism"

The historical record indicates that he safely crossed the French–Spanish border and arrived at the coastal town of [Portbou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portbou "Portbou"), in [Catalonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalonia "Catalonia") on 25 September 1940. The Franco government had cancelled all transit visas and ordered the Spanish police to return such persons to France, including the Jewish refugee group Benjamin had joined. They were told by the Spanish police that they would be deported back to France the next day, which would have thwarted Benjamin's plans to travel to the United States. Expecting repatriation to Nazi hands, Benjamin killed himself with an overdose of [morphine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphine "Morphine") tablets that night, while staying at the _Hotel de Francia_; the official Portbou register records 26 September 1940 as the date of death.<sup id="cite_ref-Witte_7-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Witte-7"><span>[</span>7<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-75"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-75"><span>[</span>75<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-76"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-76"><span>[</span>76<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-77"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-77"><span>[</span>77<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-78"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-78"><span>[</span>78<span>]</span></a></sup> Benjamin's colleague [Arthur Koestler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Koestler "Arthur Koestler"), also fleeing Europe, attempted suicide by taking some of the morphine tablets, but survived.<sup id="cite_ref-79"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-79"><span>[</span>79<span>]</span></a></sup> Benjamin's brother Georg was killed at the [Mauthausen-Gusen concentration camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauthausen-Gusen_concentration_camp "Mauthausen-Gusen concentration camp") in 1942.

The others in his party were allowed passage the next day (maybe because Benjamin's suicide shocked Spanish officials), and safely reached [Lisbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisbon "Lisbon") on 30 September. [Arendt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_Arendt "Hannah Arendt"), who crossed the French-Spanish border at Portbou a few months later, passed the manuscript of _Theses_ to Adorno. Another completed manuscript, which Benjamin had carried in his suitcase, disappeared after his death and has not been recovered.<sup id="cite_ref-80"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-80"><span>[</span>80<span>]</span></a></sup>

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f3/Klee%2C_Angelus_novus.png/250px-Klee%2C_Angelus_novus.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Klee,_Angelus_novus.png)

[Paul Klee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Klee "Paul Klee")'s 1920 painting _[Angelus Novus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelus_Novus "Angelus Novus")_, which Benjamin bought in 1921 and compared to "the angel of history"

In addition to his lifelong dialogue in letters with [Gershom Scholem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gershom_Scholem "Gershom Scholem"), Walter Benjamin maintained an intense correspondence with [Theodor Adorno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Adorno "Theodor Adorno") and [Bertolt Brecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht "Bertolt Brecht"), and was occasionally funded by the [Frankfurt School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_School "Frankfurt School") under the direction of Adorno and [Horkheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horkheimer "Horkheimer"), even from their New York City residence. At other times he received funding from Hebrew University or from funds made available by [Martin Buber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Buber "Martin Buber") and his publishing associates including [Salman Schocken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_Schocken "Salman Schocken").

The dynamism or conflict between these competing influences—Brecht's Marxism, Adorno's [critical theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_theory "Critical theory"), Scholem's Jewish mysticism—were central to his work, although their philosophic differences remained unresolved. Moreover, the critic [Paul de Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_de_Man "Paul de Man") argued that the intellectual range of Benjamin's writings flows dynamically among those three intellectual traditions, deriving a critique via juxtaposition; the exemplary synthesis is "Theses on the Philosophy of History". At least one scholar, [historian of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_studies "Religious studies") [Jason Josephson-Storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_Josephson-Storm "Jason Josephson-Storm"), has argued that Benjamin's diverse interests may be understood in part by understanding the influence of [Western Esotericism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Esotericism "Western Esotericism") on Benjamin.<sup id="cite_ref-81"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-81"><span>[</span>81<span>]</span></a></sup> Some of Benjamin's key ideas were adapted from occultists and [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age_movement "New Age movement") figures including [Eric Gutkind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Gutkind "Eric Gutkind") and [Ludwig Klages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Klages "Ludwig Klages"), and his interest in esotericism is known to have extended far beyond the Jewish [Kabbalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabbalah "Kabbalah").<sup id="cite_ref-82"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-82"><span>[</span>82<span>]</span></a></sup> In addition to Brecht's Marxism, Adorno's critical theory, and Scholem's Jewish mysticism, Howard Eiland and Michael W. Jennings have underscored the importance of [Karl Korsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Korsch "Karl Korsch")'s interpretation of _[Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_(Marx) "Capital (Marx)")_ to understanding Benjamin's engagement with [Marxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism "Marxism") in later works like the _[Arcades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcades_Project "Arcades Project")_. Karl Korsch's _Karl Marx_, which was "one of Benjamin's main sources \[on\]... Marxism," introduced him "to an advanced understanding of Marxism."<sup id="cite_ref-83"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-83"><span>[</span>83<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-84"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-84"><span>[</span>84<span>]</span></a></sup>

### "Theses on the Philosophy of History"

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Walter_Benjamin&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: "Theses on the Philosophy of History"")\]

"Theses on the Philosophy of History" is often cited as Benjamin's last complete work, having been completed, according to Adorno, in the spring of 1940. The Institute for Social Research, which had relocated to New York, published _Theses_ in Benjamin's memory in 1942. Margaret Cohen writes in the _Cambridge Companion to Walter Benjamin_:

> In the "Concept of History" Benjamin also turned to Jewish mysticism for a model of praxis in dark times, inspired by the kabbalistic precept that the work of the holy man is an activity known as [tikkun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikkun_olam "Tikkun olam"). According to the kabbalah, God's attributes were once held in vessels whose glass was contaminated by the presence of evil and these vessels had consequently shattered, disseminating their contents to the four corners of the earth. Tikkun was the process of collecting the scattered fragments in the hopes of once more piecing them together. Benjamin fused tikkun with the Surrealist notion that liberation would come through releasing repressed collective material, to produce his celebrated account of the revolutionary historiographer, who sought to grab hold of elided memories as they sparked to view at moments of present danger.

In the essay, Benjamin's famed ninth thesis struggles to reconcile the [Idea of Progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idea_of_Progress "Idea of Progress") in the present with the apparent chaos of the past:

> A [Klee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Klee "Paul Klee") painting named _[Angelus Novus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelus_Novus "Angelus Novus")_ shows an angel looking as though he is about to move away from something he is fixedly contemplating. His eyes are staring, his mouth is open, his wings are spread. This is how one pictures the angel of history. His face is turned toward the past. Where we perceive a chain of events, he sees one single catastrophe which keeps piling wreckage upon wreckage and hurls it in front of his feet. The angel would like to stay, awaken the dead, and make whole what has been smashed. But a storm is blowing from Paradise; it has got caught in his wings with such violence that the angel can no longer close them. The storm irresistibly propels him into the future to which his back is turned, while the pile of debris before him grows skyward. This storm is what we call progress.

The final paragraph about the Jewish quest for the [Messiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah "Messiah") provides a final point to Benjamin's work, with its themes of culture, destruction, Jewish heritage and the fight between humanity and nihilism. He brings up the interdiction, in some varieties of Judaism, of attempts to determine the year when the Messiah would come into the world, and points out that this did not make Jews indifferent to the future "for every second of time was the strait gate through which the Messiah might enter".

### "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction"

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Walter_Benjamin&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction"")\]

Perhaps Walter Benjamin's best-known essay, "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction," identifies the perceptual shift that takes place when technological advancements emphasize speed and reproducibility.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_85-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:0-85"><span>[</span>85<span>]</span></a></sup> Benjamin argues that the aura is found in a work of art that contains presence. The aura is precisely what cannot be reproduced in a work of art: its original presence in time and space.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_85-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:0-85"><span>[</span>85<span>]</span></a></sup> He suggests a work of art's aura is in a state of decay because it is becoming more and more difficult to apprehend the time and space in which a piece of art is created.

This essay also introduces the concept of the optical unconscious, a concept that identifies the subject's ability to identify desire in visual objects. This also leads to the ability to perceive information by habit instead of rapt attention.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_85-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:0-85"><span>[</span>85<span>]</span></a></sup>

### _The Origin of German Tragic Drama_

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Walter_Benjamin&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: The Origin of German Tragic Drama")\]

_Ursprung des deutschen Trauerspiels_ (_[The Origin of German Tragic Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Origin_of_German_Tragic_Drama "The Origin of German Tragic Drama")_, 1928), is a critical study of German baroque drama, as well as the political and cultural climate of Germany during the [Counter-Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-Reformation "Counter-Reformation") (1545–1648). Benjamin presented the work to the University of Frankfurt in 1925 as the [postdoctoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral "Postdoctoral") dissertation meant to earn him the habilitation (qualification) to become a university instructor in Germany.

Professor Schultz of the University of Frankfurt found _The Origin of German Tragic Drama_ inappropriate for his _Germanistik_ department (Department of German Language and Literature), and passed it to the Department of [Aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics "Aesthetics"), the readers of which likewise dismissed Benjamin's work.<sup id="cite_ref-Baroque_61-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-Baroque-61"><span>[</span>61<span>]</span></a></sup> The university officials recommended that Benjamin withdraw _Ursprung des deutschen Trauerspiels_ as a habilitation thesis to avoid formal rejection and public embarrassment.<sup id="cite_ref-BL_60-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-BL-60"><span>[</span>60<span>]</span></a></sup> He heeded the advice, and three years later, in 1928, he published _The Origin of German Tragic Drama_ as a book.<sup id="cite_ref-86"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-86"><span>[</span>86<span>]</span></a></sup>

_[Einbahnstraße](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Way_Street_(1928_book) "One Way Street (1928 book)")_ (_One Way Street_, 1928) is a series of meditations written primarily during the same phase as _[The Origin of German Tragic Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Origin_of_German_Tragic_Drama "The Origin of German Tragic Drama")_, after Benjamin had met [Asja Lācis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asja_L%C4%81cis "Asja Lācis") on the beach at Capri in 1924. He finished the cycle in 1926, and put it out the same year that his failed thesis was published.

_One Way Street_ is a collage work. [Greil Marcus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greil_Marcus "Greil Marcus") compares certain formal qualities of the book to the graphic novel _Hundred Headless Women_ by [Max Ernst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Ernst "Max Ernst"),<sup id="cite_ref-:2_87-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:2-87"><span>[</span>87<span>]</span></a></sup> or to [Walter Ruttman's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Ruttmann "Walter Ruttmann") _The Weekend_ (an early sound collage film)_.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_87-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:2-87"><span>[</span>87<span>]</span></a></sup>_ The book avoids "all semblance of linear-narrative...\[offering\] a jumble of sixty apparently autonomous short prose pieces: aphorisms, jokes, dream protocols, cityscapes, landscapes, and mindscapes; portions of writing manuals, trenchant contemporary political analysis; prescient appreciations of the child's psychology, behavior, and moods; decodings of bourgeois fashion, living arrangements and courtship patterns; and time and again, remarkable penetrations into the heart of every day things, what Benjamin would later call a mode of empathy with 'the soul of the commodity'" according to Michael Jennings in his introduction to the work. He continues: "Many of the pieces...first appeared in the [feuilleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feuilleton "Feuilleton") section," of newspapers and magazines which was "not a separate section but rather an area at the bottom of every page...and the spatial restrictions of the feuilleton played a decisive role in shaping the prose form on which the book is based."<sup id="cite_ref-:2_87-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:2-87"><span>[</span>87<span>]</span></a></sup>

Written contemporaneously with Martin Heidegger's _[Being & Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Being_and_Time "Being and Time")_, Benjamin's work from this period explores much of the same territory: formally in his "Epistemo-Critical Prologue" to _The Origin of German Tragic Drama_, and as sketches, allusions and asides in _One Way Street._<sup id="cite_ref-88"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-88"><span>[</span>88<span>]</span></a></sup>

### _The Arcades Project_

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Walter_Benjamin&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: The Arcades Project")\]

The _[Passagenwerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcades_Project "Arcades Project")_ (_Arcades Project_, 1927–40) was Benjamin's final, incomplete book about Parisian city life in the 19th century, especially about the _[Passages couverts de Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passages_couverts_de_Paris "Passages couverts de Paris")_—the covered passages that extended the culture of [_flânerie_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fl%C3%A2neur "Flâneur") (idling and people-watching) when inclement weather made _flânerie_ infeasible in the boulevards and streets proper. In this work Benjamin uses his fragmentary style to write about the rise of modern [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_culture "European culture") [urban culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_culture "Urban culture").<sup id="cite_ref-89"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-89"><span>[</span>89<span>]</span></a></sup> Several of the major published works that appeared in his lifetime—"[The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Work_of_Art_in_the_Age_of_Mechanical_Reproduction "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction")", "[Paris, The Capital of the 19th Century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris,_Capital_of_the_19th_Century "Paris, Capital of the 19th Century")", and his [late essays and monograph on Baudelaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Paris_of_the_Second_Empire_in_Baudelaire "The Paris of the Second Empire in Baudelaire")—are fragments of the book that he developed as standalone pieces for publication.

The _Arcades Project_, in its current form, brings together a massive collection of notes Benjamin filed together from 1927 to 1940.<sup id="cite_ref-90"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-90"><span>[</span>90<span>]</span></a></sup>

The _Arcades Project_ was published for the first time in 1982, and is over a thousand pages long.

Scholem said of Benjamin's prose: "Among the peculiarities of Benjamin's philosophical prose—the critical and metaphysical prose, in which the Marxist element constitutes something like an inversion of the metaphysical-theological—is its enormous suitability for canonization; I might almost say for quotation as a kind of Holy Writ."<sup id="cite_ref-:4_91-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:4-91"><span>[</span>91<span>]</span></a></sup> Scholem's commentary on this phenomenon continues at length. Briefly: Benjamin's texts have an occult quality in the sense that passages appearing quite lucid today may seem impenetrable later, and elements that read as indecipherable or incoherent now may read as transparently obvious upon later revisitation.<sup id="cite_ref-:4_91-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-:4-91"><span>[</span>91<span>]</span></a></sup>

[Susan Sontag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Sontag "Susan Sontag") said that in Benjamin's writing, sentences did not originate ordinarily, do not progress into one another, and delineate no obvious line of reasoning, as if each sentence "had to say everything, before the inward gaze of total concentration dissolved the subject before his eyes", a "freeze-frame baroque" style of writing and cogitation. "His major essays seem to end just in time, before they self-destruct".<sup id="cite_ref-92"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-92"><span>[</span>92<span>]</span></a></sup> The occasional difficulties of Benjamin's style are essential to his philosophical project. Fascinated by notions of reference and constellation, his goal in later works was to use [intertexts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertextuality "Intertextuality") to reveal aspects of the past that cannot, and should not, be understood within greater, monolithic constructs of historical understanding.

Benjamin's writings identify him as a [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism "Modernism") for whom the philosophic merges with the literary: logical philosophic reasoning cannot account for all experience, especially not for self-representation via art. He presented his stylistic concerns in "The Task of the Translator", wherein he posits that a literary translation, by definition, produces deformations and misunderstandings of the original text. Moreover, in the deformed text, otherwise hidden aspects of the original, source-language text are elucidated, while previously obvious aspects become unreadable. Such translational modification of the source text is productive; when placed in a specific constellation of works and ideas, newly revealed affinities, between historical objects, appear and are productive of philosophical truth.

His work "The Task of the Translator" was the subject of a commentary by the French translation scholar [Antoine Berman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Berman "Antoine Berman") (_[L'âge de la traduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%27%C3%A2ge_de_la_traduction "L'âge de la traduction")_).

## Legacy and reception

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Walter_Benjamin&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Legacy and reception")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9f/Walter_Benjamin._Passagen_%28Port-Bou%29.jpg/330px-Walter_Benjamin._Passagen_%28Port-Bou%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Walter_Benjamin._Passagen_(Port-Bou).jpg)

Memorial in Portbou  

Since the publication of _Schriften_ (_Writings_, 1955), 15 years after his death, Benjamin's work—especially the essay "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (French edition, 1936)—has become of seminal importance to academics in the humanities disciplines.<sup id="cite_ref-93"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-93"><span>[</span>93<span>]</span></a></sup> In 1968, the first [Internationale Walter Benjamin Gesellschaft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internationale_Walter_Benjamin_Gesellschaft "Internationale Walter Benjamin Gesellschaft") was established by the [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany") thinker, poet and artist [Natias Neutert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natias_Neutert "Natias Neutert"), as a free association of philosophers, writers, artists, media theoreticians and editors. They did not take Benjamin's body of thought as a scholastic "closed architecture \[...\], but as one in which all doors, windows and roof hatches are widely open", as the founder Neutert put it—more poetically than politically—in his manifesto.<sup id="cite_ref-94"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-94"><span>[</span>94<span>]</span></a></sup> The members felt liberated to take Benjamin's ideas as a welcome touchstone for social change.<sup id="cite_ref-95"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-95"><span>[</span>95<span>]</span></a></sup>

Like the first Internationale Walter Benjamin Gesellschaft, a new one, established in 2000, researches and discusses the imperative that Benjamin formulated in his "Theses on the Philosophy of History": "In every era the attempt must be made anew to wrest the tradition away from a conformism that is about to overpower it." The successor society was registered in Karlsruhe (Germany); Chairman of the Board of Directors was Bernd Witte, an internationally recognized Benjamin scholar and Professor of Modern German Literature in Düsseldorf (Germany). Its members come from 19 countries, both within and beyond Europe and it provides an international forum for discourse. The Society supported research endeavors devoted to the creative and visionary potential of Benjamin's works and their view of 20th century modernism. Special emphasis had been placed upon strengthening academic ties to Latin America and Eastern and Central Europe.<sup id="cite_ref-96"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-96"><span>[</span>96<span>]</span></a></sup> The society conducts conferences and exhibitions, as well as interdisciplinary and intermedial events, at regular intervals and different European venues:

-   Rome Conference – November 2003
-   Zurich Conference – October 2004
-   Paris Conference – June 2005
-   Düsseldorf Conference – June 2005
-   Düsseldorf Conference – November 2005
-   Antwerpen Conference – May 2006
-   Vienna Conference – March 2007<sup id="cite_ref-97"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-97"><span>[</span>97<span>]</span></a></sup>

In 2017 Walter Benjamin's _Arcades Project_ was reinterpreted in an exhibition curated by [Jens Hoffman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jens_Hoffmann "Jens Hoffmann"), held at the [Jewish Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Museum_(Manhattan) "Jewish Museum (Manhattan)") in New York City. The exhibition, entitled "The Arcades: Contemporary Art and Walter Benjamin", featured 36 contemporary artworks representing the 36 convolutes of Benjamin's Project.<sup id="cite_ref-98"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-98"><span>[</span>98<span>]</span></a></sup>

Commemorative plaque for Walter Benjamin, Berlin-Wilmersdorf

A commemorative plaque is located by the residence where Benjamin lived in Berlin during the years 1930–1933: (Prinzregentenstraße 66, [Berlin-Wilmersdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin-Wilmersdorf "Berlin-Wilmersdorf")). Close by [Kurfürstendamm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurf%C3%BCrstendamm "Kurfürstendamm"), in the district of [Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf "Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf"), a town square created by [Hans Kollhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Kollhoff "Hans Kollhoff") in 2001 was named "Walter-Benjamin-Platz".<sup id="cite_ref-101"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin#cite_note-101"><span>[</span>101<span>]</span></a></sup> There is a memorial sculpture by the artist [Dani Karavan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dani_Karavan "Dani Karavan") at Portbou, where Walter Benjamin ended his life.
