# Akrasia - Wikipedia

> From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Akrasia refers to the phenomenon of acting against one's better judgment—the state in which one intentionally performs an action while simultaneously believing that a different course of action would be better. Sometimes translated as "weakness of will" or "incontinence," akrasia describes the paradoxical human experience of knowingly choosing what one judges to be the inferior option.

In Plato's Protagoras dialogue, Socrates asks precisely how it is possible that, if one judges action A to be the best course of action, why one would do anything other than A. Socrates presents a radical thesis that fundamentally denies the existence of akrasia. His famous declaration, "No one goes willingly toward the bad" (οὐδεὶς ἑκὼν κακός), encapsulates a view known as Socratic intellectualism.

According to Socrates, genuine akrasia is impossible because human action necessarily follows knowledge. His argument proceeds through several interconnected premises: (1) The Unity of Knowledge and Virtue — virtue is knowledge of what is truly good and beneficial, and to know the good is necessarily to pursue it; (2) The Natural Orientation Toward the Good — human beings by nature seek what they perceive to be good for themselves; (3) The Power of Complete Knowledge — a thorough, all-things-considered evaluation yields complete knowledge of each potential action's outcomes and relative worth.

Given these premises, Socrates concludes that it is psychologically impossible for someone who truly knows what is best to act otherwise. What appears to be akrasia is actually a form of ignorance — incomplete knowledge of the facts, inadequate understanding of the good, or failure to properly calculate consequences.

Aristotle recognizes that acting contrary to one's best judgment is a staple of commonsense and a common human experience. He dedicates Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics to an examination of akrasia, adopting an empirical approach that contrasts with Socratic intellectualism. He distinguishes different mental faculties: akrasia results from one's opinion (δόξα, doxa), not one's desire (epithumia) per se. Desire is a natural condition of the body incapable of truth or falsity, while opinion is a belief that may or may not be true, a cognitive state. Akratic failures can therefore be explained by the incorrectness of one's best judgment rather than a failure to attempt to act according to it.

For Aristotle, the opposite of akrasia is enkrateia, a state where an agent has power over their desires. Aristotle considered one could be in a state of akrasia with respect to money or temper or glory, but its core relation was to bodily enjoyment. Its causes could be weakness of will, or an impetuous refusal to think. He did not consider it a vice, because it is not so much a product of moral choice as a failure to act on one's better knowledge.

For Augustine of Hippo, incontinence was not so much a problem of knowledge but of the will; he considered it a matter of everyday experience that men incontinently choose lesser over greater goods.

### Contemporary approaches

Donald Davidson criticized earlier thinkers who wanted to limit the scope of akrasia to agents who, despite having reached a rational decision, were somehow swerved off their "desired" tracks. Davidson expands akrasia to include any judgment that is reached but not fulfilled, whether as a result of an opinion, a real or imagined good, or a moral belief.

Davidson sees the problem as reconciling an inconsistent triad: if an agent believes A to be better than B, they want to do A more than B; if an agent wants to do A more than B, they will do A rather than B if they only do one; yet sometimes an agent acts against their better judgment. Davidson solves this by saying that when people act this way, they temporarily believe the worse course of action is better because they have not made an all-things-considered judgment but only a judgment based on a subset of possible considerations.

Amélie Rorty tackled the problem by distilling out akrasia's many forms, enumerating four types: akrasia of direction or aim, of interpretation, of irrationality, and of character, corresponding to breakdowns at different stages of the practical reasoning process.

Another explanation points to different forms of motivation that can conflict — reason versus emotion — which might make it possible to believe one should do A rather than B, but still end up wanting to do B more than A.

Psychologist George Ainslie argues that akrasia results from the empirically verified phenomenon of hyperbolic discounting, which causes people to make different judgments close to a reward than they will when further from it.

Richard Holton argues that weakness of the will involves revising one's resolutions too easily — it is possible to act against one's better judgment (be akratic) without being weak-willed. Example: Sarah judges that taking revenge upon a murderer is not the best course of action but makes the resolution to take revenge anyway and sticks to it — she behaves akratically but does not show weakness of will.

In the structural division of Dante's Inferno, incontinence is the sin punished in the second through fifth circles; the mutual incontinence of lust was for Dante the lightest of the deadly sins, even if its lack of self-control would open the road to deeper layers of Hell.

Akrasia appeared later as a character in Spenser's The Faerie Queene, representing the incontinence of lust; and as late as Jane Austen the sensibility of figures like Marianne Dashwood would be treated as a form of (spiritual) incontinence.

With the triumph of Romanticism, the incontinent choice of feeling over reason became increasingly valorised in Western culture. Blake wrote, "those who restrain desire, do so because theirs is weak enough to be restrained." Encouraged by Rousseau, there was a rise of what Arnold J. Toynbee called "an abandon (ακρατεια)...a state of mind in which antinomianism is accepted—consciously or unconsciously, in theory or in practice—as a substitute for creativeness."

A peak of such akrasia was perhaps reached in the 1960s cult of letting it all hang out—of breakdown, acting out, and emotional self-indulgence and drama. Partly in reaction, proponents of emotional intelligence looked back to Aristotle in the search for impulse control and delayed gratification — his dictum that "a person is called continent or incontinent according as his reason is or is not in control."
