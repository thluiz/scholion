Psychogeography involves self published reports and maps, using the [Situationist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situationist "Situationist") technique of detournement - using pre-found elements and subverting them. evoL PsychogeogrAphix 2003

**Psychogeography** is the exploration of urban environments that emphasizes interpersonal connections to places and arbitrary routes. It was developed by members of the [Letterist International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letterist_International "Letterist International") and [Situationist International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situationist_International "Situationist International"), which were revolutionary groups influenced by [Marxist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism "Marxism") and [anarchist theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchist_theory "Anarchist theory") as well as the attitudes and methods of [Dadaists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dadaists "Dadaists") and [Surrealists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrealism "Surrealism").<sup id="cite_ref-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-1"><span>[</span>1<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:1_2-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:1-2"><span>[</span>2<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:2_3-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:2-3"><span>[</span>3<span>]</span></a></sup>

In 1955, [Guy Debord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_Debord "Guy Debord") defined psychogeography as "the study of the precise laws and specific effects of the geographical environment, consciously organized or not, on the emotions and behavior of individuals."<sup id="cite_ref-:0_4-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:0-4"><span>[</span>4<span>]</span></a></sup> The key tactic for exploring psychogeography is the loosely defined urban walking practice known as the _[dérive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A9rive "Dérive")_. As a practice and theory, psychogeography has influenced a broad set of cultural actors, including artists, activists and academics.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_2-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:1-2"><span>[</span>2<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:2_3-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:2-3"><span>[</span>3<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:3-5"><span>[</span>5<span>]</span></a></sup>

Psychogeography was originally developed by the Lettrist International 'around the summer of 1953'.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_4-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:0-4"><span>[</span>4<span>]</span></a></sup> Debord describes psychogeography as 'charmingly vague' and emphasises the importance of practice in psychogeographical explorations.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_4-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:0-4"><span>[</span>4<span>]</span></a></sup> The first published discussion of psychogeography was in the Lettrist journal _Potlatch_ (1954), which included a 'Psychogeographical Game of the Week':

> Depending on what you are after, choose an area, a more or less populous city, a more or less lively street. Build a house. Furnish it. Make the most of its decoration and surroundings. Choose the season and the time. Gather together the right people, the best records and drinks. Lighting and conversation must, of course, be appropriate, along with the weather and your memories. If your calculations are correct, you should find the outcome satisfying. (Please inform the editors of the results.)<sup id="cite_ref-6"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-6"><span>[</span>6<span>]</span></a></sup>

The Lettrists' reimagining of the city has its precursors in aspects of Dadaism and Surrealism.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_2-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:1-2"><span>[</span>2<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:2_3-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:2-3"><span>[</span>3<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:6_7-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:6-7"><span>[</span>7<span>]</span></a></sup> The concept of the [flâneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fl%C3%A2neur "Flâneur") is also cited as an influence on the development of psychogeography.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:3-5"><span>[</span>5<span>]</span></a></sup><sup>:3;</sup><sup id="cite_ref-:5_8-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:5-8"><span>[</span>8<span>]</span></a></sup><sup>18</sup> Widely credited to [Charles Baudelaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Baudelaire "Charles Baudelaire"), who was influenced by Edgar Allan Poe's "The Man of the Crowd", it was further developed theoretically by [Walter Benjamin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin "Walter Benjamin").<sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:3-5"><span>[</span>5<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:5_8-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:5-8"><span>[</span>8<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-9"><span>[</span>9<span>]</span></a></sup>

[Ivan Chtcheglov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Chtcheglov "Ivan Chtcheglov"), in his highly influential<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2008)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup> 1953 essay "Formulaire pour un urbanisme nouveau" ("Formulary for a New Urbanism"), established many of the concepts that would inform the development of psychogeography.<sup id="cite_ref-Chtcheglov_10-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-Chtcheglov-10"><span>[</span>10<span>]</span></a></sup> Forwarding a theory of [unitary urbanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_urbanism "Unitary urbanism"), Chtcheglov wrote "Architecture is the simplest means of articulating time and space, of modulating reality, of engendering dreams".<sup id="cite_ref-Chtcheglov_10-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-Chtcheglov-10"><span>[</span>10<span>]</span></a></sup> Similarly, the Situationists found contemporary architecture both physically and ideologically restrictive, combining with outside cultural influence, effectively creating an undertow, and forcing oneself into a certain system of interaction with their environment: "\[C\]ities have a psychogeographical relief, with constant currents, fixed points and vortexes which strongly discourage entry into or exit from certain zones".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKnabb199550_11-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnabb199550-11"><span>[</span>11<span>]</span></a></sup> Following Chtcheglov's exclusion from the Lettrists in 1954,<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup> [Guy Debord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_Debord "Guy Debord") and others worked to clarify the concept of unitary urbanism, in a bid to demand a revolutionary approach to architecture.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup>

The Situationists' response was to create designs of new urbanized space, promising better opportunities for experimenting through mundane expression. Their intentions remained completely as abstractions. Guy Debord's truest intention was to unify two different factors of "ambiance" that, he felt, determined the values of the urban landscape: the soft ambiance — light, sound, time, the association of ideas — with the hard, the actual physical constructions. Debord's vision was a combination of the two realms of opposing ambiance, where the play of the soft ambiance was actively considered in the rendering of the hard. The new space creates a possibility for activity not formerly determined by one besides the individual.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2009)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup>

At a conference in Cosio di Arroscia, [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy "Italy") in 1956, the Lettrists joined the [International Movement for an Imaginist Bauhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Movement_for_an_Imaginist_Bauhaus "International Movement for an Imaginist Bauhaus") to set a proper definition for the idea announced by [Gil J. Wolman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gil_J._Wolman "Gil J. Wolman"): "Unitary Urbanism - the synthesis of art and technology that we call for — must be constructed according to certain new values of life, values which now need to be distinguished and disseminated."<sup id="cite_ref-12"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-12"><span>[</span>12<span>]</span></a></sup> It demanded the rejection of functional, [Euclidean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid "Euclid") values in [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture "Architecture"), as well as the separation between art and its surroundings. The implication of combining these two negations is that by creating abstraction, one creates art, which, in turn, creates a point of distinction that unitary urbanism insists must be nullified. This confusion is also fundamental to the execution of unitary urbanism as it corrupts one's ability to identify where "function" ends and "play" (the "ludic") begins, resulting in what the Lettrist International and [Situationist International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situationist_International "Situationist International") believed to be a utopia where one was constantly exploring, free of determining factors.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2008)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup>

One of the first collaborations between Debord and Danish [Asger Jorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asger_Jorn "Asger Jorn") is their screen printed [Guide psychogéographique de Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Guide_psychog%C3%A9ographique_de_Paris&action=edit&redlink=1 "Guide psychogéographique de Paris (page does not exist)"): discours sur les passions de l’armour (Psychogeographic Guide of Paris: 1957). Later they created _The Naked City (psychogeographic map of Paris:1958),_ for which they cut apart a typical map of Paris and repositioned the pieces. The resulting map corresponded with parts of Paris that were ‘stimulating’ and “worthy of study and preservation”;<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup> they then drew red arrows between these parts of the city to represent the fastest and most direct connections from one place to another, preferably made by taxi,<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup> as it was seen as the most independent and free way to travel through the city as opposed to buses.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup>

Eventually, Debord and [Asger Jorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asger_Jorn "Asger Jorn") resigned themselves to the fate of "urban relativity". Debord readily admits in his 1961 film _A Critique of Separation_, "The sectors of a city…are decipherable, but the personal meaning they have for us is incommunicable, as is the secrecy of private life in general, regarding which we possess nothing but pitiful documents". Despite the ambiguity of the theory, Debord committed himself firmly to its practical basis in reality, even as he later confesses, "none of this is very clear. It is a completely typical drunken monologue…with its vain phrases that do not await response and its overbearing explanations. And its silences."<sup id="cite_ref-13"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-13"><span>[</span>13<span>]</span></a></sup> "This apparently serious term 'psychogeography'", writes Debord biographer Vincent Kaufman, "comprises an art of conversation and drunkenness, and everything leads us to believe that Debord excelled at both."<sup id="cite_ref-14"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-14"><span>[</span>14<span>]</span></a></sup><sup>:114</sup>

Before settling on the impossibility of true psychogeography, Debord made another film, _On the Passage of a Few Persons Through a Rather Brief Unity of Time_ (1959). The film's narrated content concerns itself with the evolution of a generally passive group of unnamed people into a fully aware, anarchistic assemblage, and might be perceived as a biography of the situationists themselves.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research" title="Wikipedia:No original research"><span title="The material near this tag possibly contains original research. (October 2020)">original research?</span></a></i>]</sup> Among the rants which construct the film (regarding art, ignorance, consumerism, militarism) is a desperate call for psychogeographic action:

> When freedom is practised in a closed circle, it fades into a dream, becomes a mere image of itself. The ambiance of play is by nature unstable. At any moment, "ordinary life" may prevail once again. The geographical limitation of play is even more striking than its temporal limitation. Every game takes place within the boundaries of its own spatial domain.<sup id="cite_ref-auto_15-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-auto-15"><span>[</span>15<span>]</span></a></sup>

Moments later, Debord elaborates on the important goals of unitary urbanism in contemporary society:

> The atmosphere of a few places gave us a few intimations of the future powers of an architecture that it would be necessary to create in order to provide the setting for less mediocre games.<sup id="cite_ref-auto_15-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-auto-15"><span>[</span>15<span>]</span></a></sup>

Giving a quote that he attributed to [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx "Karl Marx"), Debord says:

> People can see nothing around them that is not their own image; everything speaks to them of themselves. Their very landscape is animated. Obstacles were everywhere. And they were all interrelated, maintaining a unified reign of poverty.<sup id="cite_ref-auto_15-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-auto-15"><span>[</span>15<span>]</span></a></sup>

While a reading of the texts included in the journal _Internationale Situationniste_ may lead to an understanding of psychogeography as dictated by Guy Debord, a more comprehensive elucidation of the term would come from research into those who have put its techniques into a more developed practise.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research" title="Wikipedia:No original research"><span title="The material near this tag possibly contains original research. (October 2020)">original research?</span></a></i>]</sup> While Debord's influence in bringing Chtchglov's text to an international audience is undoubted, his skill with the 'praxis' of unitary urbanism has been placed into question by almost all the subsequent protagonists of the Formulary's directives.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research" title="Wikipedia:No original research"><span title="The material near this tag possibly contains original research. (October 2020)">original research?</span></a></i>]</sup> Debord was indeed a notorious drunk (see his Panegyrique, Gallimard 1995) and this altered state of consciousness must be considered along with assertions he made regarding his attempts at psychogeographical activities such as dérive and constructed situation.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research" title="Wikipedia:No original research"><span title="The material near this tag possibly contains original research. (October 2020)">original research?</span></a></i>]</sup> The researches undertaken by WNLA, AAA and the London Psychogeographical Association during the 1990s support the contention of Asger Jorn and the Scandinavian Situationniste (Drakagygett 1962 - 1998) that the psychogeographical is a concept only known through practise of its techniques.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup> Without undertaking the programme expounded by Chtchglov, and the resultant submission to the urban unknown, comprehension of the Formulary is not possible.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research" title="Wikipedia:No original research"><span title="The material near this tag possibly contains original research. (October 2020)">original research?</span></a></i>]</sup> As Debord himself suggested, an understanding of the 'beautiful language' of situationist urbanism necessitates its practice.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup>

Along with [détournement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A9tournement "Détournement"), one of the main Situationist practices is the _dérive_ (French: [\[de.ʁiv\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"), "drift").<sup id="cite_ref-:2_3-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:2-3"><span>[</span>3<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:3-5"><span>[</span>5<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:6_7-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:6-7"><span>[</span>7<span>]</span></a></sup> The dérive is a method of drifting through space to explore how the city is constructed, as well as how it makes us feel. Guy Debord defined the _dérive_ as "a mode of experimental behavior linked to the conditions of urban society: a technique of rapid passage through varied ambiances."<sup id="cite_ref-Debord1958Definitions_16-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-Debord1958Definitions-16"><span>[</span>16<span>]</span></a></sup> He gave a fuller explanation in "Theory of the Dérive" (1956), first written as a member of the [Letterist International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letterist_International "Letterist International"):

> In a dérive one or more persons during a certain period drop their usual motives for movement and action, their relations, their work and leisure activities, and let themselves be drawn by the attractions of the terrain and the encounters they find there… But the dérive includes both this letting go and its necessary contradiction: the domination of psychogeographical variations by the knowledge and calculation of their possibilities.<sup id="cite_ref-Debord1956Theory_17-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-Debord1956Theory-17"><span>[</span>17<span>]</span></a></sup>

The _dérive_'s goals include studying the terrain of the city (psychogeography) and emotional disorientation, both of which lead to the potential creation of [Situations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situationist_International#Etymology_and_usage "Situationist International").<sup id="cite_ref-Debord1956Theory_17-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-Debord1956Theory-17"><span>[</span>17<span>]</span></a></sup>

## Contemporary psychogeography

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Psychogeography&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Contemporary psychogeography")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e2/Enkutatash-sex-majik-2004.GIF/250px-Enkutatash-sex-majik-2004.GIF)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Enkutatash-sex-majik-2004.GIF)

A proliferation of psychogeographical groups in the 90s and early 00s was credited to the re-emergence of the London Psychogeographical Association. evoL PsychogeogrAphix 2003

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/db/Sex-majik-2004.GIF/250px-Sex-majik-2004.GIF)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sex-majik-2004.GIF)

evoL PsychogeogrAphix 2004

In 1987 the psychoanthropologist Howard F. Stein of the University of Oklahoma published _Developmental Time, Cultural Space: Studies in Psychogeography,_ introducing a new way of looking at how human beings deal with geographical entities, and how the latter resonate in their unconscious mind with personal ones. This was followed in 1989 by a book titled _Maps from the Mind: Readings in Psychogeography_, edited by Howard Stein and by William Niederland, an eminent psychoanalyst, which incorporated fifteen chapters on various psychogeographical subjects by interdisciplinary scholars. The main focus of this new psychogeography was the application of psychoanalytic insights to our interactions with geographical entities.

Since the 1990s, as situationist theory became popular in artistic and academic circles, [avant-garde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde "Avant-garde"), [neoist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoist "Neoist"), and [revolutionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary "Revolutionary") groups emerged, developing psychogeographical [praxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praxis_(process) "Praxis (process)") in various ways. Influenced primarily through the re-emergence of the [London Psychogeographical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Psychogeographical_Association "London Psychogeographical Association") and the foundation of [The Workshop for Non-Linear Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Workshop_for_Non-Linear_Architecture "The Workshop for Non-Linear Architecture"), these groups have assisted in the development of a contemporary psychogeography.<sup id="cite_ref-18"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-18"><span>[</span>18<span>]</span></a></sup> Between 1992 and 1996 The Workshop for Non-Linear Architecture undertook an extensive programme of practical research into classic (situationist) psychogeography in both [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow "Glasgow") and London. The discoveries made during this period, documented in the group's journal _Viscosity_, expanded the terrain of the psychogeographic into that of [urban design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_design "Urban design") and architectural performance. Morag Rose has identified three dominant strands in contemporary psychogeography: literary, activist and creative.<sup id="cite_ref-19"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-19"><span>[</span>19<span>]</span></a></sup><sup>:29</sup>

The journal _[Transgressions: A Journal of Urban Exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transgressions:_A_Journal_of_Urban_Exploration "Transgressions: A Journal of Urban Exploration")_ (which appears to have ceased publication sometime in 2000) collated and developed a number of post-avant-garde revolutionary psychogeographical themes. The journal also contributed to the use and development of psychogeographical maps which have, since 2000, been used in political actions, drifts and projections, distributed as flyers.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup> Since 2003 in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"), separate events known as [Provflux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provflux "Provflux") and [Psy-Geo-conflux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psy-Geo-conflux "Psy-Geo-conflux") have been dedicated to action-based participatory experiments, under the academic umbrella of psychogeography. An article on the second annual [Psy-Geo-conflux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psy-Geo-conflux "Psy-Geo-conflux") described psychogeography as "a slightly stuffy term that's been applied to a whole toy box full of playful, inventive strategies for exploring cities."<sup id="cite_ref-20"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-20"><span>[</span>20<span>]</span></a></sup> Psychogeography has also made a methodological appearance in [Library and Information Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_and_Information_Science "Library and Information Science").<sup id="cite_ref-21"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-21"><span>[</span>21<span>]</span></a></sup>

Psychogeography also became a device used in [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature "Literature"). In [Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom "United Kingdom") in particular, psychogeography has become a recognised descriptive term used in discussion of successful writers such as [Iain Sinclair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iain_Sinclair "Iain Sinclair") and [Peter Ackroyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Ackroyd "Peter Ackroyd").<sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-4"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:3-5"><span>[</span>5<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:4_22-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:4-22"><span>[</span>22<span>]</span></a></sup> Sinclair is '\[a\]rguably the most high-profile British psychogeographer' and is credited with having a strong influence on the term's greater public use in the United Kingdom.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-5"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:3-5"><span>[</span>5<span>]</span></a></sup><sup>:9</sup> Though Sinclair makes infrequent use of the jargon associated with the Situationists, he has certainly popularized the term by producing a large body of work based on pedestrian exploration of the urban and suburban landscape.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup> Scholar Duncan Hay asserts that Sinclair's work does not represent the utopian and revolutionary foundations of Situationist practice, and instead 'finds its expression as a literary mode, a position that would have appeared paradoxical to its original practitioners'.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-6"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:3-5"><span>[</span>5<span>]</span></a></sup><sup>:3</sup> Sinclair has distanced himself from the term, declaring it a 'very nasty set of branding'.<sup id="cite_ref-:5_8-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:5-8"><span>[</span>8<span>]</span></a></sup><sup>:19</sup> [Will Self](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_Self "Will Self") also contributed to the popularisation of the term in Great Britain through a column in the Saturday magazine of the national [broadsheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadsheet "Broadsheet") _[The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent "The Independent")_.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-7"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:3-5"><span>[</span>5<span>]</span></a></sup><sup>:11;</sup><sup id="cite_ref-23"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-23"><span>[</span>23<span>]</span></a></sup> The column, which started out in the [British Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Airways "British Airways") [inflight magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflight_magazine "Inflight magazine"), ran in _The Independent_ until October 2008. <sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup>

Sinclair and similar thinkers draw on a longstanding British literary tradition of the exploration of urban landscapes, predating the Situationists, found in the work of writers [William Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blake "William Blake"), [Arthur Machen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Machen "Arthur Machen"), and [Thomas de Quincey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_de_Quincey "Thomas de Quincey"). The nature and history of [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London") were a central focus of these writers, utilising [romantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism "Romanticism"), [gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_novel "Gothic novel"), and [occult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occult "Occult") ideas to describe and transform the city. Sinclair drew on this tradition combined with his own explorations as a way of criticising modern developments of urban space in the key text _Lights Out for the Territory_. Peter Ackroyd's bestselling _London: A Biography_ was partially based on similar sources. Merlin Coverley gives prominence to this literary tradition in his book _Psychogeography_ (2006).<sup id="cite_ref-:4_22-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-:4-22"><span>[</span>22<span>]</span></a></sup> Coverley recognises the situationist origins of psychogeographic practice are sometimes overshadowed by literary traditions, but that they had a shared tradition through writers like [Edgar Allan Poe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Allan_Poe "Edgar Allan Poe"), [Daniel Defoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Defoe "Daniel Defoe"), and Charles Baudelaire.

The documentaries of filmmaker [Patrick Keiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Keiller "Patrick Keiller") are also considered to be an example of psychogeography.<sup id="cite_ref-Tate_Modern_24-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-Tate_Modern-24"><span>[</span>24<span>]</span></a></sup>

The concepts and themes seen in the popular comics' writer [Alan Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Moore "Alan Moore") in _[From Hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/From_Hell "From Hell")_ are also now seen as significant works of psychogeography. Other key figures in this version of the idea are [Walter Benjamin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin "Walter Benjamin"), [J. G. Ballard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._G._Ballard "J. G. Ballard"), and [Nicholas Hawksmoor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Hawksmoor "Nicholas Hawksmoor"). Part of this development saw increasing use of ideas and terminology by some psychogeographers from [Fortean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Fort "Charles Fort") and occult areas including [earth mysteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mysteries "Earth mysteries"), [ley lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ley_lines "Ley lines") and [chaos magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_magic "Chaos magic"), a course pioneered by Sinclair. A core element in virtually all these developments remains a dissatisfaction with the nature and design of the modern environment, and a desire to make the everyday world more interesting.<sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>]</sup>

[Aleksandar Janicijevic](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aleksandar_Janicijevic&action=edit&redlink=1 "Aleksandar Janicijevic (page does not exist)"), the initiator of the Urban Squares Initiative,<sup id="cite_ref-25"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-25"><span>[</span>25<span>]</span></a></sup> defined psychogeography for the group in the following terms: "The subjective analysis–mental reaction, to neighbourhood behaviours related to geographic location. A chronological process based on the order of appearance of observed topics, with the time delayed inclusion of other relevant instances".<sup id="cite_ref-26"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-26"><span>[</span>26<span>]</span></a></sup> In 2013 Aleksandar Janicijevic published "Urbis – Language of the urban fabric" as a visual attempt to rediscover lost or neglected urban symbols. In 2015 another book was published, "MyPsychogeography", an attempt to synthesize sketches and ideas which have informed his art practice.<sup id="cite_ref-27"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-27"><span>[</span>27<span>]</span></a></sup><sup>[<i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Self-published_sources" title="Wikipedia:Verifiability"><span title="The material near this tag may rely on a self-published source. (October 2020)">self-published source?</span></a></i>]</sup>

The 2019 video game _[Disco Elysium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disco_Elysium "Disco Elysium")_ draws heavily on psychogeography to tell its story, particularly through the "Shivers" skill. "Shivers" allows the main character Harry du Bois to commune directly with the fictional city of Revachol, ascribing to it a persona and a consciousness and a memory. The socio-political dynamics of the city, and the player's engagement with them, directly affect Du Bois' identity, as well as the progress of the game. The game engages widely with political ideology, and it uses psychogeography particularly to engage with Marxist and anarchist themes.<sup id="cite_ref-28"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-28"><span>[</span>28<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-29"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychogeography#cite_note-29"><span>[</span>29<span>]</span></a></sup>
