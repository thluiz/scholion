# calm technologies that excite me | abhigyan trips.

I believe that technology should be an enabler, not a subjugator. The tinkerers that love messing with dials and diving into code should continue to do so! But otherwise, technology should help you do a thing and then stand back as you hold the reins of your human experience.

For now, though, my main gripe with my tech interactions is how everything has steps. I have to jump through multiple hoops to reach the one thing I want to do in the moment. And even when I am doing a thing, there's always something else that's trying to grab my attention — an SMS, a UPI reward unlocked, or a spam call for a credit card.

Even when the device isn't trying to distract me, I now have an inbuilt Distractor 5000 in my head that impulsively checks all communication channels, just because it can. The drawback of having a rectangle that can do everything is that it can do literally everything, all the time. That can be, safe to say, quite turbulent.

There was a brief Golden Age I experienced in middle school; I would use a Kindle for reading, my mom's iPod Shuffle for listening to music, and the family computer for any Internet shenanigans. Time felt more dedicated and worry-free back then, even if that's partly because I was still in school. :P

Replicating that exact setup today would have its own problems, ofcourse — music players would be more useful if they had Bluetooth, and e-readers would be more useful with an independent Internet connection (and without companies locking down the software). But we've gotten good at building devices that communicate well with each other, and can do more things when they are offline.

What I dream of is an era of Focused Compute™, where devices target one human function and exist as a thin layer to smoothly do that function.

### The Daylight Computer

E-ink literally moves ink on the screen to change the picture, and doesn't need extra power to maintain the state of that ink. This leads to low power usage and long battery life, and these displays can have paper-like textures and stay visible in direct sunlight, acting more like paper that can change content than a greyscale display. The trade-off is that the ink is slow to change, fine for reading books but not much else — until Anjan Katta came along with Daylight Computing Co., building on Transflective LCDs into something called LivePaper, which stays visible under direct sunlight while running at normal display speed (around 60 frames per second). Their first product, the DC-1, is an Android tablet built around the idea of a "Calm Computer," with homely textured accessories and a very yellow frontlight. It's still their first iteration, doesn't ship to India, and costs $729 USD — a lot for an Android tablet with large bezels and a greyscale screen.

### Restored/Modded iPods

A wave of YouTube videos on restoring iPods — storage or battery upgrades, then USB-C, then custom exteriors, then a fully rooted OS — track a broader shift: as smartphones took over, music players narrowed to either bare-bones playback devices or high-end audiophile gear, and streaming services absorbed the mainstream. Three things pushed the author away from streaming: recommendations that all started sounding the same (see the video "The Playlistification of Music"); increasingly nonsensical, AI-generated pushes into the feed (see "The Music Industry is Broken"); and the fact that streaming revenue splits across every artist a listener streams, in proportion to listening minutes, so a diverse taste means less money to each artist — on top of how labels already treat artists through their contracts. The final push was the news that Spotify's CEO invested heavily in military drones and defense tech using subscription revenue. The author started migrating to a phone app called Classipod that simulates the iPod interface, loading FLAC albums and choosing what to queue deliberately — calmer than the constant background drone of streaming. Purchasing an actual modded iPod in India is still limited to two small outfits, The Revolver Club and RetroPodz, neither of which the author has bought from yet.

### The Clicks Communicator

Looking back at squarish Nokia and Motorola phones before the smartphone era, the author notes how much of today's doomscrolling is tied to the vertical form factor — first short-form video, then platforms like Instagram defaulting back to 4:3 photos. The Clicks Communicator, built by Michael Fischer (Mr. Mobile)'s company Clicks, is a secondary Blackberry-like device meant for communication during working hours, pitched as "focus and simplicity in communication." It has a physical keyboard, a square touchscreen, a fingerprint sensor built into the spacebar, a programmable shortcut key, and a notification light on the side. It doesn't compromise on the extensibility of WhatsApp or maps, unlike stripped-down minimalist phones. The device doesn't exist yet — even the one shown in media coverage is a dummy — the company is only taking reservations, and it doesn't ship to India.

### The TRMNL Ecosystem

TRMNL is an ecosystem of e-ink displays that periodically update as dashboards, either bought directly or built by connecting an old e-reader. Technically, a central web server (self-hosted or provided by trmnl.com) sends images to the e-ink device every 30 seconds; the device only receives, never sends data back, keeping it low-power and passive. Ideas for using it: a display mounted somewhere to check task lists and important emails at a glance, or a smaller display near houseplants showing when they need watering. The whole stack can be self-hosted — an old Kindle as receiver, a Raspberry Pi as server — with a large library of integrations and support for custom plugins.

### Dictaphones

Thoughts worth pursuing pass by like blips that need to be caught and stored before they're lost. WhatsApp messages to self and phone voice recorders both require too many steps between thinking and recording; some phones already support holding a button to record and release to save, but it's usually framed as an AI feature likely sending that data elsewhere. Dictaphones — the kind seen in journalists' and interviewers' hands — are built for exactly this: recording conversations or solo in-the-moment thoughts to revisit later. The goal would be a dictaphone that automatically transcribes at day's end, for searching and summarizing — useful for weeknotes or compiling project thoughts over time — small enough to be a minor addition to an everyday carry. Teenage Engineering's TP-7 field recorder plays music, scrubs through tracks by rotating a disc, and records notes transcribed via a companion app — beautiful, but $1,499 USD.

We've caught on to the fact that gaining more information and speed through our tools doesn't particularly mean we get to relax more; in most cases it just means our deadlines get shorter. A busy day leaves us catching our breath afterwards, and the rest we take needs to keep technology at an arm's length, as much as it can. The desire for calmer compute has grown past the stage of indie projects and tiny scales.
