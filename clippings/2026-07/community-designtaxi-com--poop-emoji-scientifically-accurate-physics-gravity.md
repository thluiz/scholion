The poop emoji has long been the butt of countless jokes, but it turns out the little swirl isn’t a load of crap. According to physicists, the smiling pile is surprisingly faithful to the way gravity shapes the real thing.

A new study published in [_Nature Communications_](http://dx.doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-72566-7) has found that the iconic soft-serve shape of the 💩 emoji closely matches how feces naturally coil when expelled under gravity. Researchers from the University of Amsterdam, Wageningen University & Research, and CNRS concluded that the familiar tapered mound is governed not by biology alone, but by a physical principle known as elastic rope-coiling theory.

The research stemmed from an unexpected observation. While walking along a beach in France, physicist Daniel Bonn noticed the neatly stacked castings produced by lugworms. Unlike most animals, which expel waste downward, lugworms push it upward from their underground burrows. Their droppings formed uniform cylindrical towers rather than pointed spirals, prompting the team to investigate why different species leave behind such distinct shapes.

To find the answer, the researchers recreated the process in the laboratory using materials including pea dough and pasta. Their experiments revealed that everything from feces to worm castings and soft extruded foods follows the same coiling behavior. As material falls under gravity, each new loop lands from a slightly lower height than the last, causing the coils to gradually shrink and form the familiar cone-shaped pile. When the material is instead pushed upward, as with lugworms, each loop remains almost identical in size, producing a straight-sided column instead.

The findings also solve a puzzle that dates back more than 140 years. Charles Darwin carefully documented the spiral castings left by earthworms in 1881 but could not explain how they formed. The new research suggests that gravity, the stiffness of the material, and the direction in which it is extruded are enough to account for the shapes seen across different species, without invoking any specialized biological mechanism.

  
The scientists even embraced the lighter side of their work. Since lugworm towers differ so dramatically from the classic poop emoji, they proposed that the Unicode Consortium consider adding a second version based on the worm's cylindrical castings.  

Beyond settling an unexpectedly relatable question, the study demonstrates how ordinary sights can reveal universal laws of physics. Sometimes, even the most humorous subjects can point researchers toward serious scientific discoveries.

So it seems the poop emoji wasn't full of it after all.
