Unsolved problem in mathematics

Do all non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function have a real part equal to one half?

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/30/Riemann_zeta_function_absolute_value.png/330px-Riemann_zeta_function_absolute_value.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Riemann_zeta_function_absolute_value.png)

This plot of Riemann's zeta (![{\\displaystyle \\zeta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d5c3916703cae7938143d38865f78f27faadd4ae)) function (here with argument ![{\\displaystyle z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bf368e72c009decd9b6686ee84a375632e11de98)) shows trivial zeros where ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta (z)=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/63f5e81207723577ab5df73862cbbcd60ae2fb5c), a pole where _ζ_(_z_) → ![{\\displaystyle \\infty }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c26c105004f30c27aa7c2a9c601550a4183b1f21), the _critical line_ of nontrivial zeros with [Re(_z_)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers#Definition_and_basic_operations "Complex numbers") = 1/2 and density of absolute values.

In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), the **Riemann hypothesis** is the [conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjecture "Conjecture") that the [Riemann zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_zeta_function "Riemann zeta function") has its [zeros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function "Root of a function") only at the negative [even integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_integer "Even integer") and at [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number "Complex number") with [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part "Real part") ![{\\displaystyle \\textstyle {\\frac {1}{2}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f4b147775b52f71eacf8937ff124ac92f0574d2). Many consider it to be the most important [unsolved problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_unsolved_problems_in_mathematics "List of unsolved problems in mathematics") in [pure mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_mathematics "Pure mathematics").[\[1\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBombieri2000-1) It is of great interest in [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory") because it implies results about the distribution of [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_numbers "Prime numbers"). It was proposed by [Bernhard Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernhard_Riemann "Bernhard Riemann"),[\[2\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTERiemann1859-2) after whom it is named. There is overwhelming numerical evidence for the hypothesis, but no proof is known.[\[3\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEConnes2026-3)

The Riemann hypothesis and some of its generalizations, along with [Goldbach's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldbach's_conjecture "Goldbach's conjecture") and the [twin prime conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime_conjecture "Twin prime conjecture"), make up [Hilbert's eighth problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_eighth_problem "Hilbert's eighth problem") in [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert")'s list of [twenty-three unsolved problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_problems "Hilbert's problems"); it is also one of the [Millennium Prize Problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Prize_Problems "Millennium Prize Problems") of the [Clay Mathematics Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_Mathematics_Institute "Clay Mathematics Institute"), which offers [US$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US$ "US$")1 million for a solution to any of them. The name is also used for some closely related analogues, some of which have been proved, such as the [Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_hypothesis_for_curves_over_finite_fields "Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields"), which was proved by [André Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9_Weil "André Weil").

The Riemann zeta function ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d5c3916703cae7938143d38865f78f27faadd4ae) is a [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_\(mathematics\) "Function (mathematics)") whose [argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_of_a_function "Argument of a function") may be any complex number other than 1, and whose values are also complex. It has zeros at the negative even integers; that is, ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta (s)=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/18590bfd730d03c72bb83e3a7e5affa9388dfc10) when ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632) is one of ![{\\displaystyle -2,-4,-6,\\dots }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d78445e396587fe1c44ea7cd103e370077f4ec79) These are called its _[trivial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triviality_\(mathematics\) "Triviality (mathematics)")_ zeros. The zeta function is also zero for other values of ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632), which are called _non-trivial_ zeros. The Riemann hypothesis is concerned with the locations of these non-trivial zeros, and states that:

The real part of every non-trivial zero of the Riemann zeta function is ![{\\displaystyle \\textstyle {\\frac {1}{2}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f4b147775b52f71eacf8937ff124ac92f0574d2).

Thus, the hypothesis states that all the nontrivial zeros lie on the _critical line_, consisting of the complex numbers ![{\\displaystyle {\\tfrac {1}{2}}+it}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/db573a4f2b83955f507228bde4180eff79c0ae4b) where ![{\\displaystyle t}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/65658b7b223af9e1acc877d848888ecdb4466560) is a [real number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number "Real number") and ![{\\displaystyle i}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/add78d8608ad86e54951b8c8bd6c8d8416533d20) is the [imaginary unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_unit "Imaginary unit").

## Riemann zeta function

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Riemann zeta function")\]

The [Riemann zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_zeta_function "Riemann zeta function") is defined for complex ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632) with real part greater than 1 by the [absolutely convergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_convergence "Absolute convergence") [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series "Infinite series") ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta (s)=\\sum \_{n=1}^{\\infty }{\\frac {1}{n^{s}}}={\\frac {1}{1^{s}}}+{\\frac {1}{2^{s}}}+{\\frac {1}{3^{s}}}+\\cdots }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a171e24fc1c6d990c65e7fe82805613da4645645) [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler "Leonhard Euler") considered this series in the 1730s for real values of ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632), in conjunction with his solution to the [Basel problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basel_problem "Basel problem"). He also proved that it equals the [Euler product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_product "Euler product") ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta (s)=\\prod \_{p{\\text{ prime}}}{\\frac {1}{1-p^{-s}}}={\\frac {1}{1-2^{-s}}}\\cdot {\\frac {1}{1-3^{-s}}}\\cdot {\\frac {1}{1-5^{-s}}}\\cdot {\\frac {1}{1-7^{-s}}}\\cdots }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3370bb9a713f7399eb728749ee303488b0d3bb26) where the [infinite product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_product "Infinite product") extends over all prime numbers ![{\\displaystyle p}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/81eac1e205430d1f40810df36a0edffdc367af36).[\[4\]](#cite_note-4)

The Riemann hypothesis discusses zeros outside the [region of convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region_of_convergence "Region of convergence") of this series and Euler product. To make sense of the hypothesis, it is necessary to [analytically continue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_continuation "Analytic continuation") the function to obtain a form that is valid for all complex ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632). Because the zeta function is [meromorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meromorphic "Meromorphic"), all choices of how to perform this analytic continuation will lead to the same result, by the [identity theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_theorem "Identity theorem"). A first step in this continuation observes that the series for the zeta function and the [Dirichlet eta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_eta_function "Dirichlet eta function") satisfy the relation ![{\\displaystyle \\left(1-{\\frac {2}{2^{s}}}\\right)\\zeta (s)=\\eta (s)=\\sum \_{n=1}^{\\infty }{\\frac {(-1)^{n+1}}{n^{s}}}={\\frac {1}{1^{s}}}-{\\frac {1}{2^{s}}}+{\\frac {1}{3^{s}}}-\\cdots ,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/26585f4e86a40f70a9fffb4886747c3c4634c05f) within the region of convergence for both series. But the eta function series on the right converges not just when the real part of ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632) is greater than one, but more generally whenever ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632) has positive real part. Thus, the zeta function can be redefined as ![{\\displaystyle \\eta (s)/(1-2/2^{s})}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fa27a7ed3f22b5f02eabb1e793174829e8b7374), extending it from ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {Re} (s)>1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/82e22215d90eb4893d05f9b2bff409094a3451f2) to the larger domain ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {Re} (s)>0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ce0992e3b29be0b1bb2feec2e4682f43fe61c38b), except for the points where ![{\\displaystyle 1-2/2^{s}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1574b3084d18ec8997f0f1537434eb55f96d6715) is zero. These are the points ![{\\displaystyle s=1+2\\pi in/\\log 2}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e7cec6a31a1556eef77dd177a7937d4173fea9f8), where ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) can be any nonzero integer; the zeta function can be extended to these values too by taking limits (see the article on the [Dirichlet eta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_eta_function#Landau's_problem_with_%CE%B6\(s\)_=_%CE%B7\(s\)/0_and_solutions "Dirichlet eta function")), giving a finite value for all values of ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632) with positive real part except the [simple pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_pole "Simple pole") at ![{\\displaystyle s=1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bac386d8f227fb823cede9b3e33d706cad3ed306).

In the strip ![{\\displaystyle 0<\\operatorname {Re} (s)<1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/32b9718e606940f63859292d9607b611a545b9d5) this extension of the zeta function satisfies the [functional equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_zeta_function#Riemann's_functional_equation "Riemann zeta function") ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta (s)=2^{s}\\pi ^{s-1}\\ \\sin \\left({\\frac {\\pi s}{2}}\\right)\\ \\Gamma (1-s)\\ \\zeta (1-s).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/37b4eab44e38b188e9cdde9f8d8344ca79a980ec) One may then define ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta (s)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cbd45922057e4d7a5718ce5ed703ab493c63897a) for all remaining nonzero complex numbers ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632) (![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {Re} (s)\\leq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/271fcd0aa61e19b573afe22ab761e0ae3dbeaa71) and ![{\\displaystyle s\\neq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b696e0b457eecf592be8e2fda417810807613c99)) by applying this equation outside the strip, and letting ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta (s)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cbd45922057e4d7a5718ce5ed703ab493c63897a) equal the right side of the equation whenever ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632) has non-positive real part (and ![{\\displaystyle s\\neq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b696e0b457eecf592be8e2fda417810807613c99)).

If ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632) is a negative even integer, then ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta (s)=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/18590bfd730d03c72bb83e3a7e5affa9388dfc10), because the factor ![{\\displaystyle \\sin(\\pi s/2)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/52055094e830bb84f31f924556e55070053172ca) vanishes; these are the zeta function's _trivial zeros_. (If ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632) is a positive even integer this argument does not apply because the zeros of the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine "Sine") function are canceled by the poles of the [gamma function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_function "Gamma function") as it takes negative integer arguments.)

The value [_ζ_(0) \= −1/2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_+_1_+_1_+_1_+_%E2%8B%AF "1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + ⋯") is not determined by the functional equation, but is the limiting value of ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta (s)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cbd45922057e4d7a5718ce5ed703ab493c63897a) as ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632) approaches zero. The functional equation also implies that the zeta function has no zeros with negative real part other than the trivial zeros, so all nontrivial zeros lie in the _critical strip_ where ![{\\displaystyle s}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01d131dfd7673938b947072a13a9744fe997e632) has real part between 0 and 1.

> _... es ist sehr wahrscheinlich, dass alle Wurzeln reell sind. Hiervon wäre allerdings ein strenger Beweis zu wünschen; ich habe indess die Aufsuchung desselben nach einigen flüchtigen vergeblichen Versuchen vorläufig bei Seite gelassen, da er für den nächsten Zweck meiner Untersuchung entbehrlich schien._
> 
> ... it is very probable that all roots are real. Of course one would wish for a rigorous proof here; I have for the time being, after some fleeting vain attempts, provisionally put aside the search for this, as it appears dispensable for the immediate objective of my investigation.

— Riemann's statement of the Riemann hypothesis, from ([Riemann 1859](#CITEREFRiemann1859)). (He was discussing a variant of the zeta function, modified in a way that the real line be mapped to the critical line.)

> At the death of Riemann, a note was found among his papers, saying "These properties of _ζ_(_s_) (the function in question) are deduced from an expression of it which, however, I did not succeed in simplifying enough to publish it." We still have not the slightest idea of what the expression could be. As to the properties he simply enunciated, some thirty years elapsed before I was able to prove all of them but one \[the Riemann Hypothesis itself\].

— [Jacques Hadamard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Hadamard "Jacques Hadamard"), The Mathematician's Mind, VIII. Paradoxical Cases of Intuition

Riemann's original motivation for studying the zeta function and its zeros was their occurrence in his [explicit formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explicit_formulae_\(L-function\) "Explicit formulae (L-function)") for the [number of primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime-counting_function "Prime-counting function") ![{\\displaystyle \\pi (x)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5a62d9bf7b2ba03b30a95e7059362cc4b3865123) less than or equal to a given number ![{\\displaystyle x}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87f9e315fd7e2ba406057a97300593c4802b53e4), which he published in his 1859 paper "[On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Number_of_Primes_Less_Than_a_Given_Magnitude "On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude")". His formula was given in terms of the related function

![{\\displaystyle \\Pi (x)=\\pi (x)+{\\frac {\\pi (x^{1/2})}{2}}+{\\frac {\\pi (x^{1/3})}{3}}+{\\frac {\\pi (x^{1/4})}{4}}+{\\frac {\\pi (x^{1/5})}{5}}+{\\frac {\\pi (x^{1/6})}{6}}+\\cdots }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c650061b0415db4164f522877423fb288d2b12fc)

which counts the primes and prime powers up to ![{\\displaystyle x}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87f9e315fd7e2ba406057a97300593c4802b53e4), counting a [prime power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_power "Prime power") ![{\\displaystyle p^{n}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c6a7a7e74ae90ab94f01e1629177758fb68b423b) as ![{\\displaystyle 1/n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f0e10667bad240500f5044257143510127e03d69). The number of primes can be recovered from this function by using the [Möbius inversion formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6bius_inversion_formula "Möbius inversion formula"):

![{\\displaystyle {\\begin{aligned}\\pi (x)&=\\sum \_{n=1}^{\\infty }{\\frac {\\mu (n)}{n}}\\Pi (x^{1/n})\\\\&=\\Pi (x)-{\\frac {1}{2}}\\Pi (x^{1/2})-{\\frac {1}{3}}\\Pi (x^{1/3})-{\\frac {1}{5}}\\Pi (x^{1/5})+{\\frac {1}{6}}\\Pi (x^{1/6})-\\cdots ,\\end{aligned}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2a673883c81557bae9a40ffbc4277381929eae38)

where ![{\\displaystyle \\mu }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fd47b2a39f7a7856952afec1f1db72c67af6161) is the [Möbius function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6bius_function "Möbius function"). Riemann's formula is then

![{\\displaystyle \\Pi \_{0}(x)=\\operatorname {li} (x)-\\sum \_{\\rho }\\operatorname {li} (x^{\\rho })-\\log 2+\\int \_{x}^{\\infty }{\\frac {dt}{t(t^{2}-1)\\log t}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3317a4c59302040455f56621454d7e1abd4998ea)

where the sum is over the nontrivial zeros of the zeta function and where ![{\\displaystyle \\Pi \_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0ff8b022bb527c2e0006fa9d6fd740ee9b9eab52) is a slightly modified version of ![{\\displaystyle \\Pi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/eed3e3db6cc2028a183af948212ed2551d25c954) that replaces its value at its points of [discontinuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discontinuity_\(mathematics\) "Discontinuity (mathematics)") by the average of its upper and lower limits: ![{\\displaystyle \\Pi \_{0}(x)=\\lim \_{\\varepsilon \\to 0}{\\frac {\\Pi (x-\\varepsilon )+\\Pi (x+\\varepsilon )}{2}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5da6ea49e9a91be9aa3e8f5960bf7474bad62e74)

The summation in Riemann's formula is not absolutely convergent, but may be evaluated by taking the zeros ![{\\displaystyle \\rho }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f7d439671d1289b6a816e6af7a304be40608d64) in order of the absolute value of their imaginary part. The function ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {li} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b5d59f5dd40d4a2be9d55f0d6b3334fbafd16775) occurring in the first term is the (unoffset) [logarithmic integral function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_integral_function "Logarithmic integral function") given by the [Cauchy principal value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_principal_value "Cauchy principal value") of the divergent integral ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {li} (x)=\\int \_{0}^{x}{\\frac {dt}{\\log t}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87e28601cb75c3e46aa750fe63e6fdc0ec5e3f6e)

The terms ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {li} (x^{\\rho })}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/88ec167c50b4ad99deeb6a40e49aa608bdf742ba) involving the zeros of the zeta function need some care in their definition as ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {li} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b5d59f5dd40d4a2be9d55f0d6b3334fbafd16775) has branch points at 0 and 1, and are defined (for ![{\\displaystyle x>1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0549e1fb7ee2023519833093c6e3b60236e7d09f)) by analytic continuation in the complex variable ![{\\displaystyle \\rho }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f7d439671d1289b6a816e6af7a304be40608d64) in the region ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {Re} (\\rho )>0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1cbc648544b0df884e71e3401af056109980692a); i.e., they should be considered as [Ei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_integral "Exponential integral")(_ρ_ log _x_). The other terms also correspond to zeros: the dominant term ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {li} (x)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f0f7c22225b6931ab65c480fc209b2dfc4b71f9e) comes from the pole at ![{\\displaystyle s=1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bac386d8f227fb823cede9b3e33d706cad3ed306), considered as a zero of multiplicity ![{\\displaystyle -1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/704fb0427140d054dd267925495e78164fee9aac), and the remaining small terms come from the trivial zeros. For some graphs of the sums of the first few terms of this series see [Riesel & Göhl (1970)](#CITEREFRieselGöhl1970) or [Zagier (1977)](#CITEREFZagier1977).

This formula says that the zeros of the Riemann zeta function control the [oscillations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillation "Oscillation") of primes around their "expected" positions. Riemann knew that the non-trivial zeros of the zeta function were symmetrically distributed about the line ![{\\displaystyle s=1/2+it}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/487b065fd87414a5ad0306d816d5dc99419ec8bd), and he knew that all of its non-trivial zeros must lie in the range ![{\\displaystyle 0\\leq \\operatorname {Re} (s)\\leq 1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/25dd554a6a6372de5f579e1e3c1a5caf33b10ce6). He checked that a few of the zeros lay on the critical line with real part ![{\\displaystyle 1/2}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e308a3a46b7fdce07cc09dcab9e8d8f73e37d935) and suggested that they all do; this is the Riemann hypothesis.

> The result has caught the imagination of most mathematicians because it is so unexpected, connecting two seemingly unrelated areas in mathematics; namely, [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), which is the study of the discrete, and [complex analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analysis "Complex analysis"), which deals with continuous processes.

— ([Burton 2006](#CITEREFBurton2006), p. 376)

The practical uses of the Riemann hypothesis include many propositions known to be true under the Riemann hypothesis, and some that can be shown to be equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis.

### Distribution of prime numbers

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Distribution of prime numbers")\]

[Riemann's explicit formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann's_explicit_formula "Riemann's explicit formula") for [the number of primes less than a given number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime-counting_function "Prime-counting function") states that, in terms of a sum over the zeros of the Riemann zeta function, the magnitude of the oscillations of primes around their expected position is controlled by the real parts of the zeros of the zeta function. In particular, the error term in the [prime number theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem "Prime number theorem") is closely related to the position of the zeros. For example, if ![{\\displaystyle \\beta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7ed48a5e36207156fb792fa79d29925d2f7901e8) is the [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound "Upper bound") of the real parts of the zeros, then[\[6\]](#cite_note-6) ![{\\displaystyle \\pi (x)-\\operatorname {li} (x)=O\\!\\left(x^{\\beta }\\log x\\right)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e62a05a24d04511d7852a11a1c82bbc162558396), where ![{\\displaystyle \\pi (x)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5a62d9bf7b2ba03b30a95e7059362cc4b3865123) is the [prime-counting function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime-counting_function "Prime-counting function") and ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {li} (x)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f0f7c22225b6931ab65c480fc209b2dfc4b71f9e) is the [logarithmic integral function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_integral_function "Logarithmic integral function"). It is already known that ![{\\displaystyle 1/2\\leq \\beta \\leq 1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3dbf1f4cf3177a053cd7e21452afc2211bbe6660).[\[7\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEIngham193282-7)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/89/Riemann_Explicit_Formula.gif/500px-Riemann_Explicit_Formula.gif?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Riemann_Explicit_Formula.gif)

Corrections to an [estimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime-counting_function#Exact_form "Prime-counting function") of the prime-counting function using zeros of the zeta function. The magnitude of the correction term is determined by the real part of the zero being added in the correction.

[Helge von Koch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helge_von_Koch "Helge von Koch") proved that the Riemann hypothesis implies the "best possible" bound for the error of the prime number theorem.[\[8\]](#cite_note-8) A precise version of von Koch's result, due to [Schoenfeld (1976)](#CITEREFSchoenfeld1976), says that the Riemann hypothesis implies ![{\\displaystyle |\\pi (x)-\\operatorname {li} (x)|<{\\frac {1}{8\\pi }}{\\sqrt {x}}\\log(x)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/71042f821d5934818c6fa33f94da784c1fa76c11) for all ![{\\displaystyle x\\geq 2657}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a8381a07cb24b939b87e5f094a214ef586ad5ac8). [Schoenfeld (1976)](#CITEREFSchoenfeld1976) also showed that the Riemann hypothesis implies ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi (x)-x|<{\\frac {1}{8\\pi }}{\\sqrt {x}}\\log ^{2}x}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/261c8a346ea0ce6159e6721ba096aaa1fd55cf23) for all ![{\\displaystyle x\\geq 73.2}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b203dccd3a73a8d1e4fb87e4c119a4143794dfb9), where ![{\\displaystyle \\psi (x)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a596a1fb4130a47f6b88c66150497338bd6cbccc) is [Chebyshev's second function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chebyshev_function "Chebyshev function").

Adrian Dudek[\[9\]](#cite_note-9) proved that the Riemann hypothesis implies that for ![{\\displaystyle x\\geq 2}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5e4cf59b5136062d043f79c2bf0c6af0443c1a), there is a prime ![{\\displaystyle p}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/81eac1e205430d1f40810df36a0edffdc367af36) satisfying ![{\\displaystyle x-{\\frac {4}{\\pi }}{\\sqrt {x}}\\log x<p\\leq x.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/584244aaeca942988ad08219c8b2807f63b0b7b1) The constant ![{\\displaystyle 4/\\pi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6c8d3d5cd470c4717a771e3fd7c59be6aff85457) may be reduced to ![{\\displaystyle 1+\\varepsilon }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1ab16dbacd42afa5d0b85c0511f3ecef34a0bdcb) provided that ![{\\displaystyle x}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87f9e315fd7e2ba406057a97300593c4802b53e4) is taken to be sufficiently large. This is an explicit version of a theorem of [Cramér](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cram%C3%A9r "Cramér").

### Growth of arithmetic functions

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Growth of arithmetic functions")\]

The Riemann hypothesis implies strong bounds on the growth of many other [arithmetic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_function "Arithmetic function"), in addition to the primes counting function above.

One example involves the [Möbius function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6bius_function "Möbius function") _μ_. The statement that the equation ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {1}{\\zeta (s)}}=\\sum \_{n=1}^{\\infty }{\\frac {\\mu (n)}{n^{s}}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/44321887183e072812ddd22b8ed103e08ced1e0d) is valid for every _s_ with real part greater than 1/2, with the sum on the right hand side converging, is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis. From this we can also conclude that if the [Mertens function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mertens_function "Mertens function") is defined by ![{\\displaystyle M(x)=\\sum \_{n\\leq x}\\mu (n)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/056958331c498fd0fdb9b4b76c1101a97f352d0b) then the claim that ![{\\displaystyle M(x)=O\\left(x^{{\\frac {1}{2}}+\\varepsilon }\\right)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cb10bacf11c4be4bdd6c9aaf58fcd8139d9e91e7) for every positive _ε_ is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis ([J. E. Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Edensor_Littlewood "John Edensor Littlewood"), 1912; see for instance: paragraph 14.25 in [Titchmarsh (1986)](#CITEREFTitchmarsh1986)). The [determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinant "Determinant") of the order _n_ [Redheffer matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redheffer_matrix "Redheffer matrix") is equal to _M_(_n_), so the Riemann hypothesis can also be stated as a condition on the growth of these determinants. Littlewood's result has been improved several times since then, by [Edmund Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Landau "Edmund Landau"),[\[10\]](#cite_note-10) [Edward Charles Titchmarsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Charles_Titchmarsh "Edward Charles Titchmarsh"),[\[11\]](#cite_note-11) Helmut Maier and [Hugh Montgomery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Lowell_Montgomery "Hugh Lowell Montgomery"),[\[12\]](#cite_note-12) and [Kannan Soundararajan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannan_Soundararajan "Kannan Soundararajan").[\[13\]](#cite_note-13) Soundararajan's result is that, conditional on the Riemann hypothesis, ![{\\displaystyle M(x)=O\\left(x^{1/2}\\exp \\left((\\log x)^{1/2}(\\log \\log x)^{14}\\right)\\right).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/db7558d9816dcaf155f4d6147f02aef077dc3226) The Riemann hypothesis puts a rather tight bound on the growth of _M_, since [Odlyzko & te Riele (1985)](#CITEREFOdlyzkote_Riele1985) disproved the slightly stronger [Mertens conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mertens_conjecture "Mertens conjecture") ![{\\displaystyle |M(x)|\\leq {\\sqrt {x}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9a932e1ee98741315c0f17350dc706e3d46f4d8a)

Another closely related result is due to [Björner (2011)](#CITEREFBjörner2011), that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the statement that the [Euler characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_characteristic "Euler characteristic") of the [simplicial complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_complex "Simplicial complex") determined by the lattice of integers under divisibility is ![{\\displaystyle o(n^{1/2+\\epsilon })}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0ed1676c40c79a388508440123f6775fbdb1f840) for all ![{\\displaystyle \\epsilon >0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/568095ad3924314374a5ab68fae17343661f2a71) (see [incidence algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidence_algebra "Incidence algebra")).

The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to many other conjectures about the rate of growth of other arithmetic functions aside from _μ_(_n_). A typical example is [Robin's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin's_theorem "Robin's theorem"),[\[14\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTERobin1984-14) which states that if _σ_(_n_) is the [sigma function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisor_function "Divisor function"), given by ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma (n)=\\sum \_{d\\mid n}d}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7a6dfd779b351698b174a64e351ff24363f53ec4) then ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma (n)<e^{\\gamma }n\\log \\log n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e169504a533384cc31b217ecb6749fc634a22fd7) for all _n_ > 5040 [if and only if](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/If_and_only_if "If and only if") the Riemann hypothesis is true, where _γ_ is the [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler%E2%80%93Mascheroni_constant "Euler–Mascheroni constant").

A related bound was given by [Jeffrey Lagarias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_Lagarias "Jeffrey Lagarias") in 2002, who proved that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the statement that: ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma (n)<H\_{n}+\\log(H\_{n})e^{H\_{n}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/148380732511f106a301ee66be10dc7647dc1f96) for every [natural number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_number "Natural number") _n_ > 1, where ![{\\displaystyle H\_{n}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e63458b04288bbe116a9a8037dfae0b36b2c639a) is the _n_th [harmonic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_number "Harmonic number").[\[15\]](#cite_note-15)

The Riemann hypothesis is also true if and only if the inequality ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {n}{\\varphi (n)}}<e^{\\gamma }\\log \\log n+{\\frac {e^{\\gamma }(4+\\gamma -\\log 4\\pi )}{\\sqrt {\\log n}}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fee6f34ce047c953cc5a99479e5c587642abdc12) is true for all _n_ ≥ 120569#, where _φ_(_n_) is [Euler's totient function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_totient_function "Euler's totient function") and 120569# is the [product of the first](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primorial "Primorial") 120569 primes.[\[16\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBroughan2017Corollary_5.35-16)

Another example was found by [Jérôme Franel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%A9r%C3%B4me_Franel "Jérôme Franel"), and extended by [Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Landau "Edmund Landau") (see [Franel & Landau (1924)](#CITEREFFranelLandau1924)). The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to several statements showing that the terms of the [Farey sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farey_sequence "Farey sequence") are fairly regular. One such equivalence is as follows: if _F__n_ is the Farey sequence of order _n_, beginning with 1/_n_ and up to 1/1, then the claim that for all _ε_ > 0 ![{\\displaystyle \\sum \_{i=1}^{m}|F\_{n}(i)-{\\tfrac {i}{m}}|=O\\left(n^{{\\frac {1}{2}}+\\epsilon }\\right)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e38ddf9978774a967f61dda3761c009357af0282) is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis. Here ![{\\displaystyle m=\\sum \_{i=1}^{n}\\varphi (i)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/945d026e6e0be10664dc5ca9e896a63b9c836a67) is the number of terms in the Farey sequence of order _n_.

For an example from [group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory "Group theory"), if _g_(_n_) is [Landau's function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landau's_function "Landau's function") given by the maximal order of elements of the [symmetric group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_group "Symmetric group") S_n_ of degree _n_, then [Massias, Nicolas & Robin (1988)](#CITEREFMassiasNicolasRobin1988) showed that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the bound ![{\\displaystyle \\log g(n)<{\\sqrt {\\operatorname {Li} ^{-1}(n)}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b1eb21548aa954dda976377acda88a686e8aead5) for all sufficiently large _n_.

### Lindelöf hypothesis and growth of the zeta function

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Lindelöf hypothesis and growth of the zeta function")\]

The Riemann hypothesis has various weaker consequences as well; one is the [Lindelöf hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindel%C3%B6f_hypothesis "Lindelöf hypothesis") on the rate of growth of the zeta function on the critical line, which says that, for any _ε_ > 0, ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta \\left({\\frac {1}{2}}+it\\right)=O(t^{\\varepsilon }),}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b3e9bee994272d9ba9708c8cb04dc69e6d301dce) as _t_ → ![{\\displaystyle \\infty }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c26c105004f30c27aa7c2a9c601550a4183b1f21).

The Riemann hypothesis also implies quite sharp bounds for the growth rate of the zeta function in other regions of the critical strip. For example, it implies that ![{\\displaystyle e^{\\gamma }\\leq \\limsup \_{t\\rightarrow +\\infty }{\\frac {|\\zeta (1+it)|}{\\log \\log t}}\\leq 2e^{\\gamma }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/41ac8bc052acf151cc33786517847aceabb1b709) ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {6}{\\pi ^{2}}}e^{\\gamma }\\leq \\limsup \_{t\\rightarrow +\\infty }{\\frac {1/|\\zeta (1+it)|}{\\log \\log t}}\\leq {\\frac {12}{\\pi ^{2}}}e^{\\gamma }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/47c2012914f7d66e8c1d928b5c4168b10dbf5f65) so the growth rate of _ζ_(1 + _it_) and its inverse would be known up to a factor of 2.[\[17\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTETitchmarsh1986-17)

### Large prime gap conjecture

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Large prime gap conjecture")\]

The prime number theorem implies that on average, the [gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_gap "Prime gap") between the prime _p_ and its successor is log _p_. However, some gaps between primes may be much larger than the average. Cramér proved that, assuming the Riemann hypothesis, every gap is _O_(√_p_ log _p_). This is a case in which even the best bound that can be proved using the Riemann hypothesis is far weaker than what seems true: [Cramér's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cram%C3%A9r's_conjecture "Cramér's conjecture") implies that every gap is _O_((log _p_)2), which, while larger than the average gap, is far smaller than the bound implied by the Riemann hypothesis. Numerical evidence supports Cramér's conjecture.[\[18\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTENicely1999-18)

### Analytic criteria equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Analytic criteria equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis")\]

Many statements equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis have been found, though so far none of them have led to much progress in proving (or disproving) it. Some typical examples are as follows. (Others involve the [divisor function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisor_function#Growth_rate "Divisor function") _σ_(_n_).)

The [Riesz criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riesz_criterion "Riesz criterion") was given by [Riesz (1916)](#CITEREFRiesz1916), to the effect that the bound ![{\\displaystyle -\\sum \_{k=1}^{\\infty }{\\frac {(-x)^{k}}{(k-1)!\\zeta (2k)}}=O\\left(x^{{\\frac {1}{4}}+\\epsilon }\\right)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f64126def053989510ecf50fa50fbdf85a83d678) holds for all ε > 0 if and only if the Riemann hypothesis holds. See also the [Hardy–Littlewood criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riesz_function#Hardy%E2%80%93Littlewood_criterion "Riesz function").

[Nyman (1950)](#CITEREFNyman1950) proved that the Riemann hypothesis is true if and only if the space of functions of the form ![{\\displaystyle f(x)=\\sum \_{\\nu =1}^{n}c\_{\\nu }\\rho \\left({\\frac {\\theta \_{\\nu }}{x}}\\right)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2ffded3ca297993db53663f6156a6586daac2c9e) where _ρ_(_z_) is the fractional part of _z_, 0 ≤ _θ__ν_ ≤ 1, and ![{\\displaystyle \\sum \_{\\nu =1}^{n}c\_{\\nu }\\theta \_{\\nu }=0,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c6e2d97dc055772113a8675a29bf68c383f4439a) is dense in the [Hilbert space _L_2(0,1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lp_space "Lp space") of square-integrable functions on the unit interval. [Beurling (1955)](#CITEREFBeurling1955) extended this by showing that the zeta function has no zeros with real part greater than 1/_p_ if and only if this function space is dense in _Lp_(0,1). This Nyman-Beurling criterion was strengthened by Baez-Duarte[\[19\]](#cite_note-baez-19) to the case where ![{\\displaystyle \\theta \_{\\nu }\\in \\{1/k\\}\_{k\\geq 1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/56ae7130d3bec0d1f12198c13a3aa1176dcacf03).

[Salem (1953)](#CITEREFSalem1953) showed that the Riemann hypothesis is true if and only if the [integral equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_equation "Integral equation") ![{\\displaystyle \\int \_{0}^{\\infty }{\\frac {z^{-\\sigma -1}\\varphi (z)}{{e^{x/z}}+1}}\\,dz=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a82945c356b02cc6d44b9f15db9424041245b23a) has no non-trivial bounded solutions ![{\\displaystyle \\varphi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/33ee699558d09cf9d653f6351f9fda0b2f4aaa3e) for ![{\\displaystyle 1/2<\\sigma <1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/dfa6bf55a169c6fbedef7fbb1eef0768059e0561).

[Weil's criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weil's_criterion "Weil's criterion") is the statement that the positivity of a certain function is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis. Related is [Li's criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li's_criterion "Li's criterion"), a statement that the positivity of a certain sequence of numbers is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis.

[Speiser (1934)](#CITEREFSpeiser1934) proved that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the statement that _ζ_′(_s_), the derivative of _ζ_(_s_), has no zeros in the strip ![{\\displaystyle 0<\\Re (s)<{\\frac {1}{2}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6be3c2ab0ab9da4312ccfdbe7301a5af3c07f8a4)

That _ζ_(_s_) has only simple zeros on the critical line is equivalent to its derivative having no zeros on the critical line.

The [Farey sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farey_sequence#Riemann_hypothesis "Farey sequence") provides two equivalences, due to [Jerome Franel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_Franel "Jerome Franel") and [Edmund Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Landau "Edmund Landau") in 1924.

The [de Bruijn–Newman constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Bruijn%E2%80%93Newman_constant "De Bruijn–Newman constant") denoted by Λ and named after [Nicolaas Govert de Bruijn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaas_Govert_de_Bruijn "Nicolaas Govert de Bruijn") and [Charles M. Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_M._Newman "Charles M. Newman"), is defined as the unique real number such that the [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_\(mathematics\) "Function (mathematics)") ![{\\displaystyle H(\\lambda ,z):=\\int \_{0}^{\\infty }e^{\\lambda u^{2}}\\Phi (u)\\cos(zu)\\,du,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d130d66731afeffdf93365687f8613274fe7365d) that is parametrised by a real parameter _λ_, has a complex variable _z_ and is defined using a super-exponentially decaying function ![{\\displaystyle \\Phi (u)=\\sum \_{n=1}^{\\infty }(2\\pi ^{2}n^{4}e^{9u}-3\\pi n^{2}e^{5u})e^{-\\pi n^{2}e^{4u}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/54c22c62b710a7561e33ab46ca7e7eed123ab5ad) has only real zeros if and only if _λ_ ≥ Λ. Since the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the claim that all the zeros of _H_(0, _z_) are real, the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the conjecture that Λ ≤ 0. Brad Rodgers and [Terence Tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence_Tao "Terence Tao") discovered the equivalence is actually Λ = 0 by proving zero to be the lower bound of the constant.[\[20\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersTao2020-20) Proving zero is also the upper bound would therefore prove the Riemann hypothesis. Newman noted that this conjecture (now theorem) "is a quantitative version of the dictum that the Riemann hypothesis, if true, is only barely so."[\[21\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTENewman1976-21) As of April 2020 the upper bound is Λ ≤ 0.2.[\[22\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlattTrudgian2021-22)

### Consequences of the generalized Riemann hypothesis

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Consequences of the generalized Riemann hypothesis")\]

Several applications use the [generalized Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Riemann_hypothesis "Generalized Riemann hypothesis") for [Dirichlet L-series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_L-series "Dirichlet L-series") or [zeta functions of number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind_zeta_function "Dedekind zeta function") rather than just the Riemann hypothesis. Many basic properties of the Riemann zeta function can easily be generalized to all Dirichlet L-series, so it is plausible that a method that proves the Riemann hypothesis for the Riemann zeta function would also work for the generalized Riemann hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions. Several results first proved using the generalized Riemann hypothesis were later given unconditional proofs without using it, though these were usually much harder. Many of the consequences on the following list are taken from [Conrad (2010)](#CITEREFConrad2010).

*   In 1913, [Grönwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hakon_Gr%C3%B6nwall "Thomas Hakon Grönwall") showed that the generalized Riemann hypothesis implies that Gauss's [list of imaginary quadratic fields with class number 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_problem "Class number problem") is complete, though Baker, Stark and Heegner later gave unconditional proofs of this without using the generalized Riemann hypothesis.
*   In 1917, Hardy and Littlewood showed that the generalized Riemann hypothesis implies a conjecture of Chebyshev that ![{\\displaystyle \\lim \_{x\\to 1^{-}}\\sum \_{p>2}(-1)^{(p+1)/2}x^{p}=+\\infty ,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87f985ea88bc9a21e500bd4b0c5cbd26ef95bd6d) which says that primes 3 mod 4 are more common than primes 1 mod 4 in some sense. (For related results, see _[Prime number theorem § Prime number race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem#Prime_number_race "Prime number theorem")_.)
*   In 1923, Hardy and Littlewood showed that the generalized Riemann hypothesis implies a weak form of the [Goldbach conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldbach_conjecture "Goldbach conjecture") for odd numbers: that every sufficiently large odd number is the sum of three primes, though in 1937 Vinogradov gave an unconditional proof. In 1997 [Deshouillers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Marc_Deshouillers "Jean-Marc Deshouillers"), Effinger, [te Riele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_te_Riele "Herman te Riele"), and Zinoviev showed that the generalized Riemann hypothesis implies that every odd number greater than 5 is the sum of three primes. In 2013 [Harald Helfgott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Helfgott "Harald Helfgott") proved the ternary Goldbach conjecture without the GRH dependence, subject to some extensive calculations completed with the help of David J. Platt.
*   In 1934, Chowla showed that the generalized Riemann hypothesis implies that the first prime in the arithmetic progression _a_ mod _m_ is at most _Km_2log(_m_)2 for some fixed constant _K_.
*   In 1967, Hooley showed that the generalized Riemann hypothesis implies [Artin's conjecture on primitive roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin's_conjecture_on_primitive_roots "Artin's conjecture on primitive roots").
*   In 1973, Weinberger showed that the generalized Riemann hypothesis implies that Euler's list of [idoneal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idoneal_number "Idoneal number") is complete.
*   [Weinberger (1973)](#CITEREFWeinberger1973) showed that the generalized Riemann hypothesis for the zeta functions of all algebraic number fields implies that any number field with class number 1 is either [Euclidean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_domain "Euclidean domain") or an imaginary quadratic number field of [discriminant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discriminant_of_an_algebraic_number_field "Discriminant of an algebraic number field") −19, −43, −67, or −163.
*   In 1976, G. Miller showed that the generalized Riemann hypothesis implies that one can [test if a number is prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primality_test "Primality test") in polynomial time via the [Miller test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller%E2%80%93Rabin_primality_test "Miller–Rabin primality test"). In 2002, Manindra Agrawal, Neeraj Kayal and Nitin Saxena proved this result unconditionally using the [AKS primality test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AKS_primality_test "AKS primality test").
*   [Odlyzko (1990)](#CITEREFOdlyzko1990) discussed how the generalized Riemann hypothesis can be used to give sharper estimates for discriminants and class numbers of number fields.
*   [Ono & Soundararajan (1997)](#CITEREFOnoSoundararajan1997) showed that the generalized Riemann hypothesis implies that [Ramanujan's integral quadratic form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan's_ternary_quadratic_form "Ramanujan's ternary quadratic form") _x_2 + _y_2 + 10_z_2 represents all integers that it represents locally, with exactly 18 exceptions.
*   In 2021, Alexander (Alex) Dunn and [Maksym Radziwill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maksym_Radziwill "Maksym Radziwill") proved [Patterson's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patterson's_conjecture?action=edit&redlink=1 "Patterson's conjecture (page does not exist)") on cubic [Gauss sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss_sums "Gauss sums"), under the assumption of the GRH.[\[23\]](#cite_note-23)[\[24\]](#cite_note-Dunn_Radziwiłł_2021_Patterson-24)

Some consequences of the RH are also consequences of its negation, and are thus theorems. In their discussion of the [Hecke, Deuring, Mordell, Heilbronn theorem](#Gauss's_class_number_conjecture), [Ireland & Rosen (1990](#CITEREFIrelandRosen1990), p. 359) say

> The method of proof here is truly amazing. If the generalized Riemann hypothesis is true, then the theorem is true. If the generalized Riemann hypothesis is false, then the theorem is true. Thus, the theorem is true!!

Care should be taken to understand what is meant by saying the generalized Riemann hypothesis is false: one should specify exactly which class of Dirichlet series has a counterexample.

#### Littlewood's theorem

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Littlewood's theorem")\]

This concerns the sign of the error in the [prime number theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem "Prime number theorem"). It has been computed that π(_x_) < li(_x_) for all _x_ ≤ 1025 (see this [table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem#Table_of_%CF%80\(x\),_x_/_log_x,_and_li\(x\) "Prime number theorem")), and no value of _x_ is known for which π(_x_) > li(_x_).

In 1914, Littlewood proved that there are arbitrarily large values of _x_ for which ![{\\displaystyle \\pi (x)>\\operatorname {li} (x)+{\\frac {1}{3}}{\\frac {\\sqrt {x}}{\\log x}}\\log \\log \\log x,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7b8233ec67e8879db2e365b4601f71bba6e8801f) and that there are also arbitrarily large values of _x_ for which ![{\\displaystyle \\pi (x)<\\operatorname {li} (x)-{\\frac {1}{3}}{\\frac {\\sqrt {x}}{\\log x}}\\log \\log \\log x.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ef4dfff50eac6a6b33351cff2b4fef00b2df27b4) Thus the difference π(_x_) − li(_x_) changes sign infinitely many times. [Skewes' number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skewes'_number "Skewes' number") is an estimate of the value of _x_ corresponding to the first sign change.

Littlewood's proof is divided into two cases: the RH is assumed false (about half a page of [Ingham 1932](#CITEREFIngham1932), Chapt. V), and the RH is assumed true (about a dozen pages). Stanisław Knapowski followed this up with a paper on the number of times ![{\\displaystyle \\Delta (n)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/97e96f5bdee7658a850d540628950cb9a5e9e49c) changes sign in the interval ![{\\displaystyle \\Delta (n)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/97e96f5bdee7658a850d540628950cb9a5e9e49c).[\[25\]](#cite_note-25)

#### Gauss's class number conjecture

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Gauss's class number conjecture")\]

This is the [conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_problem "Class number problem") (first stated in article 303 of Gauss's _[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disquisitiones_Arithmeticae "Disquisitiones Arithmeticae")_) that there are only finitely many imaginary quadratic fields with a given class number. One way to prove it would be to show that as the discriminant _D_ → −∞ the class number _h_(_D_) → ∞.

The following sequence of theorems involving the Riemann hypothesis is described in [Ireland & Rosen 1990](#CITEREFIrelandRosen1990), pp. 358–361:

**Theorem (Hecke; 1918)**—Let _D_ < 0 be the discriminant of an imaginary [quadratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_irrational_number "Quadratic irrational number") [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field "Number field") _K_. Assume the generalized Riemann hypothesis for [_L_\-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-function "L-function") of all imaginary quadratic Dirichlet characters. Then there is an absolute constant _C_ such that ![{\\displaystyle h(D)>C{\\frac {\\sqrt {|D|}}{\\log |D|}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d952a15867da8e64956e8a2172ae738ea7d70131)

**Theorem (Deuring; 1933)**—If the RH is false then _h_(_D_) > 1 if |_D_| is sufficiently large.

**Theorem (Mordell; 1934)**—If the RH is false then _h_(_D_) → ∞ as _D_ → −∞.

**Theorem (Heilbronn; 1934)**—If the generalized RH is false for the _L_\-function of some imaginary quadratic Dirichlet character then _h_(_D_) → ∞ as _D_ → −∞.

(In the work of Hecke and Heilbronn, the only [_L_\-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-function "L-function") that occur are those attached to imaginary quadratic characters, and it is only for those _L_\-functions that _GRH is true_ or _GRH is false_ is intended; a failure of GRH for the _L_\-function of a cubic Dirichlet character would, strictly speaking, mean GRH is false, but that was not the kind of failure of GRH that Heilbronn had in mind, so his assumption was more restricted than simply _GRH is false_.)

In 1935, [Carl Siegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Siegel "Carl Siegel") strengthened the result without using RH or GRH in any way.[\[26\]](#cite_note-Goldfeld_1985_pp._23–37-26)[\[27\]](#cite_note-Siegel_1935_pp._83–86-27)

#### Growth of Euler's totient

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Growth of Euler's totient")\]

In 1983 [J. L. Nicolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Louis_Nicolas "Jean-Louis Nicolas") proved that ![{\\displaystyle \\varphi (n)<e^{-\\gamma }{\\frac {n}{\\log \\log n}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/98953eb8832acb6f3cf92c1ef1d18da322c3cd77) for infinitely many _n_, where _φ_(_n_) is [Euler's totient function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_totient_function "Euler's totient function") and _γ_ is [Euler's constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_constant "Euler's constant"). Ribenboim remarks that: "The method of proof is interesting, in that the inequality is shown first under the assumption that the Riemann hypothesis is true, secondly under the contrary assumption."[\[28\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTERibenboim1996320-28)

## Generalizations and analogs

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Generalizations and analogs")\]

### Dirichlet L-series and other number fields

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Dirichlet L-series and other number fields")\]

The Riemann hypothesis can be generalized by replacing the Riemann zeta function by the formally similar, but much more general, global [L-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-function "L-function"). In this broader setting, one expects the non-trivial zeros of the global _L_\-functions to have real part 1/2. It is these conjectures, rather than the classical Riemann hypothesis only for the single Riemann zeta function, which account for the true importance of the Riemann hypothesis in mathematics.

The most common [generalized Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Riemann_hypothesis "Generalized Riemann hypothesis") extends the Riemann hypothesis to all [Dirichlet L-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_L-function "Dirichlet L-function"). In particular it implies the conjecture that [Siegel zeros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegel_zero "Siegel zero") (zeros of _L_\-functions between 1/2 and 1) do not exist.

The [extended Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_Riemann_hypothesis "Extended Riemann hypothesis") extends the Riemann hypothesis to all [Dedekind zeta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind_zeta_function "Dedekind zeta function") of [algebraic number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_number_field "Algebraic number field"). Since Dedekind zeta function for abelian extension of the rationals can be expressed as product of Dirichlet L-functions and only possible pole is in 1 for Riemann zeta function (thus no pole can cancel nontrivial zero), this version of Riemann hypothesis implies generalized Riemann hypothesis.

The Riemann hypothesis can also be extended to the _L_\-functions of [Hecke characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hecke_character "Hecke character") of number fields. Since Dirichlet L-functions are Hecke L-functions for finite characters, then this hypothesis directly implies generalized Riemann hypothesis. Dedekind zeta functions can be expressed as product of Hecke L-functions and only possible pole of Hecke L-function is at 1, then this version of Riemann Hypothesis implies also version for Dedekind zeta functions.

There are two approaches for extension of Riemann hypothesis that seem to be the most general. The [grand Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Riemann_hypothesis "Grand Riemann hypothesis") extends it to all [Automorphic L-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_L-function "Automorphic L-function"), such as [Mellin transforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mellin_transform "Mellin transform") of [Hecke eigenforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hecke_eigenform "Hecke eigenform"). The Riemann hypothesis for [Selberg class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selberg_class "Selberg class") extends it rather for functions satisfying some properties (at least conjecturaly satisfied by most functions usually called _zeta functions_ or _L-functions_) than for functions defined by direct formula. Though it is expected that Selberg class should be equal to class of automorphic L-functions, and thus this approaches should be equivalent, this is important open problem itself and part of [Langlands program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langlands_program "Langlands program").

### Function fields and zeta functions of varieties over finite fields

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Function fields and zeta functions of varieties over finite fields")\]

[Artin (1924)](#CITEREFArtin1924) introduced global zeta functions of (quadratic) [function fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_field_of_an_algebraic_variety "Function field of an algebraic variety") and conjectured an analogue of the Riemann hypothesis for them, which has been proved by Hasse in the genus 1 case and by [Weil (1948)](#CITEREFWeil1948) in general. For instance, the fact that the [Gauss sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_Gauss_sum "Quadratic Gauss sum"), of the quadratic character of a [finite field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_field "Finite field") of size _q_ (with _q_ odd), has absolute value ![{\\displaystyle {\\sqrt {q}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fec68a26ee361e6e7450c74fadea8334650ec369) is actually an instance of the Riemann hypothesis in the function field setting. This led [Weil (1949)](#CITEREFWeil1949) to conjecture a similar statement for all [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_variety "Algebraic variety"); the resulting [Weil conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weil_conjectures "Weil conjectures") were proved by [Pierre Deligne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Deligne "Pierre Deligne").[\[29\]](#cite_note-29)

### Arithmetic zeta functions of arithmetic schemes and their L-factors

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Arithmetic zeta functions of arithmetic schemes and their L-factors")\]

[Arithmetic zeta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_zeta_function "Arithmetic zeta function") generalise the Riemann and Dedekind zeta functions as well as the zeta functions of varieties over finite fields to every arithmetic scheme or a scheme of finite type over integers. The arithmetic zeta function of a regular connected [equidimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equidimensionality "Equidimensionality") arithmetic scheme of Kronecker dimension _n_ can be factorized into the product of appropriately defined L-factors and an auxiliary factor.[\[30\]](#cite_note-30) Assuming a functional equation and meromorphic continuation, the generalized Riemann hypothesis for the L-factor states that its zeros inside the critical strip ![{\\displaystyle \\Re (s)\\in (0,n)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d17074a0a626149034199b2dd2346d45afc300b9) lie on the central line. Correspondingly, the generalized Riemann hypothesis for the arithmetic zeta function of a regular connected equidimensional arithmetic scheme states that its zeros inside the critical strip lie on vertical lines ![{\\displaystyle \\Re (s)=1/2,3/2,\\dots ,n-1/2}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e52fdece6300359f08b38dfeb60dece82ee7c2a6) and its poles inside the critical strip lie on vertical lines ![{\\displaystyle \\Re (s)=1,2,\\dots ,n-1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6337ab20a3c0799a24b50108c204fc5a51538f96). This is known for schemes in positive characteristic and follows from Pierre Deligne[\[31\]](#cite_note-31), but remains entirely unknown in characteristic zero.

### Selberg zeta functions

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Selberg zeta functions")\]

[Selberg (1956)](#CITEREFSelberg1956) introduced the [Selberg zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selberg_zeta_function "Selberg zeta function") of a Riemann surface. These are similar to the Riemann zeta function: they have a functional equation, and an infinite product similar to the Euler product but taken over closed geodesics rather than primes. The [Selberg trace formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selberg_trace_formula "Selberg trace formula") is the analogue for these functions of the [explicit formulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explicit_formula_\(L-function\) "Explicit formula (L-function)") in prime number theory. Selberg proved that the Selberg zeta functions satisfy the analogue of the Riemann hypothesis, with the imaginary parts of their zeros related to the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue "Eigenvalue") of the Laplacian operator of the Riemann surface.

### Ihara zeta functions

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Ihara zeta functions")\]

The [Ihara zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ihara_zeta_function "Ihara zeta function") of a finite graph is an analogue of the [Selberg zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selberg_zeta_function "Selberg zeta function"), which was first introduced by [Yasutaka Ihara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasutaka_Ihara "Yasutaka Ihara") in the context of discrete subgroups of the two-by-two p-adic special linear group. A regular finite graph is a [Ramanujan graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan_graph "Ramanujan graph"), a mathematical model of efficient communication networks, if and only if its Ihara zeta function satisfies the analogue of the Riemann hypothesis as was pointed out by [T. Sunada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshikazu_Sunada "Toshikazu Sunada").

### Montgomery's pair correlation conjecture

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Montgomery's pair correlation conjecture")\]

[Montgomery (1973)](#CITEREFMontgomery1973) suggested the [pair correlation conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pair_correlation_conjecture "Pair correlation conjecture") that the correlation functions of the (suitably normalized) zeros of the zeta function should be the same as those of the eigenvalues of a [random hermitian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_unitary_ensemble "Gaussian unitary ensemble"). [Odlyzko (1987)](#CITEREFOdlyzko1987) showed that this is supported by large-scale numerical calculations of these correlation functions.

Montgomery showed that (assuming the Riemann hypothesis) at least 2/3 of all zeros are simple, and a related conjecture is that all zeros of the zeta function are simple (or more generally have no non-trivial integer linear relations between their imaginary parts). [Dedekind zeta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind_zeta_function "Dedekind zeta function") of algebraic number fields, which generalize the Riemann zeta function, often do have multiple complex zeros.[\[32\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTERadziejewski2007-32) This is because the Dedekind zeta functions factorize as a product of powers of [Artin L-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin_L-function "Artin L-function"), so zeros of Artin L-functions sometimes give rise to multiple zeros of Dedekind zeta functions. Other examples of zeta functions with multiple zeros are the L-functions of some [elliptic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve "Elliptic curve"): these can have multiple zeros at the real point of their critical line; the [Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer_conjecture "Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture") predicts that the multiplicity of this zero is the rank of the elliptic curve.

### Other zeta functions

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Other zeta functions")\]

There are [many other examples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeta_function "Zeta function") of zeta functions with analogues of the Riemann hypothesis, some of which have been proved. [Goss zeta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goss_zeta_function "Goss zeta function") of function fields have a Riemann hypothesis, proved by [Sheats (1998)](#CITEREFSheats1998). [The main conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_conjecture_of_Iwasawa_theory "Main conjecture of Iwasawa theory") of [Iwasawa theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwasawa_theory "Iwasawa theory"), proved by [Barry Mazur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Mazur "Barry Mazur") and [Andrew Wiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Wiles "Andrew Wiles") for [cyclotomic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotomic_field "Cyclotomic field"), and Wiles for [totally real fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_real_number_field "Totally real number field"), identifies the zeros of a _p_\-adic _L_\-function with the eigenvalues of an operator, so can be thought of as an analogue of the [Hilbert–Pólya conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert%E2%80%93P%C3%B3lya_conjecture "Hilbert–Pólya conjecture") for [_p_\-adic _L_\-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_L-function "P-adic L-function").[\[33\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWiles2000-33)

Several mathematicians have addressed the Riemann hypothesis, but none of their attempts has yet been accepted as a proof. [Watkins (2021)](#CITEREFWatkins2021) lists some incorrect solutions.

Hilbert and Pólya suggested that one way to derive the Riemann hypothesis would be to find a [self-adjoint operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint_operator "Self-adjoint operator"), from the existence of which the statement on the real parts of the zeros of _ζ_(_s_) would follow when one applies the criterion on real [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue "Eigenvalue"). Some support for this idea comes from several analogues of the Riemann zeta functions whose zeros correspond to eigenvalues of some operator: the zeros of a zeta function of a variety over a finite field correspond to eigenvalues of a [Frobenius element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_element "Frobenius element") on an [étale cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89tale_cohomology "Étale cohomology") group, the zeros of a [Selberg zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selberg_zeta_function "Selberg zeta function") are eigenvalues of a [Laplacian operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian_operator "Laplacian operator") of a Riemann surface, and the zeros of a [p-adic zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_zeta_function "P-adic zeta function") correspond to eigenvectors of a Galois action on [ideal class groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_class_group "Ideal class group").

[Odlyzko (1987)](#CITEREFOdlyzko1987) showed that the distribution of the zeros of the Riemann zeta function shares some statistical properties with the eigenvalues of [random matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_matrices "Random matrices") drawn from the [Gaussian unitary ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_unitary_ensemble "Gaussian unitary ensemble"). This gives some support to the [Hilbert–Pólya conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert%E2%80%93P%C3%B3lya_conjecture "Hilbert–Pólya conjecture").

In 1999, [Michael Berry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Berry_\(physicist\) "Michael Berry (physicist)") and [Jonathan Keating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Keating "Jonathan Keating") conjectured that there is some unknown quantization ![{\\displaystyle {\\hat {H}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6bb06de5217295d7fbdbf68fb9c5309a513fc99e) of the classical Hamiltonian _H_ = _xp_ so that ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta (1/2+i{\\hat {H}})=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d7c8f2f2893f97acb39fbd737d94cdcffd45ad38) and even more strongly, that the Riemann zeros coincide with the spectrum of the operator ![{\\displaystyle 1/2+i{\\hat {H}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/df4a5d77237b34a6da979165f8216e6fe09d91fd). This is in contrast to [canonical quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_quantization "Canonical quantization"), which leads to the [Heisenberg uncertainty principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heisenberg_uncertainty_principle "Heisenberg uncertainty principle") ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma \_{x}\\sigma \_{p}\\geq {\\frac {\\hbar }{2}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/dfad4605bd5cc074dba10330bd766f60b113fa1b) and the [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers "Natural numbers") as spectrum of the [quantum harmonic oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_harmonic_oscillator "Quantum harmonic oscillator"). The crucial point is that the Hamiltonian should be a self-adjoint operator so that the quantization would be a realization of the Hilbert–Pólya program. In a connection with this quantum mechanical problem Berry and Connes had proposed that the inverse of the potential of the Hamiltonian is connected to the [half-derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-derivative "Half-derivative") of the function ![{\\displaystyle N(s)={\\frac {1}{\\pi }}\\operatorname {Arg} \\xi (1/2+i{\\sqrt {s}})}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3a06dc29b5dee299bb6be167f294980537dc1658) then, in Hilbert-Polya approach ![{\\displaystyle V^{-1}(x)={\\sqrt {4\\pi }}{\\frac {d^{1/2}N(x)}{dx^{1/2}}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e604d5781f5e09500156fd29aeb60c240d31f44d) This yields a Hamiltonian whose eigenvalues are the square of the imaginary part of the Riemann zeros, and also that the [functional determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_determinant "Functional determinant") of this [Hamiltonian operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_operator "Hamiltonian operator") is just the [Riemann Xi function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_Xi_function "Riemann Xi function"). In fact the Riemann Xi function would be proportional to the functional determinant ([Hadamard product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadamard_product_\(matrices\) "Hadamard product (matrices)")) ![{\\displaystyle \\det(H+1/4+s(s-1))}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a697894da6fa0bac7056f9f11160f95ab0e6cf5d) ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {\\xi (s)}{\\xi (0)}}={\\frac {\\det(H+s(s-1)+1/4)}{\\det(H+1/4)}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b889a21be626801a95dbf32502a6706ca45ce65e) However this operator is not useful in practice since it includes the inverse function (implicit function) of the potential but not the potential itself. The analogy with the Riemann hypothesis over [finite fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_field "Finite field") suggests that the Hilbert space containing eigenvectors corresponding to the zeros might be some sort of first [cohomology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_group "Cohomology group") of the [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_of_a_ring "Spectrum of a ring") Spec (_Z_) of the integers. [Deninger (1998)](#CITEREFDeninger1998) described some of the attempts to find such a cohomology theory.[\[34\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeichtnam2005-34)

[Zagier (1981)](#CITEREFZagier1981) constructed a natural space of invariant functions on the upper half plane that has eigenvalues under the Laplacian operator that correspond to zeros of the Riemann zeta function—and remarked that in the unlikely event that one could show the existence of a suitable positive definite inner product on this space, the Riemann hypothesis would follow. [Cartier (1982)](#CITEREFCartier1982) discussed a related example, where due to a bizarre bug a computer program listed zeros of the Riemann zeta function as eigenvalues of the same [Laplacian operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian_operator "Laplacian operator").

[Schumayer & Hutchinson (2011)](#CITEREFSchumayerHutchinson2011) surveyed some of the attempts to construct a suitable physical model related to the Riemann zeta function.

The [Lee–Yang theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee%E2%80%93Yang_theorem "Lee–Yang theorem") states that the zeros of certain [partition functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_function_\(statistical_mechanics\) "Partition function (statistical mechanics)") in [statistical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics") all lie on a "critical line" with their real part equal to 0, and this has led to some speculation about a relationship with the Riemann hypothesis.[\[35\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnauf1999-35)

[Pál Turán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A1l_Tur%C3%A1n "Pál Turán") showed that if the functions ![{\\displaystyle \\sum \_{n=1}^{N}n^{-s}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/80313e1f98aa8dcaf8b2543713ea1a6f8948c7f7) have no zeros when the real part of _s_ is greater than one then ![{\\displaystyle T(x)=\\sum \_{n\\leq x}{\\frac {\\lambda (n)}{n}}\\geq 0{\\text{ for }}x>0,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d19e84b13ee72081ccf1b776c455a4f635150270) where λ(_n_) is the [Liouville function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville_function "Liouville function") given by (−1)_r_ if _n_ has _r_ prime factors.[\[36\]](#cite_note-36) He showed that this in turn would imply that the Riemann hypothesis is true. But [Haselgrove (1958)](#CITEREFHaselgrove1958) proved that _T_(_x_) is negative for infinitely many _x_ (and also disproved the closely related [Pólya conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%B3lya_conjecture "Pólya conjecture")), and [Borwein, Ferguson & Mossinghoff (2008)](#CITEREFBorweinFergusonMossinghoff2008) showed that the smallest such _x_ is 72185376951205. [Spira (1968)](#CITEREFSpira1968) showed by numerical calculation that the finite [Dirichlet series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_series "Dirichlet series") above for _N_ = 19 has a zero with real part greater than 1. Turán also showed that a somewhat weaker assumption, the nonexistence of zeros with real part greater than 1 + _N_−1/2+_ε_ for large _N_ in the finite Dirichlet series above, would also imply the Riemann hypothesis, but [Montgomery (1983)](#CITEREFMontgomery1983) showed that for all sufficiently large _N_ these series have zeros with real part greater than 1 + (log log _N_)/(4 log _N_). Therefore, Turán's result is [vacuously true](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuous_truth "Vacuous truth") and cannot help prove the Riemann hypothesis.

### Noncommutative geometry

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Noncommutative geometry")\]

[Alain Connes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alain_Connes "Alain Connes") has described a relationship between the Riemann hypothesis and [noncommutative geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_geometry "Noncommutative geometry"), and showed that a suitable analog of the [Selberg trace formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selberg_trace_formula "Selberg trace formula") for the action of the [idèle class group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Id%C3%A8le_class_group "Idèle class group") on the adèle class space would imply the Riemann hypothesis.[\[37\]](#cite_note-37) Some of these ideas are elaborated in [Lapidus (2008)](#CITEREFLapidus2008).

### Hilbert spaces of entire functions

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section: Hilbert spaces of entire functions")\]

[Louis de Branges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_de_Branges_de_Bourcia "Louis de Branges de Bourcia") showed that the Riemann hypothesis would follow from a positivity condition on a certain [Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_space "Hilbert space") of [entire functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entire_function "Entire function").[\[38\]](#cite_note-38) However [Conrey & Li (2000)](#CITEREFConreyLi2000) showed that the necessary positivity conditions are not satisfied. Despite this obstacle, de Branges has continued to work on an attempted proof of the Riemann hypothesis along the same lines, but this has not been widely accepted by other mathematicians.[\[39\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESarnak2005-39)

The Riemann hypothesis implies that the zeros of the zeta function form a [quasicrystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicrystal "Quasicrystal"), a distribution with discrete support whose [Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform "Fourier transform") also has discrete support. [Dyson (2009)](#CITEREFDyson2009) suggested trying to prove the Riemann hypothesis by classifying, or at least studying, 1-dimensional quasicrystals.

### Arithmetic zeta functions of models of elliptic curves over number fields

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=29 "Edit section: Arithmetic zeta functions of models of elliptic curves over number fields")\]

When one goes from geometric dimension one, e.g. an [algebraic number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_number_field "Algebraic number field"), to geometric dimension two, e.g. a regular model of an [elliptic curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve "Elliptic curve") over a number field, the two-dimensional part of the generalized Riemann hypothesis for the [arithmetic zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_zeta_function "Arithmetic zeta function") of the model deals with the poles of the zeta function. In dimension one the study of the zeta integral in [Tate's thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tate's_thesis "Tate's thesis") does not lead to new important information on the Riemann hypothesis. Contrary to this, in dimension two work of [Ivan Fesenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Fesenko "Ivan Fesenko") on two-dimensional generalisation of Tate's thesis includes an integral representation of a zeta integral closely related to the zeta function. In this new situation, not possible in dimension one, the poles of the zeta function can be studied via the zeta integral and associated adele groups. Related conjecture of [Ivan Fesenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Fesenko "Ivan Fesenko") on the positivity of the fourth derivative of a boundary function associated to the zeta integral essentially implies the pole part of the generalized Riemann hypothesis.[\[40\]](#cite_note-40) Suzuki ([2011](#CITEREFSuzuki2011)) proved that the latter, together with some technical assumptions, implies Fesenko's conjecture.

### Multiple zeta functions

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=30 "Edit section: Multiple zeta functions")\]

Deligne's proof of the Riemann hypothesis over finite fields used the zeta functions of product varieties, whose zeros and poles correspond to sums of zeros and poles of the original zeta function, in order to bound the real parts of the zeros of the original zeta function. By analogy, [Kurokawa (1992)](#CITEREFKurokawa1992) introduced multiple zeta functions whose zeros and poles correspond to sums of zeros and poles of the Riemann zeta function. To make the series converge he restricted to sums of zeros or poles all with non-negative imaginary part. So far, the known bounds on the zeros and poles of the multiple zeta functions are not strong enough to give useful estimates for the zeros of the Riemann zeta function.

## Location of the zeros

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=31 "Edit section: Location of the zeros")\]

The functional equation combined with the [argument principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_principle "Argument principle") implies that the number of zeros of the zeta function with imaginary part between 0 and _T_ is given by ![{\\displaystyle N(T)={\\frac {1}{\\pi }}\\mathop {\\mathrm {Arg} } (\\xi (s))={\\frac {1}{\\pi }}\\mathop {\\mathrm {Arg} } (\\Gamma ({\\tfrac {s}{2}})\\pi ^{-{\\frac {s}{2}}}\\zeta (s)s(s-1)/2)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e6898e859ada0de3d5e0be5e44ca56a21bd10580) for _s_ = 1/2 + _iT_, where the argument is defined by varying it continuously along the line with Im(_s_) = _T_, starting with argument 0 at ∞ + _iT_. This is the sum of a large but well understood term ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {1}{\\pi }}\\mathop {\\mathrm {Arg} } (\\Gamma ({\\tfrac {s}{2}})\\pi ^{-s/2}s(s-1)/2)={\\frac {T}{2\\pi }}\\log {\\frac {T}{2\\pi }}-{\\frac {T}{2\\pi }}+7/8+O(1/T)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8e0c219b0cb4246f8fcf09894f106d865172422c) and a small but rather mysterious term ![{\\displaystyle S(T)={\\frac {1}{\\pi }}\\mathop {\\mathrm {Arg} } (\\zeta (1/2+iT))=O(\\log T).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cbb3d13d7fd48fa8151267ce66aed90854dd761c) So the density of zeros with imaginary part near _T_ is about log(_T_)/(2π), and the function _S_ describes the small deviations from this. The function _S_(_t_) jumps by 1 at each zero of the zeta function, and for _t_ ≥ 8 it decreases [monotonically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonically_decreasing "Monotonically decreasing") between zeros with derivative close to −log _t_.

[Trudgian (2014)](#CITEREFTrudgian2014) proved that, if _T_ > _e_, then

![{\\displaystyle |N(T)-{\\frac {T}{2\\pi }}\\log {\\frac {T}{2\\pi e}}|\\leq 0.112\\log T+0.278\\log \\log T+3.385+{\\frac {0.2}{T}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e9093b1a0700052f92df5b4fef453df42a43be6f)

[Karatsuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolii_Alexeevitch_Karatsuba "Anatolii Alexeevitch Karatsuba") (1996) proved that every interval (_T_, _T_ + _H_\] for ![{\\displaystyle H\\geq T^{{\\frac {27}{82}}+\\varepsilon }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/deb900d659d59ff997c06e31da1601b3a66d1ccf) contains at least ![{\\displaystyle H(\\log T)^{\\frac {1}{3}}e^{-c{\\sqrt {\\log \\log T}}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/db130eb44e39bc37608b3be7a818daf57bd5d084) points where the function _S_(_t_) changes sign.

[Selberg (1946)](#CITEREFSelberg1946) showed that the average moments of even powers of _S_ are given by ![{\\displaystyle \\int \_{0}^{T}|S(t)|^{2k}dt={\\frac {(2k)!}{k!(2\\pi )^{2k}}}T(\\log \\log T)^{k}+O(T(\\log \\log T)^{k-1/2}).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fe755065e201b43888916edee42e9e9f5409b0fe)

This suggests that _S_(_T_)/(log log _T_)1/2 resembles a [Gaussian random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_random_variable "Gaussian random variable") with mean 0 and variance 2π2 ([Ghosh (1983)](#CITEREFGhosh1983) proved this fact). In particular |_S_(_T_)| is usually somewhere around (log log _T_)1/2, but occasionally much larger. The exact order of growth of _S_(_T_) is not known. There has been no unconditional improvement to Riemann's original bound _S_(_T_) = _O_(log _T_), though the Riemann hypothesis implies the slightly smaller bound _S_(_T_) = _O_(log _T_/log log _T_).[\[17\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTETitchmarsh1986-17) The true order of magnitude may be somewhat less than this, as random functions with the same distribution as _S_(_T_) tend to have growth of order about log(_T_)1/2. In the other direction it cannot be too small: [Selberg (1946)](#CITEREFSelberg1946) showed that _S_(_T_) ≠ _o_((log _T_)1/3/(log log _T_)7/3), and assuming the Riemann hypothesis Montgomery showed that _S_(_T_) ≠ _o_((log _T_)1/2/(log log _T_)1/2).

Numerical calculations confirm that _S_ grows very slowly: |_S_(_T_)| < 1 for _T_ < 280, |_S_(_T_)| < 2 for _T_ < 6800000, and the largest value of |_S_(_T_)| found so far is not much larger than 3.[\[41\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEOdlyzko2002-41)

Riemann's estimate _S_(_T_) = _O_(log _T_) implies that the gaps between zeros are bounded, and Littlewood improved this slightly, showing that the gaps between their imaginary parts tend to 0.

### Theorem of Hadamard and de la Vallée-Poussin

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=33 "Edit section: Theorem of Hadamard and de la Vallée-Poussin")\]

[Hadamard (1896)](#CITEREFHadamard1896) and [de la Vallée-Poussin (1896)](#CITEREFde_la_Vallée-Poussin1896) independently proved that no zeros could lie on the line Re(_s_) = 1. Together with the functional equation and the fact that there are no zeros with real part greater than 1, this showed that all non-trivial zeros must lie in the interior of the critical strip 0 < Re(_s_) < 1. This was a key step in their first proofs of the [prime number theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem "Prime number theorem").

Both the original proofs that the zeta function has no zeros with real part 1 are similar, and depend on showing that if _ζ_(1 + _it_) vanishes, then _ζ_(1 + 2_it_) is singular, which is not possible. One way of doing this is by using the inequality ![{\\displaystyle |\\zeta (\\sigma )^{3}\\zeta (\\sigma +it)^{4}\\zeta (\\sigma +2it)|\\geq 1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/773e95c78967b55426f55e3ad1db4e4826d1fc9b) for _σ_ > 1, _t_ real, and looking at the limit as _σ_ → 1. This inequality follows by taking the real part of the log of the Euler product to see that ![{\\displaystyle |\\zeta (\\sigma +it)|=\\exp \\Re \\sum \_{p^{n}}{\\frac {p^{-n(\\sigma +it)}}{n}}=\\exp \\sum \_{p^{n}}{\\frac {p^{-n\\sigma }\\cos(t\\log p^{n})}{n}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4270949065294a17ae8553308b54ef1a0bda58ad) where the sum is over all prime powers _p__n_, so that ![{\\displaystyle |\\zeta (\\sigma )^{3}\\zeta (\\sigma +it)^{4}\\zeta (\\sigma +2it)|=\\exp \\sum \_{p^{n}}p^{-n\\sigma }{\\frac {3+4\\cos(t\\log p^{n})+\\cos(2t\\log p^{n})}{n}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f0345c68f615920817013f78999f94892f93801) which is at least 1 because all the terms in the sum are positive, due to the inequality ![{\\displaystyle 3+4\\cos(\\theta )+\\cos(2\\theta )=2(1+\\cos(\\theta ))^{2}\\geq 0.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f6602420aecec7810677ac2f6fd8652ece0a794)

The most extensive computer search by Platt and [Trudgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Trudgian "Timothy Trudgian")[\[22\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlattTrudgian2021-22) for counterexamples of the Riemann hypothesis has verified it for |_t_| ≤ 3.0001753328×1012. Beyond that zero-free regions are known as inequalities concerning _σ_ + _i t_, which can be zeros. The oldest version is from [De la Vallée-Poussin (1899–1900)](#CITEREFde_la_Vallée-Poussin1899–1900), who proved there is a region without zeros that satisfies 1 − _σ_ ≥ ⁠_C_/log(_t_)⁠ for some positive constant _C_. In other words, zeros cannot be too close to the line _σ_ = 1: there is a zero-free region close to this line. This has been enlarged by several authors using methods such as [Vinogradov's mean-value theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinogradov's_mean-value_theorem "Vinogradov's mean-value theorem").

The most recent paper[\[42\]](#cite_note-42) by Mossinghoff, Trudgian and Yang is from December 2022 and provides four zero-free regions that improved the previous results of Kevin Ford from 2002, Mossinghoff and Trudgian themselves from 2015 and Pace Nielsen's slight improvement of Ford from October 2022:

## Zeros on the critical line

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=35 "Edit section: Zeros on the critical line")\]

[Hardy (1914)](#CITEREFHardy1914) and [Hardy & Littlewood (1921)](#CITEREFHardyLittlewood1921) showed there are infinitely many zeros on the critical line, by considering moments of certain functions related to the zeta function. [Selberg (1942)](#CITEREFSelberg1942) proved that at least a (small) positive proportion of zeros lie on the line. [Levinson (1974)](#CITEREFLevinson1974) improved this to one-third of the zeros by relating the zeros of the zeta function to those of its derivative, and [Conrey (1989)](#CITEREFConrey1989) improved this further to two-fifths. In 2020, this estimate was extended to five-twelfths (i.e., 41.6%) by Pratt, Robles, [Zaharescu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandru_Zaharescu "Alexandru Zaharescu") and Zeindler[\[43\]](#cite_note-43) by considering extended mollifiers that can accommodate higher order derivatives of the zeta function and their associated Kloosterman sums. Until this point, almost all improvements over the previous half-century had relied on refinements of Levinson's mollification method.

In August 2026, an unreleased research version of the [Anthropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropic "Anthropic")'s [large language model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model "Large language model") [Claude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_\(AI\) "Claude (AI)") working interactively with human researchers, proved unconditionally that at least two-thirds (66.6%) of the non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function lie on the critical line.[\[44\]](#cite_note-44) Using an optimized test family, this bound was improved to ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {3}{2}}-{\\frac {1}{\\sqrt {2}}}\\cot \\left({\\frac {1}{\\sqrt {2}}}\\right)\\approx 67.25\\%}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fc10a2a892ad87f550d30d7ac8950aaed3e898a9).[\[45\]](#cite_note-Claude2026-45)

The proof achieved this leap by entirely abandoning [Levinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Levinson "Norman Levinson")'s method. Instead, it succeeded in stripping the Riemann hypothesis assumption from [Hugh Montgomery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Montgomery_\(mathematician\) "Hugh Montgomery (mathematician)")'s 1973 pair-correlation method by interpreting Weil's [explicit formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explicit_formulae_\(L-function\) "Explicit formulae (L-function)") as a finite-dimensional [Hermitian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_matrix "Hermitian matrix") over a family of test functions (a Gabor system). The proof bounded the number of off-line zero pairs unconditionally through linear algebra techniques, specifically [Sylvester's law of inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylvester's_law_of_inertia "Sylvester's law of inertia") and a rank-trace inequality derived from [von Neumann's trace inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann's_trace_inequality "Von Neumann's trace inequality"). The logical core of the proof, including the matrix inequalities and trace asymptotics, was formally verified using the [Lean 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lean_\(proof_assistant\) "Lean (proof assistant)") theorem prover.[\[45\]](#cite_note-Claude2026-45)

In September 2026, Youness Lamzouri published a shorter and simpler proof of the same bounds. Lamzouri bypassed the finite-dimensional matrix apparatus entirely, replacing the linear algebra techniques with a single [Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_space "Hilbert space") inequality based on [Bessel's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel's_inequality "Bessel's inequality") and [Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram%E2%80%93Schmidt_process "Gram–Schmidt process").[\[46\]](#cite_note-Lamzouri2026-46)

Most zeros lie close to the critical line. More precisely, [Bohr & Landau (1914)](#CITEREFBohrLandau1914) showed that for any positive _ε_, the number of zeros with real part at least 1/2+_ε_ and imaginary part at between −_T_ and _T_ is ![{\\displaystyle O(T)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ac1c627e228735c52500a419a6b4af4ed02a1574). Combined with the facts that zeros on the critical strip are symmetric about the critical line and that the total number of zeros in the critical strip is ![{\\displaystyle \\Theta (T\\log T)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8917ac6a6c139f9f59a03f99dcfa83499b2c6981), [almost all](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_all "Almost all") non-trivial zeros are within a distance _ε_ of the critical line. [Ivić (1985)](#CITEREFIvić1985) gives several more precise versions of this result, called _zero density estimates_, which bound the number of zeros in regions with imaginary part at most _T_ and real part at least 1/2 + _ε_.

### Hardy–Littlewood conjectures

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=36 "Edit section: Hardy–Littlewood conjectures")\]

In 1914 [Godfrey Harold Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy "G. H. Hardy") proved that ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta \\left({\\tfrac {1}{2}}+it\\right)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/928fbdc88de8d575f6aa4f27efbd48fafc031a7c) has infinitely many real zeros.

The next two conjectures of [Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy "G. H. Hardy") and [John Edensor Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Edensor_Littlewood "John Edensor Littlewood") on the distance between real zeros of ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta \\left({\\tfrac {1}{2}}+it\\right)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/928fbdc88de8d575f6aa4f27efbd48fafc031a7c) and on the density of zeros of ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta \\left({\\tfrac {1}{2}}+it\\right)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/928fbdc88de8d575f6aa4f27efbd48fafc031a7c) on the interval ![{\\displaystyle (T,T+H\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5a7f206ba185f5e71e82482b52823f9cab4a9d99) for sufficiently large ![{\\displaystyle T>0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e39e90b9e1e7d5be3b5eae57729dc63494bbe3fd), and ![{\\displaystyle H=T^{a+\\varepsilon }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7b04fad3c3d01d0dc45e856f05d271dc583aebd0) and with as small as possible value of ![{\\displaystyle a>0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f34a80ea013edb56e340b19550430a8b6dfd7b9), where ![{\\displaystyle \\varepsilon >0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e04ec3670b50384a3ce48aca42e7cc5131a06b12) is an arbitrarily small number, open two new directions in the investigation of the Riemann zeta function:

1.  For any ![{\\displaystyle \\varepsilon >0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e04ec3670b50384a3ce48aca42e7cc5131a06b12) there exists a lower bound ![{\\displaystyle T\_{0}=T\_{0}(\\varepsilon )>0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f6d6537b59504874ae1259697e665ff90c2af4a7) such that for ![{\\displaystyle T\\geq T\_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0ffb4a83f66b6423208845cbd53c34044acecea2) and ![{\\displaystyle H=T^{{\\tfrac {1}{4}}+\\varepsilon }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/428c2185ec1ddf2d9471845ba74f7650b78ea2d3) the interval ![{\\displaystyle (T,T+H\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5a7f206ba185f5e71e82482b52823f9cab4a9d99) contains a zero of odd order of the function ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta {\\bigl (}{\\tfrac {1}{2}}+it{\\bigr )}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b693149f8a701807569e4ad91ae936bebcb30373).

Let ![{\\displaystyle N(T)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/891f08bfa8e782f2db0a06b00aa11668b1614537) be the total number of real zeros, and ![{\\displaystyle N\_{0}(T)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/61065a59d53db2b19b928cb09813c3f744cf2e68) be the total number of zeros of odd order of the function ![{\\displaystyle ~\\zeta \\left({\\tfrac {1}{2}}+it\\right)~}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/674a28fdaf3403f4f61868a028614e45d5c00918) lying on the interval ![{\\displaystyle (0,T\]~}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/32185e31a7ceddedfbd8767e0dd79a6afbb0582c).

2.  For any ![{\\displaystyle \\varepsilon >0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e04ec3670b50384a3ce48aca42e7cc5131a06b12) there exists ![{\\displaystyle T\_{0}=T\_{0}(\\varepsilon )>0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f6d6537b59504874ae1259697e665ff90c2af4a7) and some ![{\\displaystyle c=c(\\varepsilon )>0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/18530fcafc8a7bbfe84b253fdf94d59d04bf3bf4), such that for ![{\\displaystyle T\\geq T\_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0ffb4a83f66b6423208845cbd53c34044acecea2) and ![{\\displaystyle H=T^{{\\tfrac {1}{2}}+\\varepsilon }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f5d5d48a2b68012f86ce5fc1ae503c8d45363b54) the inequality ![{\\displaystyle N\_{0}(T+H)-N\_{0}(T)\\geq cH}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8fd2447ff5cffa6cfa95ced90f59c0de7d096362) is true.

### Selberg's zeta function conjecture

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=37 "Edit section: Selberg's zeta function conjecture")\]

[Atle Selberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atle_Selberg "Atle Selberg") investigated the problem of Hardy–Littlewood _2_ and proved that for any _ε_ > 0 there exists such ![{\\displaystyle T\_{0}=T\_{0}(\\varepsilon )>0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f6d6537b59504874ae1259697e665ff90c2af4a7) and _c_ = _c_(_ε_) > 0, such that for ![{\\displaystyle T\\geq T\_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0ffb4a83f66b6423208845cbd53c34044acecea2) and ![{\\displaystyle H=T^{0.5+\\varepsilon }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/008dfbf7fe78bb0ad6c2f9448b45d14aaeea6fb5) the inequality ![{\\displaystyle N(T+H)-N(T)\\geq cH\\log T}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5dfd594d5f0550aa1c343110af08ffbe978e7b0a) is true.[\[47\]](#cite_note-47) Selberg conjectured that this could be tightened to ![{\\displaystyle H=T^{0.5}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8eedf31cf1202cc12358d0ba9168a014189fdfb3). [Anatoly Karatsuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Karatsuba "Anatoly Karatsuba") proved that for a fixed _ε_ satisfying the condition 0 < _ε_ < 0.001, a sufficiently large _T_ and ![{\\displaystyle H=T^{a+\\varepsilon }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7b04fad3c3d01d0dc45e856f05d271dc583aebd0), ![{\\displaystyle a={\\tfrac {27}{82}}={\\tfrac {1}{3}}-{\\tfrac {1}{246}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87a1544fc16f5e4979060e373bd729c3032f6807), the interval (_T_, _T_+_H_) contains at least _cH_ log(_T_) real zeros of the [Riemann zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_zeta_function "Riemann zeta function") ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta \\left({\\tfrac {1}{2}}+it\\right)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/928fbdc88de8d575f6aa4f27efbd48fafc031a7c) and therefore confirmed the Selberg conjecture.[\[48\]](#cite_note-48) The estimates of Selberg and Karatsuba can not be improved in respect of the order of growth as _T_ → ∞.

[Karatsuba (1992)](#CITEREFKaratsuba1992) proved that an analog of the Selberg conjecture holds for almost all intervals (_T_, _T_+_H_\], ![{\\displaystyle H=T^{\\varepsilon }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/785b85d331f3ca9a48ffdc4dbc3f711e609934c0), where _ε_ is an arbitrarily small fixed positive number. The Karatsuba method permits to investigate zeros of the Riemann zeta function on "supershort" intervals of the critical line, that is, on the intervals (_T_, _T_+_H_\], the length _H_ of which grows slower than any, even arbitrarily small degree _T_. In particular, he proved that for any given numbers _ε_, ![{\\displaystyle \\varepsilon \_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9e900f9bee793f99d10877ef108da074cbca60ce) satisfying the conditions ![{\\displaystyle 0<\\varepsilon ,\\varepsilon \_{1}<1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ee7c1d1c9d4e6f672818604fa6e9d9621e709d50) almost all intervals (_T_, _T_+_H_\] for ![{\\displaystyle H\\geq \\exp {\\{(\\log T)^{\\varepsilon }\\}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d1146eb77c007aa295ffb91c17580a216b2d08ec) contain at least ![{\\displaystyle H(\\log T)^{1-\\varepsilon \_{1}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/37388d2788b58ba179410c8caad6eb764532f2aa) zeros of the function ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta \\left({\\tfrac {1}{2}}+it\\right)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/928fbdc88de8d575f6aa4f27efbd48fafc031a7c). This estimate is quite close to the one that follows from the Riemann hypothesis.

### Numerical calculations

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=38 "Edit section: Numerical calculations")\]

The function ![{\\displaystyle \\pi ^{-{\\frac {s}{2}}}\\Gamma ({\\tfrac {s}{2}})\\zeta (s)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/865be83472ebb0517bcf64a73a9a4411fa8c82e7) has the same zeros as the zeta function in the critical strip, and is real on the critical line because of the functional equation, so one can prove the existence of zeros exactly on the real line between two points by checking numerically that the function has opposite signs at these points. Usually one writes ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta ({\\tfrac {1}{2}}+it)=Z(t)e^{-i\\theta (t)}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ed8c3fcf872d4de15e1b659ca9cd5808332489bb) where Hardy's [Z function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z_function "Z function") and the [Riemann–Siegel theta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann%E2%80%93Siegel_theta_function "Riemann–Siegel theta function") _θ_ are uniquely defined by this and the condition that they are smooth real functions with _θ_(0) \= 0\. By finding many intervals where the function _Z_ changes sign one can show that there are many zeros on the critical line. To verify the Riemann hypothesis up to a given [imaginary part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part "Imaginary part") _T_ of the zeros, one also has to check that there are no further zeros off the line in this region. This can be done by calculating the total number of zeros in the region using [Turing's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing's_method "Turing's method") and checking that it is the same as the number of zeros found on the line. This allows one to verify the Riemann hypothesis computationally up to any desired value of _T_ (provided all the zeros of the zeta function in this region are simple and on the critical line).[\[49\]](#cite_note-49)[\[50\]](#cite_note-50)

These calculations can also be used to estimate ![{\\displaystyle \\pi (x)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5a62d9bf7b2ba03b30a95e7059362cc4b3865123) for finite ranges of ![{\\displaystyle x}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87f9e315fd7e2ba406057a97300593c4802b53e4). For example, using the latest result from 2020 (zeros up to height ![{\\displaystyle 3\\times 10^{12}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/500afd2e07a213b18f778f74e5fde03e637220c7)), it has been shown that ![{\\displaystyle |\\pi (x)-\\operatorname {li} (x)|<{\\frac {1}{8\\pi }}{\\sqrt {x}}\\log(x),\\qquad {\\text{for }}2657\\leq x\\leq 1.101\\times 10^{26}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f8e192f9bfb85d71dc56c27af527dad8919600d2) In general, this inequality holds if ![{\\displaystyle x\\geq 2657}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a8381a07cb24b939b87e5f094a214ef586ad5ac8) and ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {9.06}{\\log {\\log {x}}}}{\\sqrt {\\frac {x}{\\log {x}}}}\\leq T,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2a421361ab561ee065bb9858f725a91d424120ca) where ![{\\displaystyle T}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ec7200acd984a1d3a3d7dc455e262fbe54f7f6e0) is the largest known value such that the Riemann hypothesis is true for all zeros ![{\\displaystyle \\rho }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f7d439671d1289b6a816e6af7a304be40608d64) with ![{\\displaystyle \\Im {\\left(\\rho \\right)}\\in \\left(0,T\\right\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/386ca464716d11155c40348b352a968faf05bbc8).[\[51\]](#cite_note-51)

A [Gram point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram_point "Gram point") is a point on the critical line 1/2 + _it_ where the zeta function is real and non-zero. Using the expression for the zeta function on the critical line, _ζ_(1/2 + _it_) = _Z_(_t_)_e_−_iθ_(_t_), where Hardy's function, [_Z_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z_function "Z function"), is real for real _t_, and _θ_ is the [Riemann–Siegel theta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann%E2%80%93Siegel_theta_function "Riemann–Siegel theta function"), we see that zeta is real when sin(_θ_(_t_)) = 0. This implies that _θ_(_t_) is an integer multiple of π, which allows for the location of Gram points to be calculated fairly easily by inverting the formula for _θ_. They are usually numbered as _gn_ for _n_ = 0, 1, ..., where _gn_ is the unique solution of _θ_(_t_) = _n_π.

Gram observed that there was often exactly one zero of the zeta function between any two consecutive Gram points; Hutchinson called this observation **[Gram's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram's_law "Gram's law")**. There are several other closely related statements that are also sometimes called Gram's law: for example, (−1)_n__Z_(_gn_) is usually positive, or _Z_(_t_) usually has opposite sign at consecutive Gram points. A Gram point _t_ is called good if the zeta function is positive at 1/2 + _it_. The indices of the "bad" Gram points where _Z_ has the "wrong" sign are 126, 134, 195, 211, ... (sequence [A114856](https://oeis.org/A114856 "oeis:A114856") in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences "On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences")). A _Gram block_ is an interval bounded by two good Gram points such that all the Gram points between them are bad. A refinement of Gram's law called Rosser's rule due to [Rosser, Yohe & Schoenfeld (1969)](#CITEREFRosserYoheSchoenfeld1969) says that Gram blocks often have the expected number of zeros in them (the same as the number of Gram intervals), even though some of the individual Gram intervals in the block may not have exactly one zero in them. For example, the interval bounded by _g_125 and _g_127 is a Gram block containing a unique bad Gram point _g_126, and contains the expected number 2 of zeros although neither of its two Gram intervals contains a unique zero. Rosser et al. checked that there were no exceptions to Rosser's rule in the first 3 million zeros, although there are infinitely many exceptions to Rosser's rule over the entire zeta function.

Gram's rule and Rosser's rule both say that in some sense zeros do not stray too far from their expected positions. The distance of a zero from its expected position is controlled by the function _S_ defined above, which grows extremely slowly: its average value is of the order of (log log _T_)1/2, which only reaches 2 for T around 1024. This means that both rules hold most of the time for small _T_ but eventually break down often. Indeed, [Trudgian (2011)](#CITEREFTrudgian2011) showed that both Gram's law and Rosser's rule fail in a positive proportion of cases. To be specific, it is expected that in about 66% one zero is enclosed by two successive Gram points, but in 17% no zero and in 17% two zeros are in such a Gram-interval on the long run [Hanga (2020)](#CITEREFHanga2020).

### Random matrix theory and quantum chaos

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=40 "Edit section: Random matrix theory and quantum chaos")\]

Assuming the Riemann hypothesis one can ask what further regularities might govern the distribution of the zeros of the zeta function on the critical line. One conjectural picture is that the critical zeros of the zeta function behave statistically like the eigenvalues of large random [Hermitian matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_matrix "Hermitian matrix"). The idea began with [Hugh Montgomery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Montgomery_\(mathematician\) "Hugh Montgomery (mathematician)")'s work on the [pair correlation conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pair_correlation_conjecture "Pair correlation conjecture") for the zeros of the zeta function.[\[53\]](#cite_note-53) After a suitable rescaling to account for the increasing density of zeros with height, the conjectured pair correlation function agrees with that of [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue "Eigenvalue") in the [Gaussian unitary ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_unitary_ensemble "Gaussian unitary ensemble") (GUE) in random matrix theory.[\[54\]](#cite_note-54)[\[55\]](#cite_note-55)

The connection was tested numerically by [Andrew Odlyzko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Odlyzko "Andrew Odlyzko"), who found that the spacing statistics of zeros high on the critical line agree closely with the predictions of GUE random matrix theory.[\[56\]](#cite_note-56)[\[57\]](#cite_note-57) The agreement extends beyond nearest-neighbor spacings to higher correlation functions, and is widely regarded as strong evidence that the zeros are modeled by the same local statistics as random matrices.[\[58\]](#cite_note-58)[\[59\]](#cite_note-59)

The random matrix analogy is also related to the Hilbert–Pólya conjecture, and to ideas from [quantum chaos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chaos "Quantum chaos"). In quantum chaotic systems, eigenvalues often obey random matrix statistics, so the appearance of the same statistics in the zeros of the zeta function can be interpreted as evidence that they may arise from a selfadjoint operator or from a chaotic dynamical system.[\[60\]](#cite_note-60)[\[61\]](#cite_note-61) This gives a heuristic picture of why the zeros might lie on a spectral line and why their spacings exhibit strong repulsion rather than random clustering.[\[62\]](#cite_note-62)

This viewpoint was adopted by [Nicholas Katz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Katz "Nicholas Katz") and [Peter Sarnak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Sarnak "Peter Sarnak"), who proposed that families of [L-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-function "L-function") have symmetry types governed by the compact [classical groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_group "Classical group") ([unitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_group "Unitary group"), [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_group "Orthogonal group"), or [symplectic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_group "Symplectic group")), and that the distributions of their low-lying zeros should match the corresponding random-matrix ensembles.[\[63\]](#cite_note-63)[\[64\]](#cite_note-64)[\[65\]](#cite_note-65) For the Riemann zeta function, the relevant ensemble is that of the unitary group. Random matrix theory has also led to conjectures about the growth of moments of the zeta function on the critical line. In particular, [Jonathan Keating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Keating "Jonathan Keating") and [Nina Snaith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nina_Snaith "Nina Snaith") used averages over random unitary matrices to predict the main constants in asymptotic moment formulas such as ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {1}{T}}\\int \_{0}^{T}|\\zeta (1/2+it)|^{2k}\\,dt}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/48af36ed78cfdc4fa64461253384aaa3af8372a0) as ![{\\displaystyle T\\to \\infty }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6d124f9781b2ebb80044d6fb64b3a5f7e1cafdd4). Their conjectures separate a universal random-matrix factor from an arithmetic [Euler product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_product "Euler product") factor, and have influenced later work on moments and ratios of L-functions.[\[66\]](#cite_note-66)[\[67\]](#cite_note-67)[\[68\]](#cite_note-68)

Random matrix theory and quantum chaos are thus heuristic framework surrounding the Riemann hypothesis, even though no proof of the hypothesis is known from this approach.[\[69\]](#cite_note-69)

## Arguments for and against the Riemann hypothesis

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Riemann_hypothesis&action=edit&section=41 "Edit section: Arguments for and against the Riemann hypothesis")\]

Mathematical papers about the Riemann hypothesis tend to be cautiously noncommittal about its truth. Of authors who express an opinion, most of them, such as [Riemann (1859)](#CITEREFRiemann1859) and [Bombieri (2000)](#CITEREFBombieri2000), imply that they expect (or at least hope) that it is true. The few authors who express serious doubt about it include [Ivić (2008)](#CITEREFIvić2008), who lists some reasons for skepticism, and [Littlewood (1962)](#CITEREFLittlewood1962), who flatly states that he believes it false, that there is no evidence for it and no imaginable reason it would be true. The consensus of the survey articles ([Bombieri 2000](#CITEREFBombieri2000), [Conrey 2003](#CITEREFConrey2003), and [Sarnak 2005](#CITEREFSarnak2005)) is that the evidence for it is strong but not overwhelming, so that while it is probably true there is reasonable doubt.

Some of the arguments for and against the Riemann hypothesis are listed by [Conrey (2003)](#CITEREFConrey2003), [Sarnak (2005)](#CITEREFSarnak2005), and [Ivić (2008)](#CITEREFIvić2008), and include the following:

*   Several analogues of the Riemann hypothesis have already been proved. The proof of the Riemann hypothesis for varieties over finite fields by [Deligne (1974)](#CITEREFDeligne1974) is possibly the single strongest theoretical reason in favor of the Riemann hypothesis. This provides some evidence for the more general conjecture that all zeta functions associated with [automorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_form "Automorphic form") forms satisfy a Riemann hypothesis, which includes the classical Riemann hypothesis as a special case. Similarly [Selberg zeta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selberg_zeta_function "Selberg zeta function") satisfy the analogue of the Riemann hypothesis, and are in some ways similar to the Riemann zeta function, having a functional equation and an infinite product expansion analogous to the Euler product expansion. But there are also some major differences; for example, they are not given by Dirichlet series. The Riemann hypothesis for the [Goss zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goss_zeta_function "Goss zeta function") was proved by [Sheats (1998)](#CITEREFSheats1998). In contrast to these positive examples, some [Epstein zeta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epstein_zeta_function "Epstein zeta function") do not satisfy the Riemann hypothesis even though they have an infinite number of zeros on the critical line.[\[17\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTETitchmarsh1986-17) These functions are quite similar to the Riemann zeta function, and have a Dirichlet series expansion and a [functional equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_equation_\(L-function\) "Functional equation (L-function)"), but the ones known to fail the Riemann hypothesis do not have an [Euler product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_product "Euler product") and are not directly related to [automorphic representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_representation "Automorphic representation").
*   At first, the numerical verification that many zeros lie on the line seems strong evidence for it. But analytic number theory has had many conjectures supported by substantial numerical evidence that turned out to be false. See [Skewes number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skewes_number "Skewes number") for a notorious example, where the first exception to a plausible conjecture related to the Riemann hypothesis probably occurs around 10316; a counterexample to the Riemann hypothesis with imaginary part this size would be far beyond anything that can currently be computed using a direct approach. The problem is that the behavior is often influenced by very slowly increasing functions such as log log _T_, that tend to infinity, but do so so slowly that this cannot be detected by computation. Such functions occur in the theory of the zeta function controlling the behavior of its zeros; for example the function _S_(_T_) above has average size around (log log _T_)1/2. As _S_(_T_) jumps by at least 2 at any counterexample to the Riemann hypothesis, one might expect any counterexamples to the Riemann hypothesis to start appearing only when _S_(_T_) becomes large. It is never much more than 3 as far as it has been calculated, but is known to be unbounded, suggesting that calculations may not have yet reached the region of typical behavior of the zeta function.
*   [Denjoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnaud_Denjoy "Arnaud Denjoy")'s probabilistic argument for the Riemann hypothesis[\[70\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEdwards1974-70) is based on the observation that if _μ_(_x_) is a random sequence of "1"s and "−1"s then, for every _ε_ > 0, the [partial sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum "Partial sum") ![{\\displaystyle M(x)=\\sum \_{n\\leq x}\\mu (n)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/056958331c498fd0fdb9b4b76c1101a97f352d0b) (the values of which are positions in a [simple random walk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_random_walk "Simple random walk")) satisfy the bound ![{\\displaystyle M(x)=O(x^{1/2+\\varepsilon })}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2c75c3dd2aa902d62979d1d0546b7d811daca627) with [probability 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_surely "Almost surely"). The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to this bound for the [Möbius function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6bius_function "Möbius function") μ and the [Mertens function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mertens_function "Mertens function") _M_ derived in the same way from it. In other words, the Riemann hypothesis is in some sense equivalent to saying that _μ_(_x_) behaves like a random sequence of coin tosses. When _μ_(_x_) is nonzero its sign gives the [parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_\(mathematics\) "Parity (mathematics)") of the number of prime factors of _x_, so informally the Riemann hypothesis says that the parity of the number of prime factors of an integer behaves randomly. Such probabilistic arguments in number theory often give the right answer, but tend to be very hard to make rigorous, and occasionally give the wrong answer for some results, such as [Maier's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maier's_theorem "Maier's theorem").
*   The calculations in [Odlyzko (1987)](#CITEREFOdlyzko1987) show that the zeros of the zeta function behave very much like the eigenvalues of a random [Hermitian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_matrix "Hermitian matrix"), suggesting that they are the eigenvalues of some self-adjoint operator, which would imply the Riemann hypothesis. All attempts to find such an operator have failed.
*   There are several theorems, such as [Goldbach's weak conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldbach's_weak_conjecture "Goldbach's weak conjecture") for sufficiently large odd numbers, that were first proved using the generalized Riemann hypothesis, and later shown to be true unconditionally. This could be considered as weak evidence for the generalized Riemann hypothesis, as several of its "predictions" are true.
*   [Lehmer's phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lehmer_pair "Lehmer pair"),[\[71\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTELehmer1956-71) where two zeros are sometimes very close, is sometimes given as a reason to disbelieve the Riemann hypothesis. But one would expect this to happen occasionally by chance even if the Riemann hypothesis is true, and Odlyzko's calculations suggest that nearby pairs of zeros occur just as often as predicted by [Montgomery's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery's_pair_correlation_conjecture "Montgomery's pair correlation conjecture").
*   [Patterson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_James_Patterson "Samuel James Patterson") suggests that the most compelling reason for the Riemann hypothesis for most mathematicians is the hope that primes are distributed as regularly as possible.[\[72\]](#cite_note-72)
