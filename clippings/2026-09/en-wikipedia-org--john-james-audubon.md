**John James Audubon**[\[a\]](#cite_note-2) (born **Jean-Jacques Rabin**, April 26, 1785 – January 27, 1851) was a French-American [artist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artist "Artist"), [entrepreneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entrepreneur "Entrepreneur"), [naturalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalist "Naturalist"), [explorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explorer "Explorer"), and [ornithologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornithologist "Ornithologist"). His combined interests in painting and ornithology turned into a plan to make a complete pictorial record of all the bird species of North America.[\[2\]](#cite_note-3)[\[3\]](#cite_note-Fries-2006-4)[\[4\]](#cite_note-5) He was notable for his extensive studies of American birds and for his detailed (yet romantic) illustrations, which were engraved in Scotland and England for a large-format (double-elephant folio) color-plate ([intaglio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intaglio_\(printmaking\) "Intaglio (printmaking)")) book titled _[The Birds of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Birds_of_America "The Birds of America")_ (1827–1838), and five volumes of accompanying text entitled _Ornithological Biography_ (1831–1839).

Audubon's scientific contributions were considerable but controversial. He was accused of fraud, plagiarism, and scientific misconduct during his life as well as posthumously.[\[5\]](#cite_note-Ord-1840-6)[\[6\]](#cite_note-7)[\[7\]](#cite_note-8) As of 2025, the IOC World Bird List (v.15.1) attributes him as the primary author of 23 bird species (14 of which were based on specimens he purchased or received from colleagues) and 13 subspecies.[\[8\]](#cite_note-9) This tally is an overestimate because it includes at least one ambiguous species (Traill's flycatcher, _Muscicapa traillii_) that was recently stabilized (as [willow flycatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willow_flycatcher "Willow flycatcher")) with a [neotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_\(biology\) "Type (biology)"),[\[9\]](#cite_note-10) and one subspecies (northern [bald eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bald_eagle "Bald eagle"), _Haliaeetus leucocephalus washingtoniensis_) that was based on a plagiarized image and fabricated data.[\[10\]](#cite_note-Halley-2020-11) Another of Audubon's new subspecies (northern [Harris's hawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harris's_hawk "Harris's hawk"), _Parabuteo unicinctus harrisi_) was based on a stolen specimen.[\[11\]](#cite_note-12)

Audubon has also been widely criticized for buying and selling slaves, [bodysnatching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodysnatching "Bodysnatching"), and trafficking Native American remains.[\[12\]](#cite_note-Fabian2011-13)[\[13\]](#cite_note-Uteuova-14) As of 2025, more than two dozen regional Audubon societies across the United States have changed their names. However, the [National Audubon Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Audubon_Society "National Audubon Society") remains committed to their namesake,[\[14\]](#cite_note-15) and many towns, neighborhoods, and streets across the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") still bear his name (e.g., [Audubon, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audubon,_Pennsylvania "Audubon, Pennsylvania")).

Audubon was born in [Les Cayes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Cayes "Les Cayes") in the French colony of [Saint-Domingue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Domingue "Saint-Domingue") (now [Haiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haiti "Haiti"))[\[15\]](#cite_note-16) on his father's [sugarcane plantation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_plantations_in_the_Caribbean "Sugar plantations in the Caribbean"). He was the son of Lieutenant Jean Audubon, a French naval officer (and [privateer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privateer "Privateer")) from the south of [Brittany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Brittany "Duchy of Brittany"),[\[16\]](#cite_note-rhodes-17) and his mistress, Jeanne Rabine,[\[17\]](#cite_note-18) a 27-year-old chambermaid from [Les Touches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Touches "Les Touches"), Brittany (now in the modern region [Pays de la Loire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pays_de_la_Loire "Pays de la Loire")).[\[16\]](#cite_note-rhodes-17)[\[18\]](#cite_note-Souder_2005,_p._18-19) They named him Jean Rabin.[\[18\]](#cite_note-Souder_2005,_p._18-19) Another 1887 biographer has stated that his mother was a lady from a Louisiana plantation.[\[19\]](#cite_note-20) His mother died when he was a few months old, as she had suffered from tropical disease since arriving on the island. His father already had an unknown number of [mixed-race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed-race "Mixed-race") children (among them a daughter named Marie-Madeleine),[\[20\]](#cite_note-DeLatt,_Carolyne_E._2008_page_21-21) some by his mixed-race housekeeper, Catherine "Sanitte" Bouffard[\[20\]](#cite_note-DeLatt,_Carolyne_E._2008_page_21-21) (described as a [quadroon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadroon "Quadroon"), meaning she was three-quarters European in ancestry).[\[21\]](#cite_note-22) Following Jeanne Rabine's death, Audubon renewed his relationship with Sanitte Bouffard and had a daughter by her, named Muguet. Bouffard also took care of the infant boy Jean.[\[22\]](#cite_note-23)

The senior Audubon had served in the [American Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War "American Revolutionary War"), during which he was captured by the [Royal Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Navy "Royal Navy") and imprisoned. After his release, he actively supported the Patriot cause.[\[23\]](#cite_note-24) He had long worked to save money and secure his family's future with real estate. Due to repeated uprisings of slaves in the Caribbean, he sold part of his plantation in Saint-Domingue in 1789 and purchased a 284-acre farm called [Mill Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_Grove "Mill Grove"), 20 miles from [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia"), to diversify his investments. Increasing tension in Saint-Domingue between the colonists and slaves, who greatly outnumbered them, convinced the senior Audubon to return to France, where he became a member of the [Republican Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Republican_Guard "French Republican Guard"). In 1788 he arranged for Jean and in 1791 for Muguet to be transported to France.[\[24\]](#cite_note-25)[\[25\]](#cite_note-Souder_2005,_p._20-26)[\[26\]](#cite_note-27)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/57/La_Gerbeti%C3%A8re_%C3%A0_Cou%C3%ABron_2.JPG/250px-La_Gerbeti%C3%A8re_%C3%A0_Cou%C3%ABron_2.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:La_Gerbeti%C3%A8re_%C3%A0_Cou%C3%ABron_2.JPG)

[La Gerbetière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Gerbeti%C3%A8re "La Gerbetière"), mansion owned by Audubon's father in [Couëron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cou%C3%ABron "Couëron"), where young Audubon was raised

The children were raised in [Couëron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cou%C3%ABron "Couëron"), near [Nantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nantes "Nantes"), France, by Audubon and his French wife, Anne Moynet Audubon, whom he had married years before his time in Saint-Domingue. In 1794 they formally adopted both the children to regularize their legal status in France.[\[25\]](#cite_note-Souder_2005,_p._20-26) They renamed the boy Jean-Jacques Fougère Audubon and the girl Rose.[\[27\]](#cite_note-28)

From his earliest days, the younger Audubon had an affinity for birds. "I felt an intimacy with them...bordering on frenzy \[that\] must accompany my steps through life."[\[28\]](#cite_note-29) His father encouraged his interest in nature:

> He would point out the elegant movement of the birds, and the beauty and softness of their plumage. He called my attention to their show of pleasure or sense of danger, their perfect forms and splendid attire. He would speak of their departure and return with the seasons.[\[29\]](#cite_note-30)

In France during the years of the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution "French Revolution") and its aftermath, Audubon grew up to be a handsome and gregarious man. He played flute and violin, and learned to ride, [fence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fencing "Fencing"), and dance.[\[30\]](#cite_note-Rhodes_2004,_p._5-31) Audubon enjoyed roaming in the woods, often returning with natural curiosities, including birds' eggs and nests, of which he made crude drawings.[\[31\]](#cite_note-32) His father planned to make a seaman of his son. At twelve, Audubon went to military school and became a [cabin boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabin_boy "Cabin boy"). He quickly found out that he was susceptible to [seasickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seasickness "Seasickness") and not fond of mathematics or navigation. After failing the officer's qualification test, Audubon ended his incipient naval career. He returned to exploring fields again, focusing on birds.[\[32\]](#cite_note-33)

## Immigration to the United States

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=John_James_Audubon&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Immigration to the United States")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2f/41_Ruffed_Grouse.jpg/250px-41_Ruffed_Grouse.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:41_Ruffed_Grouse.jpg)

Plate 41 of _[The Birds of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Birds_of_America "The Birds of America")_ by Audubon, depicting [ruffed grouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruffed_grouse "Ruffed grouse")

In 1803, his father obtained a false passport so that Jean-Jacques could go to the United States to avoid conscription in the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars "Napoleonic Wars"). 18-year-old Jean-Jacques boarded ship, anglicizing his name to John James Audubon.[\[33\]](#cite_note-34) Jean Audubon and Claude Rozier arranged a business partnership for their sons John James Audubon and [Jean Ferdinand Rozier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Ferdinand_Rozier "Jean Ferdinand Rozier") to pursue lead mining in Pennsylvania at Audubon's Pennsylvania property of Mill Grove. The Audubon-Rozier partnership was based on Rozier's buying half of Jean Audubon's share of a plantation in Haiti, and lending money to the partnership as secured by half interest in the lead mining.[\[34\]](#cite_note-35)[\[35\]](#cite_note-Rhodes_2004-36)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/08/Long_Haired_Squirrel_by_John_James_Audubon.jpg/250px-Long_Haired_Squirrel_by_John_James_Audubon.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Long_Haired_Squirrel_by_John_James_Audubon.jpg)

John James Audubon, _Long Haired Squirrel_, c. 1841.

Audubon caught [yellow fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever "Yellow fever") upon arrival in New York City. The ship's captain placed him in a boarding house run by [Quaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaker "Quaker") women who nursed Audubon to recovery and taught him English. He traveled with the family's Quaker lawyer to the Audubon family farm at Mill Grove.[\[36\]](#cite_note-37) The 284-acre (115 ha) homestead is located on the [Perkiomen Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perkiomen_Creek "Perkiomen Creek") a few miles from [Valley Forge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley_Forge "Valley Forge").

Audubon lived with the tenants in the two-story stone house, in an area that he considered a paradise. "Hunting, fishing, drawing, and music occupied my every moment; cares I knew not, and cared naught about them."[\[30\]](#cite_note-Rhodes_2004,_p._5-31) Studying his surroundings, Audubon quickly learned the ornithologist's rule, which he wrote down as, "The nature of the place—whether high or low, moist or dry, whether sloping north or south, or bearing tall trees or low shrubs—generally gives hint as to its inhabitants."[\[37\]](#cite_note-38)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/69/1_Wild_Turkey.jpg/250px-1_Wild_Turkey.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:1_Wild_Turkey.jpg)

Plate 1 of _The Birds of America_ by Audubon depicting a [wild turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_turkey "Wild turkey")

His father hoped that the lead mines on the property could be commercially developed, as lead was an essential component of bullets. This could provide his son with a profitable occupation.[\[38\]](#cite_note-39) At Mill Grove, Audubon met the owner of the nearby [Fatland Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatland_Ford "Fatland Ford") estate, William Bakewell, and his daughter [Lucy Bakewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Bakewell_Audubon "Lucy Bakewell Audubon").

Audubon set about to study American birds, determined to illustrate his findings in a more realistic manner than most artists did then.[\[39\]](#cite_note-40) He began drawing and painting birds, and recording their behavior. After an accidental fall into a creek, Audubon contracted a severe fever. He was nursed and recovered at Fatland Ford, with Lucy at his side.

Risking conscription in France, Audubon returned in 1805 to see his father and ask permission to marry. He also needed to discuss family business plans. While there, he met the naturalist and physician Charles-Marie D'Orbigny, who improved Audubon's [taxidermy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxidermy "Taxidermy") skills and taught him scientific methods of research.[\[40\]](#cite_note-41) Although his return ship was overtaken by an English [privateer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privateer "Privateer"), Audubon and his hidden gold coins survived the encounter.[\[41\]](#cite_note-42)

Audubon resumed his bird studies and created his own nature museum, perhaps inspired by the great museum of natural history created by [Charles Willson Peale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Willson_Peale "Charles Willson Peale") in Philadelphia. Peale's bird exhibits were considered scientifically advanced. Audubon's room was brimming with birds' eggs, stuffed raccoons and opossums, fish, snakes, and other creatures. He had become proficient at specimen preparation and taxidermy.

Deeming the mining venture too risky, with his father's approval Audubon sold part of the Mill Grove farm, including the house and mine, and retaining some land for investment.[\[42\]](#cite_note-43)

## Banding experiment with eastern phoebes

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=John_James_Audubon&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Banding experiment with eastern phoebes")\]

In volume 2 of _Ornithological Biography_ (1834), Audubon told a story from his childhood, 30 years after the events reportedly took place, that has since garnered him the label of "first bird bander in America".[\[43\]](#cite_note-44) The story has since been exposed as likely apocryphal.[\[44\]](#cite_note-Halley-2018a-45) In the spring of 1804, according to the story, Audubon discovered a nest of the "Pewee Flycatcher", now known as the [eastern phoebe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_phoebe "Eastern phoebe") (_Sayornis phoebe_), in a small grotto on the property of Mill Grove. To determine whether the other phoebes on the property were "descended from the same stock", Audubon (1834:126) said that he tied [silver threads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_thread "Silver thread") to the legs of five nestlings:

> I took the whole family out, and blew off the [exuviae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exuvia "Exuvia") of the feathers from the nest. I attached light threads to their legs: these they invariably removed, either with their bills, or with the assistance of their parents. I renewed them, however, until I found the little fellows habituated to them; and at last, when they were about to leave the nest, I fixed a light silver thread to the leg of each, loose enough not to hurt the part, but so fastened that no exertions of theirs could remove it.[\[45\]](#cite_note-46)

He also said that he had "ample proof afterwards that the brood of young Pewees, raised in the cave, returned the following spring, and established themselves farther up on the creek, and among the outhouses in the neighbourhood … having caught several of these birds on the nest, \[he\] had the pleasure of finding that two of them had the little ring on the leg." However, multiple independent primary sources (including original, dated drawings of European species[\[46\]](#cite_note-47)) demonstrate that Audubon was in France during the spring of 1805, not in Pennsylvania as he later claimed.[\[44\]](#cite_note-Halley-2018a-45) Furthermore, Audubon's claim to have re-sighted 2 out of 5 of the banded phoebes as adults (i.e., a 40% rate of natal [philopatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philopatry "Philopatry")) has not been replicated by modern studies with much larger sample sizes (e.g., 1.6% rate among 549 nestlings banded; and 1.3% rate among 217 nestlings banded).[\[47\]](#cite_note-48) These facts cast doubt on the truth of Audubon's story.[\[44\]](#cite_note-Halley-2018a-45)

## Marriage and family

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=John_James_Audubon&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Marriage and family")\]

Lucy Bakewell Audubon

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/50/Zenaida_macrouraAWP17AA.jpg/250px-Zenaida_macrouraAWP17AA.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Zenaida_macrouraAWP17AA.jpg)

Plate from _The Birds of America_ by Audubon of a Carolina pigeon (now called [mourning dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mourning_dove "Mourning dove"))

In 1808, Audubon moved to Kentucky, which was rapidly being settled. Six months later, he married Lucy Bakewell at her family estate, [Fatland Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatland_Ford "Fatland Ford"), Pennsylvania, and took her the next day to Kentucky. The two shared many common interests, and began to explore the natural world around them. Though their finances were tenuous, the Audubons started a family. They had two sons, Victor Gifford (1809–1860) and [John Woodhouse Audubon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Woodhouse_Audubon "John Woodhouse Audubon") (1812–1862), and two daughters who died while still young, Lucy at two years (1815–1817) and Rose at nine months (1819–1820).[\[48\]](#cite_note-49) Both sons eventually helped publish their father's works. John W. Audubon became a naturalist, writer, and painter in his own right.[\[49\]](#cite_note-50)

## Starting out in business

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=John_James_Audubon&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Starting out in business")\]

Audubon and [Jean Ferdinand Rozier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Ferdinand_Rozier "Jean Ferdinand Rozier") moved their merchant business partnership west at various stages, ending ultimately in [Ste. Genevieve, Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ste._Genevieve,_Missouri "Ste. Genevieve, Missouri"), a former French colonial settlement west of the [Mississippi River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_River "Mississippi River") and south of [St. Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Louis "St. Louis"). Shipping goods ahead, Audubon and Rozier started a [general store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_store "General store") in [Louisville, Kentucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisville,_Kentucky "Louisville, Kentucky") on the [Ohio River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_River "Ohio River");\[_[when?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")_\] the city had an increasingly important slave market and was the most important port between [Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh "Pittsburgh") and [New Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans "New Orleans"). Soon he was drawing bird specimens again. He regularly burned his earlier efforts to force continuous improvement.[\[50\]](#cite_note-51) He also took detailed field notes to document his drawings.

Due to rising American tensions with the British, President [Thomas Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson "Thomas Jefferson") ordered an embargo on US trade with Britain in 1808, hurting Audubon's trading business.[\[51\]](#cite_note-Streshinsky_1993,_p._64-52) In 1810, Audubon moved his business further west to the less competitive [Henderson, Kentucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henderson,_Kentucky "Henderson, Kentucky"), area. He and his small family took over an abandoned log cabin. In the fields and forests, Audubon wore typical frontier clothes and moccasins, having "a ball pouch, a buffalo horn filled with gunpowder, a butcher knife, and a tomahawk on his belt".[\[51\]](#cite_note-Streshinsky_1993,_p._64-52)

He frequently turned to hunting and fishing to feed his family, as business was slow. On a prospecting trip down the Ohio River with a load of goods, Audubon joined up with [Shawnee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shawnee "Shawnee") and [Osage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osage_Nation "Osage Nation") hunting parties, learning their methods, drawing specimens by the bonfire, and finally parting "like brethren".[\[52\]](#cite_note-53) Audubon had great respect for [Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas "Indigenous peoples of the Americas"): "Whenever I meet Indians, I feel the greatness of our Creator in all its splendor, for there I see the man naked from His hand and yet free from acquired sorrow."[\[53\]](#cite_note-54) Audubon also admired the skill of Kentucky riflemen and the "regulators", citizen lawmen who created a kind of justice on the Kentucky frontier. In his travel notes, he claims to have encountered [Daniel Boone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Boone "Daniel Boone").[\[54\]](#cite_note-55) The Audubon family owned several slaves while he was in Henderson, until they needed money at which point they were sold. Audubon was condemned contemporaneously by [abolitionists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionists "Abolitionists"). Audubon was dismissive of abolitionists in both the US and the United Kingdom.[\[55\]](#cite_note-56)

Audubon and Rozier mutually agreed to end their partnership at Ste. Genevieve on April 6, 1811. Audubon had decided to work at ornithology and art and wanted to return to Lucy and their son in Kentucky. Rozier agreed to pay Audubon US$3,000 (equivalent to $58,050 in 2025), with $1,000 in cash and the balance to be paid over time.[\[56\]](#cite_note-57)[\[57\]](#cite_note-58)[\[58\]](#cite_note-Rozier1890-59)

The terms of the dissolution of the partnership include those by Audubon:

> I John Audubon, having this day mutual consent with Ferdinand Rozier, dissolved and forever closed the partnership and firm of Audubon and Rozier, and having Received from said Ferdinand Rozier payment and notes to the full amount of my part of the goods and debts of the late firm of Audubon and Rozier, I the said John Audubon one of the firm aforesaid do hereby release and forever quit claim to all and any interest which I have or may have in the stock on hand and debts due to the late firm of Audubon and Rozier assign, transfer and set over to said Ferdinand Rozier, all my rights, titles, claims and interest in the goods, merchandise and debts due to the late firm of Audubon and Rozier, and do hereby authorize and empower him for my part, to collect the same in any manner what ever either privately or by suit or suits in law or equity hereby declaring him sole and absolute proprietor and rightful owner of all goods, merchandise and debts of this firm aforesaid, as completely as they were the goods and property of the late firm Audubon and Rozier.
> 
> In witness thereof I have set my hand and seal this Sixth day of April 1811
> 
> John Audubon
> 
> Ed D. DeVillamonte

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/63/John_James_Audubon%3B_his_house._Colour_line_block_by_Richards_Wellcome_V0018684.jpg/250px-John_James_Audubon%3B_his_house._Colour_line_block_by_Richards_Wellcome_V0018684.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:John_James_Audubon;_his_house._Colour_line_block_by_Richards_Wellcome_V0018684.jpg)

John James Audubon house, Henderson, Kentucky.

Audubon was working in Missouri and out riding when the [1811 New Madrid earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1811%E2%80%931812_New_Madrid_earthquakes "1811–1812 New Madrid earthquakes") struck on December 16, 1811, followed by a moment magnitude 7.4 aftershock on the same day. Two additional earthquakes of similar magnitude followed in January and February 1812. When Audubon reached his house, he was relieved to find no major damage, but the area was shaken by [aftershocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aftershocks "Aftershocks") for months.[\[59\]](#cite_note-60) The quake is estimated to have ranked from 8.4 to 8.8 on today's [moment magnitude scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_magnitude_scale "Moment magnitude scale") of severity, stronger than the [San Francisco earthquake of 1906](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_earthquake_of_1906 "San Francisco earthquake of 1906") which is estimated at 7.9. Audubon writes that while on horseback, he first believed the distant rumbling to be the sound of a [tornado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado "Tornado"),

> but the animal knew better than I what was forthcoming, and instead of going faster, so nearly stopped that I remarked he placed one foot after another on the ground with as much precaution as if walking on a smooth piece of ice. I thought he had suddenly foundered, and, speaking to him, was on point of dismounting and leading him, when he all of a sudden fell a-groaning piteously, hung his head, spread out his forelegs, as if to save himself from falling, and stood stock still, continuing to groan. I thought my horse was about to die, and would have sprung from his back had a minute more elapsed; but as that instant all the shrubs and trees began to move from their very roots, the ground rose and fell in successive furrows, like the ruffled water of a lake, and I became bewildered in my ideas, as I too plainly discovered, that all this awful commotion was the result of an earthquake. I had never witnessed anything of the kind before, although like every person, I knew earthquakes by description. But what is description compared to reality! Who can tell the sensations which I experienced when I found myself rocking, as it were, upon my horse, and with him moving to and fro like a child in a cradle, with the most imminent danger around me.[\[60\]](#cite_note-postbook-61)

He noted that as the earthquake retreated, "the air was filled with an extremely disagreeable sulphurous odor."[\[61\]](#cite_note-62)

## Citizenship and debt

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=John_James_Audubon&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Citizenship and debt")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fd/Cinnamon_bear_by_J_T_Bowen_after_John_James_Audubon.jpg/250px-Cinnamon_bear_by_J_T_Bowen_after_John_James_Audubon.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cinnamon_bear_by_J_T_Bowen_after_John_James_Audubon.jpg)

A cinnamon bear by J.T. Bowen after Audubon

During a visit to Philadelphia in 1812 following Congress's' declaration of war against Britain, Audubon became an American citizen and had to give up his French citizenship.[\[62\]](#cite_note-63) After his return to Kentucky, he found that rats had eaten his entire collection of more than 200 drawings. After weeks of [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_\(mood\) "Depression (mood)"), he took to the field again, determined to re-do his drawings to an even higher standard.[\[63\]](#cite_note-64)

The [War of 1812](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_1812 "War of 1812") upset Audubon's plans to move his business to [New Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans "New Orleans"). He formed a partnership with Lucy's brother and built up their trade in Henderson. Between 1812 and the [Panic of 1819](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_of_1819 "Panic of 1819"), times were good. Audubon bought land and [slaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_United_States "Slavery in the United States"), founded a flour mill, and enjoyed his growing family. After 1819, Audubon went bankrupt and was thrown into jail for debt. The little money he earned was from drawing portraits, particularly [death-bed sketches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deathbed_portrait "Deathbed portrait"), greatly esteemed by country folk before photography.[\[64\]](#cite_note-65) He wrote, "\[M\]y heart was sorely heavy, for scarcely had I enough to keep my dear ones alive; and yet through these dark days I was being led to the development of the talents I loved."[\[65\]](#cite_note-66)

## Early ornithological career

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=John_James_Audubon&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Early ornithological career")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6c/181_Goldon_Eagle.jpg/250px-181_Goldon_Eagle.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:181_Goldon_Eagle.jpg)

Plate 181 of _The Birds of America_ by Audubon depicting a [golden eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_eagle "Golden eagle"), 1833–34

Audubon worked for a brief time as the first paid employee of the Western History Society, now known as The Museum of Natural History at The [Cincinnati Museum Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cincinnati_Museum_Center "Cincinnati Museum Center").[\[66\]](#cite_note-67) He then traveled south on the Mississippi with his gun, paintbox, and assistant [Joseph Mason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Mason_\(artist\) "Joseph Mason (artist)"), who stayed with him from October 1820 to August 1822 and painted the plant life backgrounds of many of Audubon's bird studies. He was committed to find and paint all the birds of North America for eventual publication. His goal was to surpass the earlier ornithological work of poet-naturalist [Alexander Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Wilson_\(ornithologist\) "Alexander Wilson (ornithologist)").[\[67\]](#cite_note-68) Though he could not afford to buy Wilson's work, Audubon used it to guide him when he had access to a copy.

In 1818, [Rafinesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafinesque "Rafinesque") visited Kentucky and the Ohio River valley to study fishes and was a guest of Audubon. In the middle of the night, Rafinesque noticed a bat in his room and thought it was a new species. He happened to grab Audubon's favourite violin in an effort to knock the bat down, resulting in the destruction of the violin. Audubon reportedly took revenge by showing drawings and describing some fictitious fishes and rodents to Rafinesque; Rafinesque gave scientific names to some of these fishes in his _Ichthyologia Ohiensis_.[\[68\]](#cite_note-Markle-69)[\[69\]](#cite_note-Woodman-2016-70)

On October 12, 1820, Audubon traveled into [Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi "Mississippi"), [Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alabama "Alabama"), and [Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida "Florida") in search of ornithological specimens. He traveled with [George Lehman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Lehman?action=edit&redlink=1 "George Lehman (page does not exist)"), a professional Swiss landscape painting artist. The following summer, he moved upriver to the [Oakley Plantation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oakley_Plantation "Oakley Plantation") in [Feliciana Parish, Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feliciana_Parish,_Louisiana "Feliciana Parish, Louisiana"), where he taught drawing to Eliza Pirrie, the young daughter of the owners. Though low-paying, the job was ideal, as it afforded him much time to roam and paint in the woods. (The plantation has been preserved as the [Audubon State Historic Site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audubon_State_Historic_Site "Audubon State Historic Site"), and is located at 11788 Highway 965, between [Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackson,_Louisiana "Jackson, Louisiana") and [St. Francisville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Francisville,_Louisiana "St. Francisville, Louisiana").)

Audubon called his future work _[The Birds of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Birds_of_America "The Birds of America")_. He attempted to paint one page each day. Painting with newly discovered technique, he decided his earlier works were inferior and re-did them.[\[70\]](#cite_note-71) He hired hunters to gather specimens for him. Audubon realized the ambitious project would take him away from his family for months at a time.

Audubon sometimes used his drawing talent to trade for goods or sell small works to raise cash. He made charcoal portraits on demand at $5 each and gave drawing lessons.[\[71\]](#cite_note-72) In 1823, Audubon took lessons in oil painting technique from John Steen, a teacher of American landscape, and history painter [Thomas Cole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Cole "Thomas Cole"). Though he did not use oils much for his bird work, Audubon earned good money painting oil portraits for patrons along the Mississippi. (Audubon's account reveals that he learned oil painting in December 1822 from Jacob Stein, an itinerant portrait artist. After they had enjoyed all the portrait patronage to be expected in [Natchez, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natchez,_Mississippi "Natchez, Mississippi"), during January–March 1823, they resolved to travel together as perambulating portrait-artists.)[\[72\]](#cite_note-Punke,_p._21-73)[\[73\]](#cite_note-Arthur,_pp._256-7-74) During this period (1822–1823), Audubon also worked as an instructor at [Jefferson College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jefferson_College_\(Mississippi\) "Jefferson College (Mississippi)") in [Washington, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_Mississippi "Washington, Mississippi").

Lucy became the steady breadwinner for the couple and their two young sons. Trained as a teacher, she conducted classes for children in their home. Later she was hired as a local teacher in Louisiana. She boarded with their children at the home of a wealthy plantation owner, as was often the custom of the time.[\[72\]](#cite_note-Punke,_p._21-73)[\[74\]](#cite_note-Arthur,_pp._258-9-75)

In 1824, Audubon returned to Philadelphia to seek a publisher for his bird drawings. He took oil painting lessons from [Thomas Sully](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Sully "Thomas Sully") and met [Charles Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Lucien_Bonaparte "Charles Lucien Bonaparte"), who admired his work and recommended he go to Europe to have his bird drawings engraved.[\[75\]](#cite_note-76) Audubon was nominated for membership at the [Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Natural_Sciences_of_Philadelphia "Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia") by [Charles Alexandre Lesueur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Alexandre_Lesueur "Charles Alexandre Lesueur"), [Reuben Haines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuben_Haines_III "Reuben Haines III"), and [Isaiah Lukens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaiah_Lukens "Isaiah Lukens"), on July 27, 1824.[\[76\]](#cite_note-Halley-2018b-77) However, he failed to gather enough support, and his nomination was rejected by vote on August 31, 1824;[\[76\]](#cite_note-Halley-2018b-77) around the same time accusations of scientific misconduct were levied by Alexander Lawson and others.[\[77\]](#cite_note-Dunlap-1834-78)

## _The Birds of America_

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=John_James_Audubon&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: The Birds of America")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/39/Campephilus_principalisAWP066AA2.jpg/250px-Campephilus_principalisAWP066AA2.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Campephilus_principalisAWP066AA2.jpg)

Plate from _The Birds of America_, featuring the extinct [ivory-billed woodpecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker "Ivory-billed woodpecker")

With his wife's support, in 1826 at age 41, Audubon took his growing collection of work to England. He sailed from New Orleans to [Liverpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool "Liverpool") on the cotton-hauling ship _Delos_, reaching England in the autumn of 1826 with his portfolio of over 300 drawings.[\[78\]](#cite_note-79) With letters of introduction to prominent Englishmen, and paintings of imaginary species including the "[Bird of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_of_Washington "Bird of Washington")",[\[10\]](#cite_note-Halley-2020-11) Audubon gained their quick attention. "I have been received here in a manner not to be expected during my highest enthusiastic hopes."[\[79\]](#cite_note-80)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/83/Brooklyn_Museum_-_American_Flamingo_-_John_J._Audubon.jpg/250px-Brooklyn_Museum_-_American_Flamingo_-_John_J._Audubon.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Brooklyn_Museum_-_American_Flamingo_-_John_J._Audubon.jpg)

An [American flamingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_flamingo "American flamingo") by Audubon, Brooklyn Museum

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/18/Brooklyn_Museum_-_American_Crow_-_John_J._Audubon.jpg/250px-Brooklyn_Museum_-_American_Crow_-_John_J._Audubon.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Brooklyn_Museum_-_American_Crow_-_John_J._Audubon.jpg)

An [American crow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_crow "American crow") by Audubon, Brooklyn Museum

Audubon's images of backwoods America and its natural attractions enjoyed widespread success among British customers. He met with great acceptance as he toured around England and Scotland, and was lionized as "the American woodsman". He raised enough money to begin publishing his _[The Birds of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Birds_of_America "The Birds of America")_. This monumental work consists of 435 hand-colored, life-size prints of 497 bird species, made from engraved copper plates of various sizes depending on the size of the image. They were printed on sheets measuring about 39 by 26 inches (990 by 660 mm).[\[80\]](#cite_note-Rhodes_2004,_p._403-81) The work illustrates slightly more than 700 North American bird species, of which some were based on specimens collected by fellow ornithologist [John Kirk Townsend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kirk_Townsend "John Kirk Townsend") on his journey across America with [Thomas Nuttall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Nuttall "Thomas Nuttall") in 1834 as part of [Nathaniel Jarvis Wyeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathaniel_Jarvis_Wyeth "Nathaniel Jarvis Wyeth")'s second expedition across the Rocky Mountains to the Pacific Ocean.[\[81\]](#cite_note-82)[\[82\]](#cite_note-83)

The pages were organized for artistic effect and contrasting interest, as if the reader were taking a visual tour. (Some critics thought he should have organized the plates in Linnaean order as befitting a "serious" ornithological treatise.)[\[83\]](#cite_note-84) The first and perhaps most famous plate was the wild turkey. Among the earliest plates printed was the "Bird of Washington", which generated favorable publicity for Audubon as his first discovery of a new species. However, no specimen of the species has ever been found, and research published in 2020 argues that this plate was a mixture of plagiarism and ornithological fraud.[\[10\]](#cite_note-Halley-2020-11)

The cost of printing the entire work was $115,640 (over $2,000,000 today), paid for from advance subscriptions, exhibitions, oil painting commissions, and animal skins, which Audubon hunted and sold.[\[80\]](#cite_note-Rhodes_2004,_p._403-81) Audubon's great work was a remarkable accomplishment. It took more than 14 years of field observations and drawings, plus his single-handed management and promotion of the project to make it a success. A reviewer wrote,

> All anxieties and fears which overshadowed his work in its beginning had passed away. The prophecies of kind but overprudent friends, who did not understand his self-sustaining energy, had proved untrue; the malicious hope of his enemies, for even the gentle lover of nature has enemies, had been disappointed; he had secured a commanding place in the respect and gratitude of men.[\[84\]](#cite_note-85)

Colorists applied each color in assembly-line fashion (over fifty were hired for the work).[\[85\]](#cite_note-86) The original edition was engraved in [aquatint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquatint "Aquatint") by Robert [Havell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havell_family "Havell family") Jr., who took over the task after the first ten plates engraved by [W. H. Lizars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._H._Lizars "W. H. Lizars") were deemed inadequate. Known as the Double Elephant folio for its [double elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_elephant "Double elephant") paper size, it is often regarded as the greatest picture book ever produced and the finest aquatint work. By the 1830s the aquatint process had been largely superseded by [lithography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithography "Lithography").[\[86\]](#cite_note-87) A contemporary French critic wrote, "A magic power transported us into the forests which for so many years this man of genius has trod. Learned and ignorant alike were astonished at the spectacle ... It is a real and palpable vision of the New World."[\[87\]](#cite_note-88)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fc/Brooklyn_Museum_-_Green_Heron_-_John_J._Audubon.jpg/250px-Brooklyn_Museum_-_Green_Heron_-_John_J._Audubon.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Brooklyn_Museum_-_Green_Heron_-_John_J._Audubon.jpg)

A [green heron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_heron "Green heron") by Audubon, Brooklyn Museum

Audubon sold oil-painted copies of the drawings to make extra money and publicize the book. A potential publisher had Audubon's portrait painted by John Syme, who clothed the naturalist in frontier clothes; the portrait was hung at the entrance of his exhibitions, promoting his rustic image. The painting is now held in the [White House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_House "White House") art collection, and is not frequently displayed.[\[88\]](#cite_note-89) The [New-York Historical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New-York_Historical_Society "New-York Historical Society") holds all 435 of the preparatory watercolors for _The Birds of America_. Lucy Audubon sold them to the society after her husband's death. All but 80 of the original copper plates were melted down when Lucy Audubon, desperate for money, sold them for scrap to the [Phelps Dodge Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phelps_Dodge_Corporation "Phelps Dodge Corporation").[\[89\]](#cite_note-90)

King [George IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_IV "George IV") was among the avid fans of Audubon and subscribed to support publication of the book. The British [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") recognized Audubon's achievement by electing him as a [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society"), the second American to be elected after [Benjamin Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin "Benjamin Franklin"). While in [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh "Edinburgh") to seek subscribers for the book, Audubon gave a demonstration of his method of supporting birds with wire at professor [Robert Jameson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Jameson "Robert Jameson")'s [Wernerian Natural History Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wernerian_Natural_History_Association "Wernerian Natural History Association"). Student [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin "Charles Darwin") was in the audience. Audubon also visited the dissecting theater of the anatomist [Robert Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Knox_\(surgeon\) "Robert Knox (surgeon)"). Audubon was also successful in France, gaining the King and several of the nobility as subscribers.[\[90\]](#cite_note-91)

[![Roseate Spoonbill](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/67/Large_Audubon_Spoonbill.jpg/250px-Large_Audubon_Spoonbill.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Large_Audubon_Spoonbill.jpg)

A painting of the [roseate spoonbill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roseate_spoonbill "Roseate spoonbill") by John James Audubon. Plate CCCXXI.

_The Birds of America_ became very popular during Europe's Romantic era.[\[91\]](#cite_note-audubon.org-92) Audubon's dramatic portraits of birds appealed to people in this period's fascination with natural history.[\[91\]](#cite_note-audubon.org-92)[\[92\]](#cite_note-93)[\[93\]](#cite_note-94)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/12/Falco_rusticolusAWP366AAA.jpg/250px-Falco_rusticolusAWP366AAA.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Falco_rusticolusAWP366AAA.jpg)

Two white [gyrfalcons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyrfalcon "Gyrfalcon") by Audubon

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7e/Lucy_Bakewell_Audubon.jpg/250px-Lucy_Bakewell_Audubon.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Lucy_Bakewell_Audubon.jpg)

Lucy Audubon c. 1870

Audubon returned to America in 1829 to complete more drawings for his magnum opus. He also hunted animals and shipped the valued skins to British associates. He was reunited with his family. After settling business affairs, Lucy accompanied him back to England. Audubon found that during his absence, he had lost some subscribers due to the uneven quality of coloring of the plates. Others were in arrears in their payments. His engraver fixed the plates and Audubon reassured subscribers, but a few begged off. He responded, "_The Birds of America_ will then raise in value as much as they are now depreciated by certain fools and envious persons."[\[94\]](#cite_note-95) He was elected a Fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences")[\[95\]](#cite_note-AAAS-96) in 1830 and to the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society")[\[96\]](#cite_note-97) in 1831.

He followed _The Birds of America_ with a sequel _Ornithological Biography_. This was a collection of life histories of each species written with Scottish ornithologist [William MacGillivray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_MacGillivray "William MacGillivray"). The two books were printed separately to avoid a British law requiring copies of all publications with text to be deposited in [copyright libraries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_libraries "Copyright libraries"), a huge financial burden for the self-published Audubon.[\[97\]](#cite_note-98) Both books were published between 1827 and 1839.

During the 1830s, Audubon continued making expeditions in North America. During a trip to [Key West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_West "Key West"), a companion wrote in a newspaper article, "Mr. Audubon is the most enthusiastic and indefatigable man I ever knew ... Mr. Audubon was neither dispirited by heat, fatigue, or bad luck ... he rose every morning at 3 o'clock and went out ... until 1 o'clock." Then he would draw the rest of the day before returning to the field in the evening, a routine he kept up for weeks and months.[\[98\]](#cite_note-99) In the posthumously published book _The Life of John James Audubon The Naturalist_,[\[60\]](#cite_note-postbook-61) edited by his widow and derived primarily from his notes, Audubon related visiting the northeastern Florida coastal sugar plantation of John Joachim Bulow for Christmas 1831/early January 1832. It was started by his father and at 4,675 acres, was the largest in East Florida.[\[99\]](#cite_note-memory-100) Bulow had a sugar mill built there under direction of a Scottish engineer, who accompanied Audubon on an excursion in the region. The mill was destroyed in 1836 in the [Second Seminole War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Seminole_War "Second Seminole War"). The plantation site is preserved today as the [Bulow Plantation Ruins Historic State Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulow_Plantation_Ruins_Historic_State_Park "Bulow Plantation Ruins Historic State Park").[\[99\]](#cite_note-memory-100)

In March 1832, Audubon booked passage at [St. Augustine, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Augustine,_Florida "St. Augustine, Florida"), aboard the schooner _Agnes_, bound for [Charleston, South Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charleston,_South_Carolina "Charleston, South Carolina"). A gale forced the vessel to berth at the mouth of the [Savannah River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savannah_River "Savannah River"), where an officer of the [United States Army Corps of Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Corps_of_Engineers "United States Army Corps of Engineers") on [Cockspur Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockspur_Island "Cockspur Island") where [Fort Pulaski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Pulaski "Fort Pulaski") was under construction, transported Audubon upstream to [Savannah, Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savannah,_Georgia "Savannah, Georgia"), on their barge. Just as he was about to board a Charleston-bound stage coach, he remembered William Gaston, a Savannah resident who had once befriended him. Audubon stayed at [City Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/21_West_Bay_Street "21 West Bay Street"), and the next day sought out and found the acquaintance, "who showed but little enthusiasm for his _Birds of America_" and who doubted that the book would sell a single copy in the city.[\[100\]](#cite_note-bell-101) A dejected Audubon continued to talk to the merchant and a mutual friend who, by chance, had appeared. The merchant, having further considered his position, said, "I subscribe to your work", gave him $200 for the first volume, and promised to act as his agent in finding additional subscriptions.[\[100\]](#cite_note-bell-101)

In 1833, Audubon sailed north from Maine, accompanied by his son John, and five other young colleagues, to explore the [ornithology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornithology "Ornithology") of [Labrador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labrador "Labrador"). On the return voyage, their ship _Ripley_ made a stop at St. George's, [Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newfoundland_\(island\) "Newfoundland (island)"). There Audubon and his assistants documented 36 species of birds.[\[101\]](#cite_note-102)

Audubon painted some of his works while staying at the Key West house and gardens of Capt. John H. Geiger. This site was preserved as the [Audubon House and Tropical Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audubon_House_and_Tropical_Gardens "Audubon House and Tropical Gardens").[\[102\]](#cite_note-103)

In 1841, having finished the _Ornithological Biographies_, Audubon returned to the United States with his family. He bought an estate on the [Hudson River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_River "Hudson River") in northern Manhattan. (The roughly 20-acre estate came to be known as [Audubon Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audubon_Park,_Manhattan "Audubon Park, Manhattan") in the 1860s when Audubon's widow began selling off parcels of the estate for the development of free-standing single family homes.)[\[103\]](#cite_note-104) Between 1840 and 1844, he published an [octavo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavo "Octavo") edition of _The Birds of America_, with 65 additional plates.[\[104\]](#cite_note-105) Printed in standard format to be more affordable than the oversize British edition, it earned $36,000 and was purchased by 1100 subscribers.[\[105\]](#cite_note-106) Audubon spent much time on "subscription-gathering trips", drumming up sales of the octavo edition, as he hoped to leave his family a sizeable income.[\[106\]](#cite_note-107)

Audubon sold human skulls to [Samuel George Morton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_George_Morton "Samuel George Morton"), including ones [stolen from graveyards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grave_robbery "Grave robbery").[\[12\]](#cite_note-Fabian2011-13) Yale professor [Ann Fabian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ann_Fabian "Ann Fabian") asserts Audubon raided at least 10 grave sites, collecting Native American skulls for Morton.[\[107\]](#cite_note-Clifton2023-108)

Audubon made some excursions out West where he hoped to record Western species he had missed, but his health began to fail. In 1848, he manifested signs of senility or possibly [dementia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dementia "Dementia") from what is now called [Alzheimer's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer's_disease "Alzheimer's disease"), his "noble mind in ruins".[\[108\]](#cite_note-109) He died at his family home in northern Manhattan on January 27, 1851. Audubon is buried in the graveyard at the [Church of the Intercession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_Intercession,_New_York "Church of the Intercession, New York") in the [Trinity Church Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_Church_Cemetery "Trinity Church Cemetery") and Mausoleum at 155th Street and Broadway in [Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan "Manhattan"), near his home. An imposing monument in his honor was erected at the cemetery, which is now recognized as part of the [Heritage Rose District of NYC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heritage_Rose_District_of_NYC "Heritage Rose District of NYC").[\[109\]](#cite_note-110)

Audubon's final work dealt with [mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammal "Mammal"); he prepared _The Viviparous Quadrupeds of North America_ (1845–1849) in collaboration with his good friend [Rev. John Bachman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bachman "John Bachman") of [Charleston, South Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charleston,_South_Carolina "Charleston, South Carolina"), who supplied much of the scientific text. His son, John Woodhouse Audubon, drew most of the plates. The work was completed by Audubon's sons, and the second volume was published posthumously in 1851.

Audubon developed his own methods for drawing birds. First, he killed them using fine shot. He then used wires to prop them into a natural position, unlike the common method of many ornithologists, who prepared and stuffed the specimens into a rigid pose. When working on a major specimen like an eagle, he would spend up to four 15-hour days, preparing, studying, and drawing it.[\[110\]](#cite_note-111) His paintings of birds are set true-to-life in their natural habitat. He often portrayed them as if caught in motion, especially feeding or hunting. This was in stark contrast to the stiff representations of birds by his contemporaries, such as [Alexander Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Wilson_\(ornithologist\) "Alexander Wilson (ornithologist)"). Audubon based his paintings on his extensive field observations. He worked primarily with watercolor early on. He added colored chalk or pastel to add softness to feathers, especially those of owls and herons.[\[111\]](#cite_note-112) He employed multiple layers of watercoloring, and sometimes used [gouache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gouache "Gouache"). All species were drawn life size which accounts for the contorted poses of the larger birds as Audubon strove to fit them within the page size.[\[112\]](#cite_note-113) Smaller species were usually placed on branches with berries, fruit, and flowers. He used several birds in a drawing to present all views of anatomy and wings. Larger birds were often placed in their ground habitat or perching on stumps. At times, as with woodpeckers, he combined several species on one page to offer contrasting features. He frequently depicted the birds' nests and eggs, and occasionally natural predators, such as snakes. He usually illustrated male and female variations, and sometimes juveniles. In later drawings, Audubon used assistants to render the habitat for him. In addition to faithful renderings of anatomy, Audubon also employed carefully constructed composition, drama, and slightly exaggerated poses to achieve artistic as well as scientific effects.\[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

## Dispute over accuracy

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=John_James_Audubon&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Dispute over accuracy")\]

The success of _Birds of America_ has been marred by numerous accusations of [plagiarism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagiarism "Plagiarism"), [scientific fraud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_fraud "Scientific fraud"), and deliberate manipulation of the primary record.[\[44\]](#cite_note-Halley-2018a-45)[\[10\]](#cite_note-Halley-2020-11)[\[5\]](#cite_note-Ord-1840-6)[\[77\]](#cite_note-Dunlap-1834-78)[\[113\]](#cite_note-Hunter-1983-114)[\[114\]](#cite_note-Halley-2022-115) Research has uncovered that Audubon falsified (and fabricated) scientific data,[\[69\]](#cite_note-Woodman-2016-70)[\[68\]](#cite_note-Markle-69) published fraudulent data and images in scientific journals and commercial books,[\[44\]](#cite_note-Halley-2018a-45)[\[10\]](#cite_note-Halley-2020-11)[\[5\]](#cite_note-Ord-1840-6)[\[114\]](#cite_note-Halley-2022-115) invented new species to impress potential subscribers[\[10\]](#cite_note-Halley-2020-11) and to "prank" rivals,[\[69\]](#cite_note-Woodman-2016-70)[\[68\]](#cite_note-Markle-69) and most likely stole the holotype specimen of [Harris's hawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harris's_hawk "Harris's hawk") (_Parabuteo unicinctus harrisi_) before pretending not to know its collector, who was one of his subscribers.[\[3\]](#cite_note-Fries-2006-4) He failed to credit work by Joseph Mason, prompting a series of articles in 1835 by critic [John Neal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Neal "John Neal") questioning Audubon's honesty and trustworthiness.[\[115\]](#cite_note-116) Audubon also repeatedly lied about the details of his autobiography, including the place and circumstances of his birth.[\[116\]](#cite_note-117)[\[117\]](#cite_note-118) His diaries, which might have cleared up some of these issues, were destroyed in 1895 by his granddaughter, Maria R. Audubon, who published a doctored version that realigned the "primary" record with some of his false narratives.[\[114\]](#cite_note-Halley-2022-115) Maria Audubon admitted to a biographer that she had "copied from it all \[she\] ever meant to give to the public" and then burned the original journals herself.

The litany of misconduct in Audubon's scientific career has drawn comparisons to others such as [Richard Meinertzhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Meinertzhagen "Richard Meinertzhagen").[\[10\]](#cite_note-Halley-2020-11)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/54/John_James_Audubon2.jpg/250px-John_James_Audubon2.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:John_James_Audubon2.jpg)

Audubon in later years, c. 1850

Audubon's influence on ornithology and natural history was far reaching. Nearly all later ornithological works were inspired by his artistry and high standards. [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin "Charles Darwin") cited Audubon three times in _[On the Origin of Species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species "On the Origin of Species")_ and also in later works.[\[118\]](#cite_note-119) Despite some errors in field observations, he made a significant contribution to the understanding of bird anatomy and behavior through his field notes. _The Birds of America_ is still considered one of the greatest examples of book art. Audubon discovered 25 new species and 12 new subspecies.[\[119\]](#cite_note-120)

*   He was elected to the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh "Royal Society of Edinburgh"), the [Linnean Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linnean_Society "Linnean Society"), and the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") in recognition of his contributions.
*   The homestead Mill Grove in [Audubon, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audubon,_Pennsylvania "Audubon, Pennsylvania"), is open to the public and contains a museum presenting all his major works, including _The Birds of America_.
*   The Audubon Museum at [John James Audubon State Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_James_Audubon_State_Park "John James Audubon State Park") in [Henderson, Kentucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henderson,_Kentucky "Henderson, Kentucky"), houses many of Audubon's original [watercolors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watercolor "Watercolor"), oils, [engravings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraving "Engraving") and personal memorabilia.
*   In 1905, the [National Audubon Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Audubon_Society "National Audubon Society") was incorporated and named in his honor. Its mission "is to conserve and restore natural ecosystems, focusing on birds ..."
*   He was honored in 1940 by the US Post Office with a 1 cent Famous Americans Series [postage stamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp "Postage stamp"); the stamp is green.
*   He was honored by the [United States Postal Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Postal_Service "United States Postal Service") with a 22¢ [Great Americans series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Americans_series "Great Americans series") [postage stamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp "Postage stamp").
*   On December 6, 2010, a copy of _The Birds of America_ was sold at a [Sotheby's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sotheby's "Sotheby's") auction for $11.5 million, the second highest price for a single printed book.[\[120\]](#cite_note-121)
*   On April 26, 2011, [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google "Google") celebrated his 226th birthday by displaying a special [Google Doodle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Doodle "Google Doodle") on its global homepage.[\[121\]](#cite_note-122)
*   Audubon's life and contributions to science and art was the subject of the 2017 film _[Audubon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audubon_\(film\) "Audubon (film)")_.
*   Since 2022, the National Audubon Society has been [undergoing discussion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audubon#Namesake_controversy "Audubon") about changing their name to distance themselves from Audubon's legacy of enslavement. Several local chapters, including Seattle, Chicago, Portland and New York City, have changed their names, while the board of the national body voted against doing so in 2023.[\[13\]](#cite_note-Uteuova-14)[\[122\]](#cite_note-123)[\[123\]](#cite_note-124)

In July 2007, [PBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBS "PBS")'s _[American Masters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Masters "American Masters")_ series aired an episode titled "John James Audubon: Drawn from Nature",[\[129\]](#cite_note-130) Supplemental material is available on the PBS website.

Audubon appears in the short story "Audubon In Atlantis" by [Harry Turtledove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Turtledove "Harry Turtledove"), published in the 2010 collection _Atlantis and Other Places_.[\[130\]](#cite_note-131) Audubon's drawings are also the important part of the plot of [Gary D. Schmidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_D._Schmidt "Gary D. Schmidt")'s children's novel _[Okay for Now](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okay_for_Now "Okay for Now")_.[\[131\]](#cite_note-132)

The choral oratorio _Audubon_ by James Kallembach was premiered on November 9, 2018, in Boston, Massachusetts by Chorus pro Musica.[\[132\]](#cite_note-133) The work depicts scenes of Audubon's life and descriptions of the birds he drew with text drawn from the 2004 biography by Richard Rhodes.[\[133\]](#cite_note-134)
