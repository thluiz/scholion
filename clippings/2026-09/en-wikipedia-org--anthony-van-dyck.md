**Sir Anthony van Dyck** (; [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language "Dutch language"): Antoon van Dijck [\[ˈɑntoːɱ‿vɑn ˈdɛik\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Dutch "Help:IPA/Dutch");[\[a\]](#cite_note-4) 22 March 1599 – 9 December 1641)[\[2\]](#cite_note-RKD-2)[\[1\]](#cite_note-bapt-1) was a [Flemish Baroque artist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flemish_Baroque_painting "Flemish Baroque painting"), who became the leading [court painter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_painter "Court painter") in England after success in the [Spanish Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Netherlands "Spanish Netherlands") and [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy "Italy").

The seventh child of Frans van Dyck, a wealthy silk merchant in [Antwerp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerp "Antwerp"), Anthony painted from an early age. He was successful as an independent painter in his late teens and became a master in the [Guild of Saint Luke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild_of_Saint_Luke "Guild of Saint Luke") of Antwerp on 18 October 1617.[\[4\]](#cite_note-davies-5) By this time, he was working in the studio of the leading northern painter of the day, [Peter Paul Rubens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Paul_Rubens "Peter Paul Rubens"), who became a major influence on his work.

Van Dyck worked in London for some months in 1621, then returned to Flanders for a brief time, before travelling to Italy, where he stayed until 1627, mostly in [Genoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genoa "Genoa").[\[5\]](#cite_note-6) In the late 1620s he completed his greatly admired _Iconography_ series of portrait [etchings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etching "Etching") of mainly other artists and other famous contemporaries. He spent five years in Flanders after his return from Italy, and from 1630 was court painter for the Archduchess [Isabella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabella_Clara_Eugenia "Isabella Clara Eugenia"), Habsburg Governor of Flanders. At the request of [Charles I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_England "Charles I of England") he returned in 1632 to London as the main court painter.

With the exception of [Holbein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Holbein_the_Younger "Hans Holbein the Younger"), van Dyck and his contemporary [Diego Velázquez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diego_Vel%C3%A1zquez "Diego Velázquez") were the first painters of pre-eminent talent to work mainly as court portraitists, revolutionising the genre. Van Dyck is best known for his portraits of the aristocracy, most notably Charles I, and his family and associates. He was the dominant influence on [English portrait-painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_school_of_painting "English school of painting") for over 150 years. He also painted [mythological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythological "Mythological"), [allegorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegorical "Allegorical") and [biblical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical "Biblical") subjects, including altarpieces, displayed outstanding facility as a draughtsman, and was an important innovator in [watercolour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watercolour "Watercolour") and [etching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etching "Etching").

His influence extends into the modern period. The [Van Dyke beard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Dyke_beard "Van Dyke beard") is named after him. During his lifetime, Charles I granted him a [knighthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor"), and he was buried in [St Paul's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Paul's_Cathedral "St Paul's Cathedral"), an indication of his standing at the time of his death.

### Family and early life

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anthony_van_Dyck&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Family and early life")\]

Anthony van Dyck was born in Antwerp on 22 March 1599 as the seventh of 12 children of his parents. He was baptised the next day in the _Onze-Lieve-Vrouwekerk_ (now the [Antwerp Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerp_Cathedral "Antwerp Cathedral")).[\[7\]](#cite_note-stichel-8) His father was Francoïs "Frans" van Dijck,[\[2\]](#cite_note-RKD-2)[\[1\]](#cite_note-bapt-1) a well-to-do silk merchant. His mother, Frans' second wife, was Maria Cupers,[\[b\]](#cite_note-cup-9)[\[2\]](#cite_note-RKD-2)[\[1\]](#cite_note-bapt-1) the daughter of Dirk Cupers[\[b\]](#cite_note-cup-9) and Catharina Conincx. Anthony was baptised on 23 March 1599 (as Anthonis van Dijck).[\[2\]](#cite_note-RKD-2)[\[1\]](#cite_note-bapt-1) His parental grandfather, also called Anthoni, had commenced his career as a painter and had been registered in 1556 as a master painter at the Antwerp [Guild of Saint Luke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild_of_Saint_Luke "Guild of Saint Luke") as a pupil of Jan Ghendrick, alias van Cleve. The elder brother of Anthony's grandfather was also admitted to the Guild as a master painter and had studied with Geert Ghendrick.[\[8\]](#cite_note-oxf-10) He had later become a successful merchant in silk and small writing articles. He had bought the house where Anthony was born, a property known as _Den Berendans_ ('The Bear Dance'), situated on Antwerp's [Grote Markt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grote_Markt,_Antwerp "Grote Markt, Antwerp") (main square) in 1579. On Anthony's mother's family there were also a several artists who were Guild members.[\[7\]](#cite_note-stichel-8)

After the birth of Anthony, his family moved to a house called the _Kasteel van Rijssel_ ('Castle of [Lille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lille "Lille")') in the city's Korte Nieuwstraat. His mother died when he was only 8 years old. At that time the family was living in a more luxurious house in the same street called the _Stadt van Ghent_ ('City of [Ghent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghent "Ghent")').[\[7\]](#cite_note-stichel-8)

Anthony's artistic talent became evident very early on. When he was 10 years old, he began formal training as a painter with [Hendrick van Balen the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrick_van_Balen_the_Elder "Hendrick van Balen the Elder"). Hendrick was a successful painter of small cabinet paintings who had several pupils. It is not known how long Anthony studied with van Balen, with estimates varying from two to four years. While it was common for apprentices to stay on in their master's workshop until they were formally registered as a master in the local guild, van Dyck is believed to have left his master's workshop in 1615 or 1616 to set up his independent workshop before he became a master. The reason was that in that period his father was experiencing financial difficulties and would have needed any assistance he could get. It may have been at this time that the young van Dyck may have started painting the series of panels of Christ and the Apostles in bust-length, although it is also possible that he undertook this only after returning from his first visit to Italy in 1620–21.[\[8\]](#cite_note-oxf-10)[\[7\]](#cite_note-stichel-8)

By the age of fifteen he was already a highly accomplished artist, as shown by his _Self-portrait_ dated 1613–14.[\[9\]](#cite_note-11) He was admitted to the Antwerp [Guild of Saint Luke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild_of_Saint_Luke "Guild of Saint Luke") as a free master on Saint Luke's day, 18 October 1617.[\[4\]](#cite_note-davies-5)

Within a few years he became the chief assistant to [Peter Paul Rubens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Paul_Rubens "Peter Paul Rubens"), the leading master painter of Antwerp and indeed of all Northern Europe. Rubens operated a large workshop and often relied on sub-contracted artists. His influence on the young artist was immense and for his part Rubens referred to the nineteen-year-old van Dyck as "the best of my pupils".[\[10\]](#cite_note-12)

The origins and exact nature of the relationship Dyck had with Rubens are unclear. It has been speculated that van Dyck was a pupil of Rubens from about 1613, as even his early work shows little trace of van Balen's style, but there is no clear evidence for this.[\[11\]](#cite_note-EW-13)

Certainly, the dominance of Rubens in Antwerp, a relatively small and declining city, could explain why, despite periodic returns to the city, van Dyck spent most of his career abroad.[\[11\]](#cite_note-EW-13) In 1620, in Rubens's contract for the major commission for the ceiling of the [Carolus Borromeuskerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolus_Borromeuskerk "Carolus Borromeuskerk"), the [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit "Jesuit") church at Antwerp (lost to fire in 1718), van Dyck is named as one of the "discipelen" who was to execute the paintings to Rubens' designs.[\[12\]](#cite_note-Martin,_op_and_page_cit-14) Unlike van Dyck, Rubens worked for most of the courts of Europe, but avoided exclusive attachment to any of them.\[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

In 1620, at the instigation of [George Villiers, Marquess of Buckingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Villiers,_1st_Duke_of_Buckingham "George Villiers, 1st Duke of Buckingham"), van Dyck went to England for the first time where he worked for King [James I of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_I_of_England "James I of England"), receiving £100.[\[11\]](#cite_note-EW-13) It was in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London") in the collection of the [Earl of Arundel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Howard,_21st_Earl_of_Arundel "Thomas Howard, 21st Earl of Arundel") that he first saw the work of [Titian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titian "Titian"), whose use of colour and subtle modelling of form would prove transformational, offering a new stylistic language that would enrich the compositional lessons van Dyck had learned from Rubens.[\[13\]](#cite_note-15)

Returned to Flanders after about four months, van Dyke then left in late 1621 for Italy, where he remained for six years. There he studied the Italian masters while starting a successful career as a portraitist. He was already presenting himself as a figure of consequence, annoying the rather bohemian colony of Northern artists in [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome "Rome"), as [Giovan Pietro Bellori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovan_Pietro_Bellori "Giovan Pietro Bellori") recounts, appearing with "the pomp of [Zeuxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeuxis_\(painter\) "Zeuxis (painter)") ... his behaviour was that of a nobleman rather than an ordinary person, and he shone in rich garments. Since he was accustomed in the circle of Rubens to noblemen, and being naturally of elevated mind, and anxious to make himself distinguished, he therefore wore, as well as silks, a hat with feathers and brooches, gold chains across his chest, and was accompanied by servants."[\[14\]](#cite_note-16)

[Peter Paul Rubens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Paul_Rubens "Peter Paul Rubens") – _Anthony van Dyck_ (1627–28)

While mostly based in [Genoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genoa "Genoa"), he also travelled extensively to other cities, and stayed for some time in [Palermo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palermo "Palermo"), in [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily "Sicily"), where he was quarantined during the 1624 plague, one of the worst in the island's history. There he produced an important series of paintings of the city's plague saint [Saint Rosalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Rosalia "Saint Rosalia"). His depictions of a young woman with flowing blonde hair wearing a Franciscan cowl and reaching down toward the imperilled city of Palermo, became the standard iconography of the saint from then onward and was extremely influential for Italian Baroque painters, from [Luca Giordano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luca_Giordano "Luca Giordano") to [Pietro Novelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietro_Novelli "Pietro Novelli"). Versions include those in [Madrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Rosalia_\(Anthony_van_Dyck\) "Saint Rosalia (Anthony van Dyck)"), [Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Rosalia_Crowned_by_Angels_\(Houston\) "Saint Rosalia Crowned by Angels (Houston)"), [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Rosalia_Crowned_by_Angels_\(London\) "Saint Rosalia Crowned by Angels (London)"), [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Rosalie_Interceding_for_the_Plague%E2%80%93Stricken_of_Palermo "Saint Rosalie Interceding for the Plague–Stricken of Palermo") and [Palermo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Rosalia_Crowned_by_Angels_\(Palermo\) "Saint Rosalia Crowned by Angels (Palermo)"), as well as [Saint Rosalia Interceding for the City of Palermo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Rosalia_Interceding_for_the_City_of_Palermo "Saint Rosalia Interceding for the City of Palermo") in Puerto Rico, and [Coronation of Saint Rosalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_of_Saint_Rosalia "Coronation of Saint Rosalia") in Vienna. Van Dyck's series of St. Rosalia paintings have been studied by [Gauvin Alexander Bailey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauvin_Alexander_Bailey "Gauvin Alexander Bailey") and [Xavier F. Salomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xavier_F._Salomon "Xavier F. Salomon"), both of whom curated or co-curated exhibitions devoted to the theme of Italian art and the plague.[\[15\]](#cite_note-17)[\[16\]](#cite_note-18)[\[17\]](#cite_note-19) In 2020, the _[New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times "New York Times")_ published an article about the [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art "Metropolitan Museum of Art")'s painting of Saint Rosalia by Van Dyck in the context of the [COVID-19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19 "COVID-19") virus.[\[18\]](#cite_note-20)

For the Genoese aristocracy, at that time enjoying a final flush of prosperity, van Dyke developed a full-length portrait style, drawing on [Paolo Veronese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Veronese "Paolo Veronese") and Titian as well as Rubens' style from the latter's own period in Genoa, where extremely tall but graceful figures look down on the viewer with great hauteur. In 1627, van Dyke went back to Antwerp where he remained for five years, painting portraits that were more affable in tone while still making his Flemish patrons look as stylish as possible. A life-size group portrait of twenty-four City Councillors of [Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels "Brussels") which he painted for the council-chamber was destroyed in 1695.[\[19\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECust1899-21) Evidently van Dyke was very charming to his patrons, and, like Rubens, well able to mix in aristocratic and court circles, a fact that added to his skill in obtaining commissions. By 1630, he was described as the court painter of the Habsburg Governor of Flanders, the Archduchess Isabella. In this period he also produced many religious works, including large [altarpieces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altarpiece "Altarpiece"), and began his printmaking.

_[Lord John Stuart and his Brother, Lord Bernard Stuart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_John_Stuart_and_his_Brother,_Lord_Bernard_Stuart "Lord John Stuart and his Brother, Lord Bernard Stuart")_ (c. 1638), exemplifies the more intimate, but still elegant style he developed in England

King Charles I was the most passionate collector of art among the [Stuart kings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Stuart "House of Stuart") and saw painting as a way of promoting his elevated view of the monarchy. In 1628, Charles bought the fabulous collection that the [Duke of Mantua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I,_Duke_of_Mantua "Charles I, Duke of Mantua") was forced to sell, and from his accession to the throne in 1625 made efforts to bring leading foreign painters to England. In 1626, he was able to persuade [Orazio Gentileschi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orazio_Gentileschi "Orazio Gentileschi") to settle in England, later to be joined by Orazio's daughter, [Artemisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisia_Gentileschi "Artemisia Gentileschi"), and some of his sons. Charles made a special point of targeting Rubens, who eventually came in 1630 on a diplomatic mission, which included painting, and who later sent Charles further paintings from Antwerp. Rubens was very well-treated during his nine-month visit, during which he was [knighted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor"). Charles's court portraitist, [Daniel Mytens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Mytens "Daniel Mytens"), was a somewhat pedestrian Dutchman. Moreover, Charles was very short, less than 5 feet (1.5 m) tall, and presented challenges to any portrait artist.

Van Dyck remained in touch with the English court and helped King Charles's agents in their search for pictures. He sent some of his own works, including a self-portrait (1623) with [Endymion Porter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endymion_Porter "Endymion Porter"), one of Charles's agents, his _Rinaldo and [Armida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armida "Armida")_ (1629), and a religious picture for Queen [Henrietta Maria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Maria_of_France "Henrietta Maria of France"). He had also painted Charles's sister, Queen [Elizabeth of Bohemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_of_Bohemia "Elizabeth of Bohemia"), at [The Hague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hague "The Hague") in 1632. In April of that year, van Dyck returned to London and was immediately taken under the wing of the court, being knighted in July and at the same time being granted a pension of £200 a year, the grant describing him as _[principalle Paynter in ordinary to their majesties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_Painter_in_Ordinary "Principal Painter in Ordinary")_.[\[20\]](#cite_note-Grosvenor_Gallery_Stephens_1887_p._14-22)[\[21\]](#cite_note-Sharpe_Lake_1993_p._223-23)

In addition, van Dyke was well paid for his paintings, or at least in theory, as King Charles did not actually hand over the pension for five years and reduced the price of many paintings. A house was provided on the [River Thames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Thames "River Thames") at [Blackfriars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackfriars,_London "Blackfriars, London"), then just outside the [City of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London "City of London"), thus avoiding for van Dyke any difficulty with the monopoly of the [Worshipful Company of Painter-Stainers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worshipful_Company_of_Painter-Stainers "Worshipful Company of Painter-Stainers"). A suite of rooms in [Eltham Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eltham_Palace "Eltham Palace"), no longer used by the royal family, was also put at his disposal as a country retreat. These residences were managed by his mistress [Margaret Lemon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Lemon "Margaret Lemon").[\[22\]](#cite_note-james-24)

His Blackfriars studio was frequently visited by the King and Queen (later a special causeway was built to ease their access), who hardly ever sat for another painter as long as van Dyck lived.[\[11\]](#cite_note-EW-13)[\[19\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECust1899-21)

So it was that van Dyck was an immediate success in England, where he painted large numbers of portraits of the King and Queen, as well as their children. Many portraits were done in several versions, to be sent as diplomatic gifts or given to supporters of the increasingly embattled king. Altogether van Dyck has been estimated to have painted forty portraits of King Charles himself, as well as about thirty of the Queen, nine of the [Earl of Strafford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wentworth,_1st_Earl_of_Strafford "Thomas Wentworth, 1st Earl of Strafford"), and multiple ones of other courtiers.[\[23\]](#cite_note-25) He painted many of the court, and also himself and his mistress, Margaret Lemon.[\[22\]](#cite_note-james-24)

In England van Dyck developed an adapted version of his style which combined a relaxed elegance and ease with an understated authority in his subjects. This style was to dominate English portrait-painting to the end of the 18th century. His portraits of Charles on horseback updated the grandeur of Titian's _[Equestrian Portrait of Charles V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equestrian_Portrait_of_Charles_V "Equestrian Portrait of Charles V")_, but even more effective and original is his portrait of Charles dismounted in the [Louvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvre "Louvre"): "Charles is given a totally natural look of instinctive sovereignty, in a deliberately informal setting where he strolls so negligently that he seems at first glance nature's gentleman rather than England's King".[\[24\]](#cite_note-26) Although his portraits have created the classic idea of "[Cavalier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalier "Cavalier")" style and dress, in fact a majority of his most important patrons in the nobility, such as [Lord Wharton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Wharton,_4th_Baron_Wharton "Philip Wharton, 4th Baron Wharton") and the Earls of [Bedford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Russell,_4th_Earl_of_Bedford "Francis Russell, 4th Earl of Bedford"), [Northumberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algernon_Percy,_10th_Earl_of_Northumberland "Algernon Percy, 10th Earl of Northumberland") and [Pembroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Herbert,_4th_Earl_of_Pembroke "Philip Herbert, 4th Earl of Pembroke"), were to take the [Parliamentarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundhead "Roundhead") side in the [English Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War "English Civil War") that broke out soon after the painter's death.[\[19\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECust1899-21)

The King [in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_in_Council "Queen in Council") [Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privy_Council_of_England "Privy Council of England") by [letters patent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_patent "Letters patent") granted van Dyck _[denizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denization "Denization")_ in 1638. On 27 February 1640 he married Mary Ruthven, with whom he had one daughter.[\[25\]](#cite_note-Wood_2010-27)[\[26\]](#cite_note-28) Mary was the daughter of [Patrick Ruthven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Ruthven_\(physician\)?action=edit&redlink=1 "Patrick Ruthven (physician) (page does not exist)"), who, although the title was forfeited, styled himself [Lord Ruthven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_of_Gowrie#Lords_Ruthven_\(1488\) "Earl of Gowrie").[\[27\]](#cite_note-29) She was a [lady-in-waiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady-in-waiting "Lady-in-waiting") to the Queen in 1639–40; this may have been instigated by the King in an attempt to keep van Dyke in England.[\[11\]](#cite_note-EW-13) He had spent most of 1634 in Antwerp, returning the following year, and in 1640–41, as the Civil War loomed, spent several months in Flanders and [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_France "Kingdom of France"). In 1640 he accompanied prince [John Casimir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_II_Casimir_Vasa "John II Casimir Vasa") of [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish-Lithuanian_Commonwealth "Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth") after he was freed from French imprisonment.[\[28\]](#cite_note-treasures-30)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/11/Anthonis_van_Dyck_036.jpg/250px-Anthonis_van_Dyck_036.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Anthonis_van_Dyck_036.jpg)

_Portrait of [Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary,_Princess_Royal_and_Princess_of_Orange "Mary, Princess Royal and Princess of Orange"), daughter of [Charles I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_England "Charles I of England") with her husband the [Prince of Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_II,_Prince_of_Orange "William II, Prince of Orange")_ (1641), [Rijksmuseum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijksmuseum "Rijksmuseum")

A letter dated 13 August 1641, from [Lady Roxburghe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Ker,_Countess_of_Roxburghe "Jean Ker, Countess of Roxburghe") in England to a correspondent in The Hague, reported that van Dyck was recuperating from a long illness.[\[29\]](#cite_note-Jaffe-31) In November, van Dyck's condition worsened, and he returned to England from Paris, where he had gone to paint [Cardinal Richelieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_Richelieu "Cardinal Richelieu").[\[29\]](#cite_note-Jaffe-31) He died at his home in Blackfriars on 9 December 1641,[\[2\]](#cite_note-RKD-2) the same day as the baptism of his new-born daughter Justiniana.[\[2\]](#cite_note-RKD-2)[\[25\]](#cite_note-Wood_2010-27) He was buried on 11 December,[\[2\]](#cite_note-RKD-2) in the choir of [St Paul's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_St_Paul's_Cathedral "Old St Paul's Cathedral"). His body and tomb (erected by the king) were destroyed in the [Great Fire of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Fire_of_London "Great Fire of London") in 1666.[\[25\]](#cite_note-Wood_2010-27)[\[30\]](#cite_note-32)

## Portraits and other works

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anthony_van_Dyck&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Portraits and other works")\]

In the 17th century, demand for portraits was stronger than for other types of work. Van Dyck tried to persuade Charles to commission large-scale series on the history of the [Order of the Garter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_Garter "Order of the Garter") for the [Banqueting House, Whitehall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banqueting_House,_Whitehall "Banqueting House, Whitehall"), for which Rubens had earlier completed the large ceiling paintings (sending them from Antwerp). A sketch for one wall remains, but by 1638 Charles was too short of money to proceed.[\[11\]](#cite_note-EW-13) This was a problem Velázquez did not have, but equally van Dyck's daily life was not encumbered by trivial court duties as faced by Velázquez. In his visits to Paris in his last years, van Dyck attempted to obtain the commission to paint the Grande Gallerie of the [Louvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvre "Louvre") without success.[\[31\]](#cite_note-33)

A list of history paintings produced by van Dyck in England survives. It was compiled by van Dyck's biographer Bellori, based on information from [Sir Kenelm Digby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Kenelm_Digby "Sir Kenelm Digby"). None of these works appear to remain, except the _Eros and Psyche_ done for the King.[\[11\]](#cite_note-EW-13) But many other works, rather more religious than mythological, do survive, and though they are very fine, they do not reach the heights of Velázquez's history paintings. Earlier ones remain very much within the style of Rubens, although some of his Sicilian works are individualistic.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/86/Anthonis_van_Dyck_013.jpg/250px-Anthonis_van_Dyck_013.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Anthonis_van_Dyck_013.jpg)

_[Henrietta Maria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Maria "Henrietta Maria") and the [dwarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarfism "Dwarfism"), [Sir Jeffrey Hudson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Jeffrey_Hudson "Sir Jeffrey Hudson")_ (1633)

Van Dyck's portraits flattered more than Velázquez's. When [Sophia of Hanover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophia_of_Hanover "Sophia of Hanover") first met Queen Henrietta Maria (who was in exile in Holland) in 1641, she wrote: "Van Dyck's handsome portraits had given me so fine an idea of the beauty of all English ladies, that I was surprised to find that the Queen, who looked so fine in painting, was a small woman raised up on her chair, with long skinny arms and teeth like defence works projecting from her mouth..."[\[11\]](#cite_note-EW-13)

Some critics have blamed van Dyck for diverting a nascent, tougher English portrait tradition—of painters such as [William Dobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Dobson "William Dobson"), [Robert Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Walker_\(painter\) "Robert Walker (painter)") and [Isaac Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Fuller "Isaac Fuller")—into what certainly became elegant blandness in the hands of many of van Dyck's successors, like [Peter Lely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lely "Peter Lely") or [Godfrey Kneller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godfrey_Kneller "Godfrey Kneller").[\[11\]](#cite_note-EW-13) The conventional view has always been more favourable: "When Van Dyck came hither he brought Face-Painting to us; ever since which time ... England has excel'd all the World in that great Branch of the Art" ([Jonathan Richardson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Richardson "Jonathan Richardson"): _An Essay on the Theory of Painting_ (1715, 41). [Thomas Gainsborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Gainsborough "Thomas Gainsborough") is reported to have said on his deathbed "We are all going to heaven, and Van Dyck is of the Company."[\[19\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECust1899-21)

A fairly small number of [landscape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape_art "Landscape art") pen and wash drawings or [watercolours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watercolour "Watercolour") made in England played an important part in introducing the Flemish watercolour landscape tradition to England. Some are studies, which reappear in the background of paintings, but many are signed and dated and were probably regarded as finished works to be given as presents. Several of the most detailed are of [Rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rye,_East_Sussex "Rye, East Sussex"), a port for ships to the Continent, suggesting that van Dyck did them casually whilst waiting for wind or tide to improve.[\[32\]](#cite_note-34)<

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4e/Van_Dyck_Pieter_Brueghel_the_Younger.jpg/250px-Van_Dyck_Pieter_Brueghel_the_Younger.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Van_Dyck_Pieter_Brueghel_the_Younger.jpg)

Etching of [Pieter Brueghel the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieter_Brueghel_the_Younger "Pieter Brueghel the Younger") from the _Iconography_

Probably during his period in Antwerp after his return from Italy, van Dyck began his _Iconographie_, which became a very large series of [prints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_master_print "Old master print") with half-length portraits of eminent contemporaries. He produced drawings, and for eighteen of the portraits he himself [etched](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etching "Etching") the heads and main outlines of the figure, for an [engraver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraving "Engraving") to work up: "Portrait etching had scarcely had an existence before his time, and in his work it suddenly appears at the highest point ever reached in the art".[\[33\]](#cite_note-Hind-35)[\[34\]](#cite_note-36)

He left most of the [printmaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printmaking "Printmaking") to specialists, who engraved after his drawings. His etched plates appear not to have been published until after his death, and early states are very rare.[\[35\]](#cite_note-Decker-37) Most of his plates were printed after only his work had been done. Some exist in further [states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_\(printmaking\) "State (printmaking)") after engraving had been added, sometimes obscuring his etching. He continued to add to the series until at least his departure for England, and presumably added [Inigo Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inigo_Jones "Inigo Jones") whilst in London.

The series was a great success, but was his only venture into printmaking; portraiture probably paid better. At his death there were eighty plates by others, of which fifty-two were of artists, as well as his own eighteen. The plates were bought by a publisher; with the plates reworked periodically as they wore out they continued to be printed for centuries, and the series added to, so that it reached over two hundred portraits by the late 18th century. In 1851, the plates were bought by the _Calcographie du [Louvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvre "Louvre")_.[\[35\]](#cite_note-Decker-37)

The _Iconographie_ was highly influential as a commercial model for reproductive printmaking; now forgotten series of portrait prints were enormously popular until the advent of [photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photography "Photography"): "the importance of this series was enormous, and it provided a repertory of images that were plundered by portrait painters throughout Europe over the next couple of centuries".[\[19\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECust1899-21) Van Dyck's brilliant etching style, which depended on open lines and dots, was in marked contrast to that of the other great portraitist in prints of the period, [Rembrandt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rembrandt "Rembrandt"), and had little influence until the 19th century, when it had a great influence on artists such as [Whistler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_McNeill_Whistler "James McNeill Whistler") in the last major phase of portrait etching.[\[33\]](#cite_note-Hind-35) [Hyatt Mayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyatt_Mayor "Hyatt Mayor") wrote:

> Etchers have studied Van Dyck ever since, for they can hope to approximate his brilliant directness, whereas nobody can hope to approach the complexity of Rembrandt's portraits.[\[36\]](#cite_note-38)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b5/Portrait_of_Adriaen_Brouwer_by_Anthony_van_Dyck.jpg/250px-Portrait_of_Adriaen_Brouwer_by_Anthony_van_Dyck.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Portrait_of_Adriaen_Brouwer_by_Anthony_van_Dyck.jpg)

_Portrait of [Adriaen Brouwer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adriaen_Brouwer "Adriaen Brouwer")_

Van Dyck's success led him to maintain a large workshop in London, which became "virtually a production line for portraits". According to a visitor he usually only made a drawing on paper, which was then enlarged onto canvas by an assistant; he then painted the head himself. He often used [blue paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_paper "Blue paper") for these preparatory studies. The costume in which the client wished to be painted was left at the studio and often with the unfinished canvas sent out to artists specialised in rendering such clothing.[\[19\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTECust1899-21) In his last years these studio collaborations accounted for some decline in the quality of work.[\[37\]](#cite_note-39)

In addition many copies untouched by him, or virtually so, were produced by the workshop, as well as by professional copyists and later painters. The number of paintings ascribed to him had by the 19th century become huge, as with Rembrandt, [Titian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titian "Titian") and others. However, most of his assistants and copyists could not approach the refinement of his manner, so compared to many masters consensus among [art historians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_historian "Art historian") on attributions to him is usually relatively easy to reach, and museum labelling is now mostly updated ([country house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_country_houses "English country houses") attributions may be more dubious in some cases).\[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

The relatively few names of his assistants that are known are Dutch or Flemish. He probably preferred to use trained Flemish artists, as no equivalent English training existed in this period.[\[11\]](#cite_note-EW-13) Van Dyck's enormous influence on English art does not come from a tradition handed down through his pupils. In fact it is not possible to document a connection to his studio for any English painter of any significance.[\[11\]](#cite_note-EW-13) Dutchman [Adriaen Hanneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adriaen_Hanneman "Adriaen Hanneman") (1604–1671) returned to his native city, [The Hague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hague "The Hague") in 1638 to become the leading portraitist there.[\[38\]](#cite_note-40)

Flemish painter [Pieter Thijs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieter_Thijs "Pieter Thijs") studied in van Dyck's workshop as one of van Dyck's last pupils. He became a very successful portrait and history painter in his native Antwerp.[\[39\]](#cite_note-ox-41)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b0/Sir_Anthony_Van_Dyck_-_Charles_I_%281600-49%29_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg/330px-Sir_Anthony_Van_Dyck_-_Charles_I_%281600-49%29_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sir_Anthony_Van_Dyck_-_Charles_I_\(1600-49\)_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)

_[Charles I in Three Positions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_in_Three_Positions "Charles I in Three Positions")_ (1635–36), [Royal Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Collection "Royal Collection"). A triple portrait of Charles I, was sent to Rome for [Bernini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernini "Bernini") to model a bust on.

Much later, the styles worn by his models provided the names of the [Van Dyke beard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Dyke_beard "Van Dyke beard") for the sharply pointed and trimmed goatees popular for men in his day, and the [van Dyke collar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clothing_terminology#Costume_historian's_terms "Clothing terminology"), "a wide collar across the shoulders edged copiously with lace".[\[40\]](#cite_note-beard-42) During the reign of [George III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_III "George III"), a generic "Cavalier" fancy-dress costume called a _Van Dyke_ was popular. Gainsborough's _[The Blue Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blue_Boy "The Blue Boy")_ is wearing such a _Van Dyke_ outfit. In 1774 [Derby porcelain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derby_porcelain "Derby porcelain") advertised a figure, after a portrait by [Johann Zoffany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Zoffany "Johann Zoffany"), of "[the King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_III "George III") in a Vandyck dress".[\[41\]](#cite_note-43)

A confusing number of different pigments used in painting have been called "Vandyke brown" (mostly in English-language sources). Some predate van Dyck, and it is not clear that he used any of them.[\[42\]](#cite_note-44) [Van Dyke brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Dyke_brown_\(printing\) "Van Dyke brown (printing)") is an early photographic printing process using such a colour.

When van Dyck was [knighted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor") in 1632, he [anglicised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicised "Anglicised") his name to Vandyke.[\[43\]](#cite_note-Massie-45) The heraldic [blazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blazon "Blazon") of his [coat of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms "Coat of arms") is _Quarters 1 & 4. Azure six roundels 3, 2 and 1 Or and for augmentation on a chief Gules a lion passant gardant Or. 2 & 3. Sable a saltire Or. Over all an inescutcheon Or thereon a bend sinister Azure_. The coat of arms is crested with a greyhound's head.[\[44\]](#cite_note-Siddons-46)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/Bewening_van_Christus%2C_Anthony_van_Dyck%2C_%281635%29%2C_Koninklijk_Museum_voor_Schone_Kunsten_Antwerpen%2C_404.jpg/330px-Bewening_van_Christus%2C_Anthony_van_Dyck%2C_%281635%29%2C_Koninklijk_Museum_voor_Schone_Kunsten_Antwerpen%2C_404.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bewening_van_Christus,_Anthony_van_Dyck,_\(1635\),_Koninklijk_Museum_voor_Schone_Kunsten_Antwerpen,_404.jpg)

_Lamentation of Christ_ (1635), [Royal Museum of Fine Arts Antwerp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Museum_of_Fine_Arts_Antwerp "Royal Museum of Fine Arts Antwerp")

The British [Royal Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Collection "Royal Collection"), which still contains many of his paintings, has a total of twenty-six paintings.[\[45\]](#cite_note-47) The [National Gallery, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery,_London "National Gallery, London") (fourteen works), The _[Museo del Prado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museo_del_Prado "Museo del Prado")_ (Spain) (twenty-five Works, such as: _Self-portrait with Endymion Porter_, _The Metal Serpent_, _Christ Crowned with Thorns_, _The taking of Christ_, _Portrait of Mary Ruthven_, the painter's Wife),[\[46\]](#cite_note-48) The [Louvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvre "Louvre") in [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris "Paris") (eighteen works), the [Alte Pinakothek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alte_Pinakothek "Alte Pinakothek") in [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich "Munich"), the [National Gallery of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Art "National Gallery of Art") in [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C. "Washington, D.C."), the [Museum of Fine Arts, Boston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Fine_Arts,_Boston "Museum of Fine Arts, Boston"), and the [Frick Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frick_Collection "Frick Collection") have examples of his portrait style. [Wilton House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilton_House "Wilton House") still holds the works he did for one of his main patrons, the Earl of Pembroke, including his largest work, a huge family group portrait with ten main figures. [Petworth House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petworth_House "Petworth House") also contains numerous works, many executed for the [Percy family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_family "Percy family").[\[47\]](#cite_note-49)

Spanish museums own a rich presence of this artist in addition to the Prado's ensemble. The [Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thyssen-Bornemisza_Museum "Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum") preserves _the Portrait of Jacques Le Roy_,[\[48\]](#cite_note-50) property of The Carmen Thyssen-Bornemisza Collection but also on display at the Museum there's a _Crucified Christ_,[\[49\]](#cite_note-51) and the [Bilbao Fine Arts Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilbao_Fine_Arts_Museum "Bilbao Fine Arts Museum") houses a great _Lamentation before the dead Christ_.[\[50\]](#cite_note-52) In 2008, [Patrimonio Nacional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrimonio_Nacional "Patrimonio Nacional") of Spain recovered a _Martyrdom of Saint Sebastian_ and returned it to [El Escorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Escorial "El Escorial"), two centuries after its removal and, subsequently, The [Real Academia de Bellas Artes de San Fernando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Academia_de_Bellas_Artes_de_San_Fernando "Real Academia de Bellas Artes de San Fernando") has revealed as its own a long-stored painting, added to another, _The Virgin with the Child with the repentant sinners_,[\[51\]](#cite_note-53) in addition the institution has an original sketch. In addition, in December 2017, a _Virgin with Child_, which is kept in the [Cerralbo Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerralbo_Museum "Cerralbo Museum") and was previously considered the work of [Mateo Cerezo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mateo_Cerezo "Mateo Cerezo"), was revealed as the painter's original after an exhaustive study and restoration project.[\[52\]](#cite_note-54) Finally, the [Museum of Fine Arts of Valencia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museu_de_Belles_Arts_de_Val%C3%A8ncia "Museu de Belles Arts de València") owns an _Equestrian Portrait of Don Francisco de Moncada_.[\[53\]](#cite_note-55)

[Tate Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tate_Britain "Tate Britain") held the exhibition _Van Dyck & Britain_ in 2009.[\[54\]](#cite_note-56) In 2016 the [Frick Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frick_Collection "Frick Collection") in New York had an exhibition "Van Dyck: The Anatomy of Portraiture", the first major survey of the artist's work in the United States in over two decades.[\[55\]](#cite_note-57)

The estate of the [Earl Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Spencer,_9th_Earl_Spencer "Charles Spencer, 9th Earl Spencer") at [Althorp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Althorp "Althorp") houses a small collection of van Dycks including _War and Peace_ (Portrait of Sir [George Digby, 2nd Earl of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Digby,_2nd_Earl_of_Bristol "George Digby, 2nd Earl of Bristol"), English Royalist politician with [William Russell, 1st Duke of Bedford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Russell,_1st_Duke_of_Bedford "William Russell, 1st Duke of Bedford")), which is the most valuable painting in the collection and the favourite of the earl.[\[56\]](#cite_note-58)

The last name is spelt in (old-)Dutch as 'van Dijck', 'van Dyck', 'van Dijk', and 'van Deick',[\[2\]](#cite_note-RKD-2) , meaning "from \[the\] [dyke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levee "Levee")". The Dutch 'Dyck' is an old-fashioned contraction of the spelling "'Dijck'", with the ["IJ" digraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IJ_\(digraph\) "IJ (digraph)").[\[3\]](#cite_note-3) In Dutch "[van](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_\(Dutch\) "Van (Dutch)")" is not capitalised, and in modern-Dutch the last name is spelt as '[van Dijk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Dijk "Van Dijk")'. In English, a capitalised "Van" in "Van Dyck" was more usual until recent decades (used by the Waterhouse paint brand for example), and the 'Dyke' spelling was often used during his lifetime and later (and is usual for [the beard style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Dyke_beard "Van Dyke beard")).
