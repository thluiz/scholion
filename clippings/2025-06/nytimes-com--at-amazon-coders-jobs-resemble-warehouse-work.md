# At Amazon, Some Coders Say Their Jobs Have Begun to Resemble Warehouse Work - The New York Times

> Pushed to use artificial intelligence, software developers at the e-commerce giant say they must work faster and have less time to think. Others welcome the shift.

Since at least the industrial revolution, workers have worried that machines would replace them. But when technology transformed auto-making, meatpacking and even secretarial work, the response typically wasn't to slash jobs and reduce the number of workers. It was to "degrade" the jobs, breaking them into simpler tasks to be performed over and over at a rapid clip. Small shops of skilled mechanics gave way to hundreds of workers spread across an assembly line. The personal secretary gave way to pools of typists and data-entry clerks. Workers "complained of speed-up, work intensification, and work degradation," as labor historian Jason Resnikoff described it.

Something similar appears to be happening with artificial intelligence in one of the fields where it has been most widely adopted: coding. As A.I. spreads through the labor force, many white-collar workers have expressed concern about mass unemployment, but the more immediate downside for software engineers appears to be a change in the quality of their work — more routine, less thoughtful, and much faster paced.

A recent paper by researchers at Microsoft and three universities found that programmers' use of Copilot increased a key measure of output more than 25 percent. At Amazon, which is making big investments in generative A.I., the culture of coding is changing rapidly. CEO Andy Jassy wrote in his recent shareholder letter that generative A.I. was yielding big returns for companies using it for "productivity and cost avoidance," and cited coding as an activity where A.I. would "change the norms."

Three Amazon engineers said managers had increasingly pushed them to use A.I. over the past year, that the company had raised output goals and become less forgiving about deadlines, and had even encouraged coders to build new A.I. productivity tools at an internal hackathon. One engineer said his team was roughly half the size it had been last year, but expected to produce roughly the same amount of code using A.I. Amazon said it conducts regular reviews to ensure teams are adequately staffed and may increase their size if necessary.

Other tech companies are moving similarly: Shopify's CEO announced "A.I. usage is now a baseline expectation" and would add A.I. usage questions to performance reviews. Google held a companywide hackathon with a category for tools that "enhance overall daily productivity"; a Google spokesman noted more than 30% of the company's code is now suggested by A.I. and accepted by developers.

The shift has not been all negative. Managers argue A.I. can relieve employees of tedious tasks. Jassy wrote the company saved "the equivalent of 4,500 developer-years" using A.I. to upgrade old software. Lawrence Katz, a labor economist at Harvard, said eliminating tedious work may benefit a subset of accomplished programmers, but for inexperienced programmers the result can resemble the shift from artisanal to factory work: "Things look like a speed-up for knowledge workers... There is a sense that the employer can pile on more stuff."

## Bystanders in Their Own Jobs

Amazon engineers have watched their blue-collar counterparts undergo a similar transition. Amazon warehouses increasingly rely on robotics — pickers stand in one spot while lawn-mower-like robots deliver inventory. The robots haven't displaced humans broadly (Amazon has hired hundreds of thousands of warehouse workers since introducing them) but have increased items picked per hour into the hundreds from dozens, with some workers complaining the job became hyper-repetitive and physically taxing.

One engineer said building a website feature used to take a few weeks; now it must frequently be done within a few days, possible only by using A.I. to automate coding and cutting down on meetings for feedback and alternative ideas (a second engineer said her efficiency gains were more modest — Amazon has more than 10,000 software engineers, and different teams use the tools more or less intensively). Katz noted the new approach has eliminated much of the time developers spend reflecting on their work: "It used to be that you had a lot of slack... Now, you have the whole thing monitored, and it can be done quickly."

Amazon has rolled out A.I. tools that can generate large portions of a program on its own; one engineer called them "scarily good," though many colleagues have been reluctant to use them because they require a lot of double-checking. Simon Willison, a longtime programmer, said: "It's more fun to write code than to read code... If you're told you have to do a code review, it's never a fun part of the job. When you're working with these tools, it's most of the job."

Managers have encouraged engineers to use A.I. to draft one-page memos proposing solutions from scattered thoughts, and to test software features — a tedious job that nonetheless forced deep thinking about the code. One engineer said automating these functions could deprive junior engineers of the know-how needed for promotion. Amazon said collaboration and experimentation remain critical and it considers A.I. a tool for augmenting rather than replacing engineers' expertise.

Harper Reed, another longtime programmer who was CTO of Barack Obama's re-election campaign, agreed career advancement could be an issue in an A.I. world, but cautioned against being overly precious about deeply understanding one's code: "It would be crazy if in an auto factory people were measuring to make sure every angle is correct... It's not as important as when it was a group of ten people pounding out the metal." He added that just as offshore manufacturing made it cheap to make physical products, A.I. is likely to democratize software-making: "If you're a prototyper, this is a gift from heaven. You can knock something out that illustrates the idea."

## The Dreaded Speed Up

Many Amazon engineers have joined Amazon Employees for Climate Justice, a group that has become a clearinghouse for workers' anxieties about issues including A.I. and return-to-office mandates. Eliza Pan, a former Amazon employee and spokeswoman for the group, said complaints center on "what their careers are going to look like... And not just their careers, but the quality of the work."

There is no rush to unionize coders at Amazon, but such a move wouldn't be unheard of. When General Motors workers struck in 1936 to demand union recognition, it was the dreaded speed-up that spurred them on. Historian Sidney Fine wrote that the typical worker felt "that he was not free, as perhaps he had been on some previous job, to set the pace of his work, and to determine the manner in which it was to be performed."
