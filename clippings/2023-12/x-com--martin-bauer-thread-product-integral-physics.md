## 1/12

If you want to define a continuous addition you get the Riemann integral

This is what happens if you want to define a continuous *product* 

One of the weirdest and most satisfying integrals you've ever seen and why it's important for physics

## 2/12

If you derive the Riemann integral you do so by approximating the area under a function with discrete blocks and take the limit of their sum where they become infinitesimally thin 
2/12

## 3/12

But what happens if instead of the sum you take the product? You get the 'product integral'

## 4/12

If f(x) is a nice enough function you can use the properties of the e-function and the logarithm to rewrite it as the exponent of an ordinary integral

So  'product integral' calculus is covered by 'normal' calculus if one can take the exponent of the integral

4/12

## 5/12

The properties of this object justify the name product integral. If you choose f(x)=1 you get 1, for a constant function f(x)=c the product integral returns c^(b-a) just as one would expect

## 7/12

Why is this relevant for physics? 

The product integral was introduced by Volterra to solve a class of differential equations. What the ordinary integral is to y'(x) = a(x) is the product integral to y'(x) = a(x)y(x)

7/12

([tweet](https://x.com/martinmbauer/status/1733589350783336524), 2023-12-09 20:48 UTC)

## 8/12
