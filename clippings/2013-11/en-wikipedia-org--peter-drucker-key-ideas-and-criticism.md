### Key ideas[[edit](http://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Peter_Drucker&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Key ideas")]

Several ideas run through most of Drucker's writings:

- Decentralization and simplification.[[27]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-27) Drucker discounted the [command and control](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Command_and_control_(management) "Command and control (management)") model and asserted that companies work best when they are decentralized. According to Drucker, corporations tend to produce too many products, hire employees they don't need (when a better solution would be [outsourcing](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outsourcing "Outsourcing")), and expand into economic sectors that they should avoid.
- The concept of "Knowledge Worker" in his 1959 book "The Landmarks of Tomorrow".[[28]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-28) Since then, knowledge-based work has become increasingly important in businesses worldwide.
- The prediction of the death of the "Blue Collar" worker.[[29]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-29) A blue collar worker is typically a high school dropout paid middle class wages with all benefits for assembling cars in Detroit. The changing face of the US Auto Industry is a testimony to this prediction.
- The concept of what eventually came to be known as "outsourcing."[[30]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-30) He used the example of front room and a back room of each business: A company should be engaged in only the front room activities that are core to supporting its business. Back room activities should be handed over to other companies, for whom these are the front room activities.
- The importance of the non-profit sector,[[31]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-31) which he calls the third sector (private sector and the Government sector being the first two.) Non-Government Organizations (NGOs) play crucial roles in countries around the world.
- A profound skepticism of [macroeconomic](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroeconomic "Macroeconomic") theory.[[32]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-32) Drucker contended that economists of all schools fail to explain significant aspects of modern economies.
- Respect for the worker. Drucker believed that employees are assets not liabilities. He taught that knowledgeable workers are the essential ingredients of the modern economy. Central to this philosophy is the view that people are an organization's most valuable resource, and that a manager's job is both to prepare people to perform and give them freedom to do so.[[33]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-33)
- A belief in what he called "the sickness of government." Drucker made nonpartisan claims that government is often unable or unwilling to provide new services that people need or want, though he believed that this condition is not inherent to the form of government. The chapter "The Sickness of Government"[[34]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-34) in his book *The Age of Discontinuity* formed the basis of [New Public Management](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Public_Management "New Public Management"),[[35]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-35) a theory of public administration that dominated the discipline in the 1980s and 1990s.
- The need for "planned abandonment." Businesses and governments have a natural human tendency to cling to "yesterday's successes" rather than seeing when they are no longer useful.[[36]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-36)
- A belief that taking action without thinking is the cause of every failure.
- The [need for community](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense_of_community "Sense of community"). Early in his career, Drucker predicted the "end of economic man" and advocated the creation of a "plant community"[[37]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-37) where an individual's social needs could be met. He later acknowledged that the plant community never materialized, and by the 1980s, suggested that volunteering in the nonprofit sector was the key to fostering a healthy society where people found a sense of belonging and civic pride.[[38]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-38)
- The need to manage business by balancing a variety of needs and goals, rather than subordinating an institution to a single value.[[39]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-39)[[40]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-40) This concept of [management by objectives](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Management_by_objectives "Management by objectives") forms the keynote of his 1954 landmark *The Practice of Management*.[[41]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-41)
- A company's primary responsibility is to serve its customers. Profit is not the primary goal, but rather an essential condition for the company's continued existence.[[42]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-42)
- A belief in the notion that great companies could stand among humankind's noblest inventions.[[43]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-43)

## Criticism of Drucker's work[[edit](http://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Peter_Drucker&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Criticism of Drucker's work")]

[C. L. R. James](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._L._R._James "C. L. R. James"), [Raya Dunayevskaya](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raya_Dunayevskaya "Raya Dunayevskaya") and [Grace Lee Boggs](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Lee_Boggs "Grace Lee Boggs") criticised Drucker in their 1950 text *State Capitalism and World Revolution*: "the Christian Humanists (for example, Peter Drucker) will join with the labor bureaucracy to keep the mass of workers in their place at the base of the hierarchy in production."[[44]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-44) *The Wall Street Journal* researched several of his lectures in 1987 and reported that he was sometimes loose with the facts. Drucker was off the mark, for example, when he told an audience that English was the official language for all employees at Japan’s Mitsui trading company. (Drucker’s defense: “I use anecdotes to make a point, not to write history.”) And while he was known for his prescience, he wasn’t always correct in his forecasts. He predicted, for instance, that the nation’s financial center would shift from New York to Washington.[[45]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-45)

Others maintain that one of Drucker’s core concepts—“[management by objectives](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Management_by_objectives "Management by objectives")”—is flawed and has never really been proven to work effectively. Critic Dale Krueger said that the system is difficult to implement, and that companies often wind up overemphasizing control, as opposed to fostering creativity, to meet their goals.[[46]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-46)

Drucker's classic *[Concept of the Corporation](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept_of_the_Corporation "Concept of the Corporation")* criticized [General Motors](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Motors "General Motors") at a time when it was, in some ways, the most successful corporation in the world. Many of GM's executives considered Drucker [persona non grata](http://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/persona_non_grata#Noun "wikt:persona non grata") for a long time afterward. [Alfred P. Sloan](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_P._Sloan "Alfred P. Sloan") refrained from personal hostility toward Drucker, but even Sloan considered Drucker's critiques of GM's management to be "dead wrong".[[47]](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Drucker#cite_note-Drucker_1990_new_foreword-47)
