| |  | | --- | | Hey Thiago Luiz,     "Sorry, I don't have any openings on the development team."   Crap, so much for my "backup plan."   This was all going down a few months after I escaped the Consulting Contract From Hell, the one with the firm in New Jersey.   (They called it a "layoff." I called it "merciful release.")   Since then I'd been beating the bushes to scare up some work without much luck.   One thing I tried during this was applying for a couple of openings at my previous employer, HP.   Both times they brought me in for an interview, and I was frothing at the mouth for the chance to show what I could do.   See, while the micromanaging weenie of a boss I had at my last job thought he was breaking my spirit, inwardly I was seething with rage at the way I was being treated.   And I used all of that pent-up frustration to turn myself into a C++ devouring MONSTER.   I spent every spare minute tearing through books, pounding through puzzles and coding challenges, and building my own "side project" apps.   At the end of those 11 months, I was one of the top-ranked C++ coders in the U.S.—a legit baller.   The interviews at HP were grueling, day-long marathons.   But I swaggered into those conference rooms with my chest out, practically daring my interviewers, "Just TRY and stump me."   I \*aced\* the interviews.   And afterward I felt sure I was a lock for at least ONE of the openings.   Wrong.   A couple weeks later the rejection letters show up.   "Thank you for your participation. The position has been filled by another candidate."   How in the world?!?   (Later I found out: The interviews were a sham. HP had a corporate policy that requires them to interview a certain number of external candidates during the hiring process. The jobs went to INTERNAL candidates who were hand-picked before I even sent in my resume. Yep, I was window dressing. Remember what I said earlier about how you never even get a shot at the \*best\* development jobs?)   The clock was REALLY ticking now—and I was running out of options.   So I made a last-ditch shot at getting back into HP.   I called up my old boss there, a guy named Chuck, on the off chance that he might have something for me.   "Sorry, I don't have any openings on the development team."   Game over.   "But," he continued, "I am looking for a QA lead."   Let me tell you about QA at HP.   HP makes printers, right?   Well, "testing" means putting together these huge matrixes of all kinds of different sizes and shapes of paper... Then printing out test sheets on each of them and visually comparing the outputs, looking for tiny discrepancies that might be bugs in the software.   I can't even begin to say how much I did NOT want that job.   Oh, and the hourly rate was pitiful—it was a big cut from what I'd made at my last job. Which had been a pay cut from the one before...   A lot of developers would have walked away right there.   I almost did myself.   See, there are rules to how this whole game of "getting ahead in your career" works.   A company decides to grow the development team.   HR posts the job.   Developers apply.   The hiring manager interviews candidates...   Etc.   That's just how the game is played.   Except... Except that's total B.S.   The reality is, in business, there are no "rules"—there are only people and deals.   When you put yourself in the right place at the right time with the right offer, well... the "rules" go right out the window.   So instead of turning my nose up at that QA lead position like I wanted to, or accept it and grumble and complain about how miserable I was...   I decided to look at this as the "lucky break" I'd been searching for.   From the moment I walked in the door, I was gunning for a spot on the dev team.   Every morning I'd crank out my test plans, and then I'd spend the afternoon hanging out with the developers...   Pair programming with them, mentoring them, tackling sticky design questions...   I became a "stealth" member of the team.   And before long they realized that they couldn't afford to lose me.   Several of the developers got together and went to our boss to say:   "Look, you HAVE to hire John—there is no way we can hit our deadlines without him!"   And just like that, I was a developer again...   Working in a position that didn't exist—until I \*made\* it exist.    As software developers, we're using to working with constraints and orderly systems.   You can only allocate so much memory, 8 bits to a byte, don't inject user-generated data into your SQL queries.   Predictable rules, cause and effect.   And it's easy to forget that:   When it comes to the world of business there are NO rules, only business propositions.   Now I'm not saying to go out and be a rebel, breaking all the rules on purpose just to "rage against the machine."   What I AM saying, though, is that you should never accept the constraints others put on you "because that's just how it works."   You CAN choose to opt out of the system...   You can find creative, ethical ways to jump to the head of the line.    You can learn to see opportunity where others see only defeat.
