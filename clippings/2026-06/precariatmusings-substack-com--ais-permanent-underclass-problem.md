# AI's permanent underclass problem - by Nigel Bowen

> What happens when the jobs start disappearing at scale?

_"The 'AI job loss' narratives are all fake. AI = massive ramp in productivity = massive ramp in demand = massive jobs boom. Watch."_ — Marc Andreessen, X, 6/4/26

_"jobs doomerism is likely long-term wrong. though of course there will be disruption/significant transition as we switch to new jobs, the jobs of the future may look v different, etc."_ — Sam Altman, X, 2/5/26

I've spent the last 18 months or so attempting to warn people that Silicon Valley is bracing for a permanent underclass. So, I was happy to see this New York Times thinkpiece, entitled "Silicon Valley Is Bracing For A Permanent Underclass," go viral over the last week.

The author, fellow Substacker Jasmine Sun, made many sensible points about the entirely predictable outcomes of automating away jobs.

**First things first – you and everybody you know is screwed**

Sun's opening paragraph: "Most people I know in the A.I. industry think the median person is screwed, and they have no idea what to do about it… While Silicon Valley has long warned about the risk of rogue A.I., it has recently woken up to a more mundane nightmare: one in which many ordinary people lose their economic leverage as their jobs are automated away."

Whether you talk with engineers, venture capitalists, founders or managers, or with doomers, accelerationists, lefties or libertarians, the so-called San Francisco consensus on the impact of A.I. for workers is bleak. Many high-profile tech industry figures – including OpenAI's Altman, Anthropic's Amodei and Grok's Musk – have spent years warning of just such a scenario.

**We've seen this movie before**

The term "underclass" gained currency in the 1960s to describe the factory workers left behind by the postwar automation boom. Today it describes a theory that people have a limited window of time to build wealth before A.I. and robotics are advanced enough to fully replace human labor, after which the rich deploy superintelligent machines to do their bidding while everyone else lives off welfare scraps.

**The inescapable market logic**

Neither China nor the US can allow the other to gain a commanding lead in AI. None of the big AI companies can afford to let their rivals steal a march on them. Any individual business that hesitates to automate away human labour will soon be driven out of the market by competitors. Corporate executives accelerate layoffs and slow hiring because they don't want to be the firm lagging behind — sometimes layoffs happen even before executives know how or whether A.I. will replace those roles.

Tech workers are scrambling for lucrative A.I. jobs in hopes of securing financial freedom, even when they harbor ethical hangups. Steven Adler, a former employee on OpenAI's safety team, said: "People feel like there are not that many opportunities to make money in the future... Even if someone thinks it is personally distasteful to make money from building technology that companies say may literally kill everyone, many people are just cogs in the machine."

**Escaping the inescapable market logic**

A society in which an ever-increasing share of resources funnels to an ever-decreasing proportion of the population is a pre-revolutionary society. There's clearly an anti-AI backlash building in the US and the rest of the Anglosphere.

OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind have set up think tanks on how AI companies can keep making money without creating mass unemployment that results in the companies getting nationalised. OpenAI released a white paper, "Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age," calling for ameliorative measures: a 32-hour workweek, higher taxes on corporations and capital gains, and a "public wealth fund." The document is vague on implementation mechanics and whether OpenAI will advocate the policies listed; an OpenAI spokesperson declined to provide examples of specific legislation the company supports.

**How do the 'Useless Eaters' react?**

Helots, slaves, peasants, serfs, proletarians and other non-elite actors have always had at least a modicum of political power, because they could withdraw their labour. But what happens when elites no longer require that labour?

Anthropic's Amodei: "The balance of power of democracy is premised on the average person having leverage through creating economic value. If that's not present, I think things become kind of scary."

Sun predicts Anthropic's coffers probably won't be emptied in service of public work-force programs unless politicians compel the company to do so; when asked if the Anthropic Institute planned to lobby for redistributive measures, its head described policy advocacy as "the end of a very, very long chain of work."

Summary of the current state:
1. Those most knowledgeable about AI expect it will eliminate a lot of cognitive labour in the near term (1-3 years) and then physical labour in the medium term (3-8 years).
2. No nation, industry or business can take its foot off the AI accelerator lest it get outcompeted.
3. Severe labour market disruption could (kind of) be managed with redistributive policies.
4. Many prominent tech companies and their founders have called for such policies.
5. The tech companies and founders calling for redistribution game tax systems across the globe to make the minimal possible contribution.
6. While the Silicon Valley bigwigs talk a good fight about shifting things in a more Scandinavian direction, there's little evidence they are lobbying to have the policies they putatively support introduced.

Sun wraps up her article with an account of attending a "How to Prepare Our Politics for AGI" event put on by Democratic pollster and strategist David Shor. Shor argues that the AI-enabled automation threat has even constitutionally optimistic Americans considering the possibility they're not just temporarily embarrassed millionaires.

"Right now, the argument is, 'You're all about to lose your jobs, and the choice is either you get nothing and starve, or we do something fair,'" Shor said. "People don't want to be members of the permanent underclass."

Sun observes that AI is now doing to the Professional Managerial Class what a combination of globalisation and automation have been doing to the working class since the 1990s. "The China shock unfolded over several years, whereas this could happen over two years," said Bharat Ramamurti, a former deputy director of the National Economic Council in the Biden White House.

Elsewhere, Sun muses that A.I.'s broad capabilities foster a "rare class solidarity between white-collar and blue-collar workers" — a claim the author is skeptical of, given how the Professional Managerial Class has treated the working class with contempt for decades, particularly during 2014–2024.

Sun suggests that either a restive electorate will force politicians to slam on the brakes or AI will wreak enough labour-market havoc to trigger a business-shuttering, plutocrat-liquidating revolution. There are new proposals across the US for bans on data center construction, self-driving cars, and chatbots for uses like therapy and law. In April, an attacker tried to firebomb Sam Altman's home. In March, Palantir CEO Alex Karp said on a panel with Teamsters president Sean O'Brien: "The biggest challenge to A.I. in this country is political unrest. If I were sitting here in private with my peers, I'd be telling them the country could blow up politically and none of us are going to make any money when the country blows up."
