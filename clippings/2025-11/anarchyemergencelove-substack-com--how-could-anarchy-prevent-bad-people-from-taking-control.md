_Hello, and welcome to Anarchy Unfolds._

_This is the second in a series responding to some excellent comments I got on my post, [May all roads lead to solarpunk](https://anarchyemergencelove.substack.com/p/may-all-roads-lead-to-solarpunk). It can be read as a standalone piece._

We [talked previously](https://anarchyemergencelove.substack.com/p/is-anarchy-compatible-with-modern) about anarchy at scale, and how it wouldn’t resemble the economies of scale we have today. Instead, widespread anarchy would look like many interconnected societies, in communication with each other. People’s needs would be met without keeping count, as a varied and flexible social fabric maintains norms against the accumulation of wealth and power.

All well and good, if we can get there. But Stephen Thier’s second point is about the problem of defectors:

> _This is the old “tragedy of the commons”, “prisoners’ dilemma” and the “free rider problem” stuff. **What happens when someone doesn’t want to follow the “rules” and wants to impose hierarchy again?** Fine back in the Blackfoot days when Mr Colt’s “equaliser” wasn’t as widely available, but firearms and other modern force-multipliers make it was too easy for a small minority to exert disproportionate force. Assuming the universal perfectability of human nature flies in the face of our lived experiences. **Some people are violent, power-hungry, domineering assholes**_**.** \[emphasis mine\]

The first and easiest answer is that anarchy would radically change the social environment, resulting in many fewer bad apples. A free society that provides for everyone’s basic needs would not engender so much interpersonal violence.

This isn’t dodging the question, it’s understanding how we seem to have so many bad people in power today.

In short, we built them a world that facilitates violence. Patriarchy, capitalism, white supremacy — these systems create the culture and conditions necessary for violence on a mass scale. They also catapult the worst people into positions of power, as we’re seeing in real time. In a state of anarchy, none of these systems would continue, thus no would-be tyrant would have the benefits they enjoy today.

It’s important to frame the discussion of violence in this way, because otherwise the assumption is that violence is somehow under control in capitalist society, and that anarchy has to prove it could do the same — keep the lid on Pandora’s box.

To see why that’s patently absurd, let’s get into some particulars.

The tragedy of the commons is a misunderstood myth. A truly common resource is not something exploited for money. In all the accounts I could find, the story about the tragedy of the commons involves individual actors realizing they could gain from over-exploiting a resource everyone depends on. But this is not how people usually act. In reality, well-managed commons have been the sole or primary source of wellbeing for most societies, for most of human history (and prehistory too). There’s nothing stopping us from bringing the commons back.

Well, actually there are several obstacles. First is the widespread ignorance of just how complete the theft and destruction of the commons has already been. From primitive accumulation to manifest destiny, capital has been enclosing the commons for centuries. Private capture of the public commons is the primary method by which capitalist production was able to become the dominant global economic model. The real tragedy of the commons is that they’ve been stolen from us.

Next, the extent to which we are now enmeshed in extractive systems is hard to overstate. It’s incredibly difficult to build alternatives and reclaim communal space when even our vital signs are tracked, privatized, and distributed. Much has been said about “third spaces,” outside the home and the workplace, and the “civic sphere,” alongside the corporate and public sectors, and investing in those is important. At the same time, the depth to which our personal lives have been penetrated by surveilled transactions reaches to the clothes we wear, the food we eat, the appliances in our homes, the devices in our hands, and even to our circadian rhythms. We’ll have to unplug and unravel a whole lot if we want to act in common once again.

The good news is that people are always already doing the work of building, maintaining, and managing the commons. From tenant unions and community land trusts to repair cafes and tool libraries, even in the belly of the beast folks are finding ways to give and receive freely, without keeping score. The more of us contributing to such efforts, the better.

The prisoner’s dilemma is another thought experiment, with very strict guidelines. It’s more useful for studying logic and game theory than it is for talking about real people in real situations.

Like the tragedy of the commons, the prisoner’s dilemma makes remarkable assumptions that would never occur in real life. No communication between the involved parties? Each person is only interested in increasing their gain or decreasing their sentence? This is how you set up a board game, not how you manage the resources a community needs to live, or make decisions about what to say in a court of law. It also erases all context that would make a world of difference in real life. Are the prisoners experienced shoplifters, first-time bank robbers, or political activists? It seems anyone who knows real people in prison and their dilemmas wouldn’t take this seriously.

Then we have “free riders,” the idea that some people take advantage of common or collective goods, and refuse to do their part of the upkeep. These people actually pose very little risk to a freely-managed collective. Unlike in capitalist societies, in a state of anarchy people’s basic needs aren’t behind a paywall. No one demands rent for shelter. No one demands payment for food.

But in a society like ours, free riders pose a huge risk. We are currently suffering from the unimaginable hoarding of a group of freeloaders: the billionaire class. We’re seeing right now the result of letting free riders take advantage of the system and keep for themselves what we’ve spent our lives working for.

Just look at who’s running the country. Some might say Trump, Musk, and their lackeys are an exception to the rule, that they lied and cheated to get ahead while the respectable rich played fair and square. This is blatantly false, but it’s also a misunderstanding of how our systems work. Why is anyone able to lie and cheat and steal their way to the top? Why do we have a top? Because capitalism requires endless growth to continue, because it runs on the profit motive. Everything else, even the rules-based order of democracy, is subservient to that growth imperative.

So really, any change on this front would be an improvement. In a free society, power-hungry people would not have the means to hoard on the scale they do now. The structures would not be in place to support their dizzying accumulation of wealth at our expense. Taking care of people’s basic needs is not a huge drain on anyone’s resources. We only think it is because of propaganda, or because we’re personally responsible for care that feels demanding. Anarchy gives everyone the freedom to meet their obligations without fearing for their livelihood. Anarchy means we take care of each other.

Modern force-multipliers (like guns) do make a difference. But again, we tend to think of this issue upside down. A small minority exerting disproportionate force on the rest of society, which we need to defend against, is our current situation. It’s the government, a group that maintains a monopoly on the use of violence. It’s corporations, groups that claim a monopoly over our time and labor.

In a sense, I think getting into the weeds about weapons is just strategy. Right now the people with guns (the police, the military) more or less support the status quo. They are paid generously to do so. In that context, it should be obvious that pacifism is impractical. We need to find more…. creative ways to resist. Technology can be part of that.

In the other direction, if we’re imagining how an anarchist society would maintain its free status quo against a power-hungry group who wants to bring back a hierarchical system, the same is true: it’s much easier to maintain a status quo then change it. People in free societies would not be used to following orders, they wouldn’t need money for their basic needs, and the social fabric required to maintain functioning community wouldn’t tear easily. People who today are presidents and CEOs and ministers and magistrates would be, in a state of anarchy, just regular people. Achieving that level of freedom in the first place is the real challenge.

Stephen Thier poses questions I think a lot of people have. We’re all aware of the dark side of human nature; we’ve seen it in others, we know it intimately in ourselves. We’ve also been taught our whole lives to trust authority, not each other. We’ve been sorted into marketable demographics and warring tribes. It’s easy to worry that, were the rule of law ever successfully suspended, the same assholes who lord their power over others would start the vicious cycles all over again.

But the truth is that we can trust each other — not because we’re more good than bad, but because regardless of our nature, we have the capacity to organize our society in different ways. A few powerful people, over a long period of time, have organized society to suit their needs, the rest of us be damned. Imagine what the rest of us can do together, even in a short period of time, if we wrest the levers of power from the wealthy few. And the best part is we don’t have to imagine: we already have the solutions, the strategies, the science, the social skills. Every violent system inspires resistance, and the rich history of those traditions are ours for the taking and remaking.

So let’s turn that story about anarchy upside-down: in reality, right now is the best time in the world to be a violent, power-hungry, domineering asshole. Such a person can even get elected president of the United States! In a state of anarchy, such a situation would be unthinkable.

Of course, the road to anarchy from here is long. It requires us to make our own decisions, not defer to authority. It means we need to deal with problems ourselves, not rely on the state. Most of all, it means overturning the systems that put the worst people front and center instead of on the fringes.

I think once we’ve done that it will be more than easy to deal with some stragglers with big egos.
