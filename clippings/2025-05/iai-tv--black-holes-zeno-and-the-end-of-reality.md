# Black holes, Zeno and the end of reality | William Egginton » IAI TV

> ## Excerpt
> Black holes aren't just cosmic oddities—they're cracks in our idea of reality. So argues Johns Hopkins University philosopher William Egginton, whose work explores the connections between Kant, Heisenberg and Borges. He proposes that singularities lurking in Einstein's equations—the mysterious centers of black holes—are echoes of the notorious paradoxes of the ancient philosopher Zeno. Both the singularities and the paradoxes are what the Argentine poet Borges called "crevices of unreason" in the realities we've built, revealing to us, like glitches in the matrix, that our world of space and time is not real. Rather, we have "dreamt the world."

---
Languishing on the killing fields of the First World War, Karl Schwarzschild obsessively worked out the ramifications of his friend Albert Einstein's recently published equations of general relativity. Einstein had redefined gravity so that it was no longer a force acting between distant objects, but a description of the ways massive objects like planets and stars curve the space and time around them. It was Schwarzschild who drew the inescapable conclusion that if enough matter was packed into a small enough portion of space, it would curve spacetime around it to such a degree that the curvature would become infinite, creating what would come to be known years later as a singularity. The novelist Benjamin Labatut memorably depicts this realization, along with the wanton bloodshed of the war, impinging on Schwarzschild's sanity.

Schwarzschild's solution to Einstein's equations led to the idea of a singularity as a "place" (we need scare quotes because place implies space, and space and time become meaningless concepts where singularities are concerned) where the laws of physics no longer apply. It is not merely a 0, an absence, but rather the appearance of a null or infinite value in such a way that it renders the equations unsolvable. The curvature of spacetime that should, as applied to the real world, measure a perceivable force—gravity—swallows everything that crosses its border and tears open the very fabric of spacetime itself.

Black holes are now known to be ubiquitous in the universe, and likely the gravitational centers of most if not all galaxies. While we cannot, by definition, know what it is like to pass the border of no return—the event horizon, at which the curvature of spacetime causes light beams to turn back on themselves—Einstein's equations imply a very curious outcome. To begin with, the timeframes for someone entering a black hole and for someone watching this happen from the outside become completely disengaged from one another. Both would experience time as flowing normally from their own perspectives. But from the vantage point of the external observer's timeframe, the time that it takes for the person who has entered the black hole to approach the singularity at its center will stretch to eternity. This is because from the perspective of those of us on the outside, the nearer something gets to a singularity, the longer it takes to get there: time slows the nearer it gets. Therefore, something can only get closer to a singularity—it can never arrive.

What Schwarzschild had discovered was something like a real-world expression of one of the paradoxes attributed to the philosopher Zeno of Elea, which were supposed to prove that motion—indeed any change—is impossible. In the best-known of these paradoxes, the great warrior Achilles, fleet of foot, agrees to a footrace against a proverbial slowpoke, a tortoise. Given the great difference in their reported abilities to run, the tortoise is granted a head start. And there, Zeno claims, Achilles has already lost—no matter how long the race and how short the head start. The reason he gives for this is a seeming mathematical proof that Achilles will never cross the distance required to catch the tortoise.

To prove this counterintuitive point, Zeno reasons that to catch the tortoise, Achilles must first cross the distance from his starting line to the tortoise's starting line. But no matter how fast Achilles runs, so long as the tortoise is also moving, it will have traversed at least some distance during that time, meaning that when Achilles arrives at the tortoise's starting line, the tortoise has already advanced and is still ahead of him. Now it is merely a question of repeating the same procedure—literally ad infinitum. Every time Achilles arrives where the tortoise was, he has a new, albeit nearer, goal to achieve. But the point is that the goal is always nearer, never achieved.

The Argentine poet Jorge Luis Borges was entranced by this fable. In an essay that reviews various philosophical and mathematical attempts to resolve it, he argues that the most convincing rebuttals of the paradox fail. These rebuttals, Borges posits, are all variations of one suggested by John Stuart Mill, whose solution was inspired by infinitesimal calculus. While it is true that Achilles must cross an infinite number of segments of space to catch the tortoise, the diminishing length of those spaces means he will do so in infinitely decreasing amounts of time, such that, even if the series extends to eternity, "that eternity will not see the end of twelve seconds." In other words, the mathematical progression though space is endless, but since each slice takes a correspondingly smaller amount of time to traverse, the whole series can be traversed in a finite amount of time—twelve seconds, say. Thus, for Mill, the appearance of the problem is created by our failure to understand that the two infinities, that of dividing space and that of dividing time, cancel each other out. Sure, he is saying, Achilles must cross an infinity of segments, but since the amount of time it takes to do so is getting infinitely smaller, that becomes a meaningless barrier to him.

___

While accepting the mathematics, Borges was unimpressed by Mill's conclusion, dryly countering that such "methodical dissolution, that boundless descent into more and more minute precipices, is not really hostile to the problem; imagining it is the problem." The problem, that is to say, emerges from how we integrate or assimilate this mathematical proof into the reality of how we imagine space and time. How do we imagine moving toward something forever? It seems that both parts of this clause, moving toward and forever, cannot co-exist, and hence that imagining their coincidence would be akin to imagining a square circle. Perhaps even more perplexing, Borges appears to decide there is a solution, but one he is enigmatic about: Zeno, he writes, is "incontestable, unless we admit the ideality of space and time." What does this mean?

With the phrase, "the ideality of space and time," Borges is subtly alluding to the great Enlightenment philosopher Immanuel Kant. For Kant, we can never know the world as it is in itself, independent of how our cognition and our senses represent it for us. And the fundamental forms that we use to make the raw sensory data of the world intelligible to us are space and time: we synthesize this data by imposing spatial and temporal dimensions and relations on it, thereby constructing and ordering the everyday entities that we encounter in our experiences. When we do mathematics, for Kant, we are performing a science of these "forms of intuition," and can divide ever further our pure capacity to sequence events, or our capacity to map things in relation to one another. But the operations we perform on these capacities, while largely predictive of the data we receive from the world, tend to fail when we forget they are a science of how we perceive the world and not of the world itself. Thus when we perform operations that approach limits, whether infinite or infinitesimal, the predictive abilities of our mathematical models break down. Zeno is contestable, but only when we realize that his math pertains to the way we know the world, not to how the world actually is.

So how does Kant's position help us resolve Zeno's paradox? If we assume that space and time are fundamental aspects of reality then, as you divide the space that Achilles must cross into ever smaller portions, what you are dividing is real and there is no way to arrive at the destination. Mill's solution, which allows you to arrive at the destination even while crossing infinite segments of space to do so, only works if space and time are fundamentally of a different nature than reality, which is what Kant's approach permits. For Kant, space and time don't inhere in reality but are rather the way beings like us must experience the world. Therefore, what we are dividing when we consider Zeno's scenario isn't reality itself but the way reality appears to us, independent of how it is in itself. Mill's solution to Zeno's paradox does work, but only because it applies to the world as observed by us and not to the world as it is in itself.

I should hasten to add that this resolution of Zeno's paradox is not inherently opposed to what Zeno himself was trying to prove: the unreality of motion. For what is motion if not the change of space over a span of time? If space and time are not fundamental to reality but, as Kant claimed, the necessary form in which creatures like us encounter reality, then the same is true for motion. Mind-independent reality, whatever it is, doesn't undergo change, according to this theory. Or rather, what we call change is a measurable aspect of how we perceive and observe; we can know nothing about the world outside of how we perceive and observe it. Zeno and his master Parmenides thought they knew what the world was: the One, massive and unchanging. Their descendant Plotinus would poetically call time itself nothing but the moving image of eternity, an idea that resonates through the history of Christianity and remains with us today in its visions of heaven and hell. But for Kant and Borges, all we know is that reality, whatever it is, cannot be reduced to how we observe it. This much Zeno shows us.

There is a dramatic consequence for how science can understand black holes. When we talk about black holes, or see pictures of them on the front pages of newspapers, we tend to forget that they are so enigmatic, so inherently incomprehensible, that the very people from whose theories they were first inferred drew back in horror and refused to accept their reality. But perhaps this very fact is a clue as to how we should be thinking about black holes. Despite their ubiquity in the cosmos, we should avoid thinking about them as objects, as things that are locatable within space and time. On the contrary, black holes are, like Zeno's paradoxes themselves, signs that Kant was on the right track when he denied that space and time could really be the fundamental tissue of reality.

To quote Borges one more time, we have "dreamt the world. We have dreamt it as firm, mysterious, visible, ubiquitous in space and durable in time; but in its architecture we have allowed tenuous and eternal crevices of unreason that tell us it is false." Borges says that we find such crevices of unreason in the paradoxes of Zeno and the antinomies of Kant, but it may be that their most mysterious, cataclysmic manifestations are the singularities that sprang forth from Einstein's equations, and that lurk at the heart of the innumerable galaxies whose swirling matter make up the universe we call our home.
