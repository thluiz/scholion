# Why software is a craft [and also an industry]

às
[9:00 AM](http://imho.buzeto.com/2016/01/why-software-is-craft-and-also-industry.html "permanent link")

And we should deal with it.  
  
Before bombs and flames starts dropping on my head, I consider myself a Software Craftsman [and also Engineer] and my conclusion here is that software development is neither pure situational nor completely commanded by rules. The answer relies on context. Let'me tell why.  
  
The inspiration for this article came from a discussion that happened on the [Software Craftsmanship Slack Channel](http://slack.softwarecraftsmanship.org/). [Matthew](https://twitter.com/bnathyuw) posted and excerpt from a [book from Atul Gawande](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/213233.Better)¹. In the quote, the author described how medicine is better off as an industry than a craft, for its patients. If you are pregnant, you want to be sure that your doctor knows what should be done. Finding out that he is discovering his way as it goes, wouldn't be very reassuring. You want a firm assessment, a safe procedure and a happy crying baby as the output. Complications may happen, but you expect your doctor to be able to handle it, or at least to delegate to someone who can.  
  
Bringing the analogy to the software world is easy, since you can relate to the feeling of such parent. You, as a business, want software people to be sure to deliver your product without much surprise. Problems may arise, but tech folks are expected to know how handle it, of find someone who can. You want a somewhat "predictable" outcome, not some artisanal unforeseeable result.  
  
And that's where I think the analogy falls short. You see, software products are very unpredictable. It does not matter if you are working in a very specific domain (like a [CRMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_relationship_management) or [CMSs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_management_system) platform) or a fully customized solution it's very hard to know the specific details right from the beginning. Of course you can have an idea of where you want to arrive at the end. This idea can be from very fuzzy to pretty clear. But the hardships that will happen in between, and the best way to trail your way there hardly are well defined.  
  
But software development is not about despair. We're not adventurers without any idea what to do and where to start.  
  
That's why we have basic rule book that helps us know what to do. Established skills like [Clean Code](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/3735293-clean-code?from_search=true&search_version=service) and [TDD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test-driven_development), methods like [Agile](http://agilemanifesto.org/) and [Lean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lean_software_development), paradigms, frameworks and libraries and many other tools are part of the utility belt of a software person. They help us know the patterns and experiences in the industry, so we can apply on our own situation.  
  
Even though we may not agree which are best for each situation, that's no issue since you probably won't have a single right answer for each scenario that unfolds. Should I use [Rails](http://rubyonrails.org/) or [Django](https://www.djangoproject.com/)? Native App or [Cordova](https://cordova.apache.org/)? [Scrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrum_(software_development)) or [Kanban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanban_(development))? Do I build a mobile app or is a web app enough? Without knowing which to choose, the path is to use what you know, adding pieces of your experience with new experiments and learn the way, fixing things as they appear².  
  
It is clear that software development contains many nuances. As it is made of many parts, some of it may be very well defined, containing known problems where you can follow well established strategies. In other situations you need to step on top of what you have and see how far you can jump ahead. The key, is knowing your context and finding where you are going and why your are doing it.  
  
  
*¹ - If you are not eager to read the book, [here is an article](http://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2006/10/09/the-score#articleBody > p:nth-child(84)) about this topic of the same book.*  
*² - This does not mean you should not plan. You should. But you can't plan everything. In fact, you can't plan most of things that'll happen. But this should not stop you. Just be aware of this fact.*
