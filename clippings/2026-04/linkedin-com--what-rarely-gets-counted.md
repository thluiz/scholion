What Rarely Gets Counted
Rob Wyse

Did you ever have a meeting with a colleague where you both had tears in your eyes?

Not because of a loss. Not because something went wrong. Because something had gone so right... so quietly, so consistently, over so much time that when you finally stood in the same room together, the only honest response was to feel it.

I had that moment twice recently. And I have been thinking about them ever since.

The team I work with is extraordinary. Not because of what we have achieved. Because of how we show up for each other.

The first moment was with a colleague I had worked with every single day for two years before we ever met in person. She is a strong woman, deeply sensitive, fiercely committed, a mother of three, who lives in Tel Aviv and, like so many of my Israeli colleagues, is living and exhausted in a war zone. We built our relationship entirely through screens, across time zones, while she was carrying things I could only begin to imagine.

In January I went to Israel. And I met Dafna Sheinberg Bitman.

We had tears in our eyes.

Two years of daily work. Two years of trust built sentence by sentence, decision by decision, across 6,500 miles and a war. And when we finally stood in the same room, none of that needed to be said. It was just there.

The second was a review with a colleague, Olivia Arruda. We have built something together... real results, real wins, the kind that matter. But that is not why we had tears in our eyes. We had tears in our eyes because in working together we had built something that went beyond work. A trust. A dependence. A genuine love for each other as people. The results were almost beside the point.

I think about those moments when I scroll through LinkedIn and see the posts celebrating metrics and milestones and personal brand moments. The likes. The impressions. The follower counts. None of it is wrong exactly. But none of it is that either.

What gets counted is rarely what counts.

So, what does it actually take to arrive at a moment like that? I have been asking myself that question and here is what I believe.

It takes trust. Not the word, but the practice. Showing up consistently. Doing what you say. Being honest when it is uncomfortable. Trust is not given. It is built, slowly, through a thousand small decisions that nobody sees.

Not every environment works that way. I have been in situations where the opposite was true... where communication was deliberately obscured, where politics mattered more than performance, where a colleague's goal was not to build something together but to protect their own position at someone else's expense. Where the person at the top said the right things but enabled the wrong behavior. You cannot build trust in that environment. You can only survive it. I eventually chose to leave rather than keep paying the price of staying.

I have also worked with people who felt threatened by ideas rather than energized by them. Where a well-reasoned recommendation, one that was demonstrated would work, step by step, was met not with engagement but with resistance. And when the resistance ran out of arguments, the response was not to reconsider. It was to remove the person making the case. That is not leadership. That is fear wearing a title.

The absence of trust does not just slow a team down. It poisons everything. It turns energy inward. It makes people cautious, political, small. And it is always, in my experience, set from the top.

It takes psychological safety. As a leader, that means creating a space where people can be wrong, be uncertain, be human, and do so without fear. Where the culture says: you are safe here. Your ideas are welcome. Your mistakes will not define you. That safety is not soft. It is the condition under which people do their best work and become their fullest selves.

On our team, no question is stupid. But being stupid is not tolerated. There is a code of excellence we hold each other to... not because someone demands it, but because everyone on the team cares too much to settle for less. That combination is rarer than it sounds. Safety without standards produces mediocrity. Standards without safety produce fear. When you have both, you have something extraordinary.

It means never taking credit for someone else's work. Ever. The leader who claims the win and shares the blame is not a leader. The one who shares the win and owns the blame. That person builds something lasting.

And it means protecting each other. Not just when it is easy. When it costs something. When someone is under pressure, under fire, exhausted, or afraid. The colleagues who know you will protect them are the ones who will give you everything they have.

My colleague, Michael Paluszek, is an absolute pro at media relations, making sure everything is letter perfect. Technology is not his strongest suit and he would be the first to tell you. The whole team has his back on that. And he has the whole team's back in return... making sure everything that goes out is exactly right. That is not a transaction. That is how a real team works.

Another colleague, Carolina Lopez, entirely on her own initiative, advanced our work in ways none of us anticipated. She is a brilliant strategic thinker who creates work that makes you reconsider what you thought you knew. Last year she used agentic AI to automate some of our most repetitive tasks, cutting creation time from a week to an hour. Nobody asked her to. She saw what was possible and she made it happen. That is what it looks like when someone holds themselves to a standard.

None of this shows up in a metric. None of it gets likes. You cannot post a screenshot of trust. You cannot measure psychological safety in impressions.

What makes a team extraordinary is not talent alone. It is not results alone. It is what happens between the people when nobody is watching.

And a leader's job, the real job, is to create the conditions for all of it and then get out of the way.

None of this happens in a vacuum. Everything described in this article, the trust, the psychological safety, the autonomy, the culture, is only possible when an organization makes it possible. It takes a leader who gives people room to operate, to make decisions, to be wrong sometimes and learn from it. That choice unlocks something that cannot be mandated or measured. It simply becomes the way the organization breathes. If you are a leader reading this, that foundation is yours to lay. Or not.

But you can feel it in a room when two people meet in person for the first time and have tears in their eyes.

That is the moment that counts. Not the thousand likes.

I hope it helps you think out loud too.
