Everything you know about media is about to change.

For this week's post, I'd like to turn our attention to the media industry — specifically, how it's changed and where it's going. This isn't another think-piece about the streaming wars or the death of cable. This is about something newer and more structural, a seismic shift that's transforming the way we consume information at a global scale. Not streaming. Not AI. Clips.

Yes, clips, those one-minute videos you see every day on your social media feed. Some would simply call this "short-form content," but clips are slightly different. They are specifically snippets of long-form content — things like podcasts, livestreams, and TV shows — a byproduct of something else. Every clip is short-form content, but not all short-form content is a clip.

Our journey into the world of clips starts with an unexpected character: OpenAI. OpenAI acquired popular tech talk show TBPN for 'low hundreds of millions'. The largest podcast deal in history was between Spotify and The Joe Rogan Experience, which reportedly rakes in more than ten million listeners per episode. This TBPN deal is larger than that, and makes TBPN three times more valuable than Dish TV, nearly ten times more valuable than BuzzFeed.

The average TBPN show receives roughly 7,000 views per episode. So why would OpenAI pay $200 million? One word: clips. Despite its relatively small live audience, TBPN reaches hundreds of thousands every day with its clips — the average TBPN clip receives around 257,000 views, thirty-seven times larger than the viewership of the show itself. Unlike most media organizations, TBPN bakes ads directly into their clips ("This clip was brought to you by…"), which enabled TBPN to generate $5 million in revenue last year. The podcast isn't so much a podcast as a vehicle for generating clips.

## Creators → Clippers

TBPN is the tip of the iceberg. Clips have birthed an entire generation of media stars: Nick Fuentes, the new king of the far-right; Hasan Piker, the controversial livestreamer; Clavicular, a viral streamer. All of them have one thing in common: their clips go extremely viral.

Despite a modest livestream audience of less than 20,000, Nick Fuentes reaches more than half a million people on average through his clips; one of his most recent clips reached eleven million people, more than the population of New York City. Clavicular's actual show averages only 16,000 viewers, but his clips average a quarter of a million, and his past ten clips together reached roughly seventy million, more than the population of France. The pattern is roughly the same across all of them — the shows are small, but the clips are massive.

These numbers are actually underestimates, since they reflect only the views of clips posted directly by the creators themselves and don't account for the millions of times those clips are re-clipped by other social media "fan accounts," which often draw even larger audiences.

Clips are no longer the byproduct of the main product — they're the main product. This is the new media economy: "the clip economy."

The clip economy can be gamed. The man who pioneered this is Andrew Tate, who realized that the best way to win the clip economy isn't to post clips yourself, but to have other people post them for you. In 2021, he started an online course (pyramid scheme) called Hustlers University, whose real goal was to get young men to post his clips: subscribers clipped his livestreams and posted them with an affiliate link, earning a commission per new subscriber. Clips of Andrew Tate were viewed over a billion times on TikTok before he was banned, and even after the ban Tate clips still regularly go viral.

The pay-to-clip model has since gone mainstream. "Clipping agencies" are popping up all over the internet. The streamer known as "N3on" recently revealed how much he pays his clippers collectively each month, on a per-view basis, with the best ones making millions of dollars a year. MrBeast launched his own clipping platform, paying clippers to post clips of his theme park, Beast Land. Even in Washington, the last presidential election saw Trump and Harris hire employees to clip their content.

## The Path Back

Legacy media isn't doing well — companies like Disney, Warner Bros. Discovery, and Comcast have all lost more than a third of their value over the past five years. No amount of streaming acquisitions, rebrands, or spinoffs has stopped the bleeding, because people don't watch traditional content anymore, they watch clips.

There's only one path back for legacy media: they have to win the clip economy. The good news is it will be easy, because legacy media is better at original content than anyone, and original content is to clips what light sweet crude is to gasoline — Disney and Warner Bros. are sitting on a clip-field the size of the Permian Basin. The next step, monetization, will also be easy: "This clip of the New Year's Eve Ball Drop was brought to you by Heineken." Legacy media has two options: sell clips, or die.

The point of this post isn't to say the clip economy is a good thing or a bad thing. There is a large body of research suggesting short-form content decreases attention span and increases anxiety and depression, and the clip economy will exacerbate polarization. But the point here is simply to describe where we're headed.
