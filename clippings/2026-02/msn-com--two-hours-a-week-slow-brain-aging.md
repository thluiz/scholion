New research suggests that volunteering can slow cognitive aging. The study author and a brain health expert explain how to get the most out of the effect.

A new study of 30,000 adults found that those who volunteered or offered informal help regularly had 15 to 20 percent less age-related cognitive decline. People who spent around two to four hours a week helping others saw the biggest benefit. Experts say helping in person may be the most potent, as doing so strengthens the social and psychological rewards that help promote cognitive health.

Living a long, physically healthy life isn't enough—you want to be mentally sharp along the way, too. There are many everyday habits that can improve your brain health, but there's one that benefits you as well as those around you: helping others.

Both volunteering and offering informal help to neighbors and friends may slow down cognitive aging, which can impact your ability to think, learn, remember, and make decisions, suggests a new study published in the journal Social Science & Medicine.

Meet the experts: Sae Hwang Han, PhD, is the lead study author and associate professor in the Department of Human Development and Family Sciences at The University of Texas at Austin. Faye Begeti, PhD, is a neuroscientist at Oxford University Hospitals.

What did the study find?
For the study, researchers analyzed data from more than 30,000 U.S. adults over 20 years using data from the Health and Retirement Study.

The researchers discovered that people who volunteered their time or regularly offered less formal help to neighbors, relatives, or friends had 15 percent to 20 percent less cognitive decline linked with aging than those who didn't help others.

When they dove a little deeper into the data, they discovered that people who spent around two to four hours a week giving back saw the biggest benefit.

Why might volunteering be linked with slower cognitive decline?
There are likely a few things going on here. "Volunteering and helping others engage both the brain and body in ways that protect against cognitive aging," says Sae Hwang Han. "These activities provide social connection, a sense of purpose, and often an environment that can be cognitively stimulating—all of which are linked to better cognitive health."

Mental stimulation is paramount, says Faye Begeti. "The key factor to prevent cognitive decline is to continue challenging our brains throughout life," she says. "I often see people who slow down after retirement, and I always encourage them to stay cognitively active. Volunteering is a wonderful way to do that."

Helping others may even reduce stress and inflammation, two factors that are linked with cognitive decline, Han adds.

What kind of volunteering is best?
In-person seems to be the ideal way to go, Han says. Think: offering a hand at a soup kitchen or reading to kids. "Face-to-face engagement likely strengthens the social psychological rewards that help promote cognitive health," he says. "That said, remote or virtual volunteering could still offer benefits if it's cognitively stimulating or provides a meaningful human connection."

And don't forget, you don't necessarily have to sign up for a formal volunteer opportunity—the study found that offering informal help to others was just as effective. So, why not agree to water a neighbor's plants or pick up their mail while they're out of town? Or if cooking is more your thing, making a lasagna for a friend recovering from a surgery could be your way of showing love and support.

Ultimately, Han recommends doing what you can. "Even modest levels of helping—about two to four hours a week—can make a real difference for long-term cognitive health," he says. "There's just robust evidence that around two to four hours a week is the sweet spot where the benefits become most pronounced," Han says.

At the end of the day, a small time investment has potentially large returns, especially compared to how much time many of us spend in front of screens each day, Han says. "The key message is that meaningful engagement with others isn't just good for the community—it's good for your brain."
