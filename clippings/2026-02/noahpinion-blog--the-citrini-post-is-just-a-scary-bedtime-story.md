If you don’t like posts about AI, I have some bad news: For the next few years, there are probably going to be a lot of them. It’s not often one gets to live through an industrial revolution in real time, especially one that moves so quickly. There will be very few pieces of the economy — if any — that this revolution doesn’t touch, and it will have major implications for other things I write about (geopolitics, society, etc.). AI is not going to be a special, compartmentalized topic for a long time; it’s going to be central to a lot of what’s going on. If you find that boring, well, all I can say is, we don’t get to choose the times we live in.

Anyway, today’s post is about the macroeconomics of AI, so that’s fun. I started out writing about macro long ago, and I haven’t really kept up with it in recent years.

Every couple of weeks, someone comes out with a big post about how AI is changing everything, and the post goes viral and everyone talks about it for a few days. A couple of weeks ago it was Matt Shumer’s “[Something Big is Happening](https://shumer.dev/something-big-is-happening)”. This week it’s Citrini Research’s “[THE 2028 GLOBAL INTELLIGENCE CRISIS](https://www.citriniresearch.com/p/2028gic)” (yes, the title is in all caps):

The post paints a picture of a future in which AI disrupts lots of different kinds of white-collar work and service-industry business models in industries like software, finance, business services, and so on, and in which this disruption causes an economic crisis.

This is really two theses in one — a **microeconomic thesis** about which industries and jobs AI will disrupt, and a **macroeconomic thesis** about what this will do to the economy overall. People are debating both. For a counterargument to the idea that AI is about to take all the white-collar jobs, I recommend this post by John Loeber:

And I also recommend [this post from January by Seb Krier](https://aleximas.substack.com/p/853abb61-a5b5-48e5-a04d-23394b6a6536), which of course doesn’t address the Citrini piece, but which does paint a vivid scenario for how humans might still have jobs in the age of AI.

I don’t really have a dog in this microeconomic fight, because, frankly speaking, I don’t have an expert-level understanding of either the industries or the jobs in question. It has been interesting to see the market react to the Citrini post, though. A bunch of [software and finance stocks fell](https://www.wsj.com/finance/stocks/global-stocks-markets-dow-news-02-23-2026-06a32080?gaa_at=eafs&gaa_n=AWEtsqel_-ZFZkBwMhAmAMb45ZdozUIyyIb2mTz8PakGLBnX2Y9wGmKs9AnGuKeajwM%3D&gaa_ts=699cf722&gaa_sig=d1GqOE2C4sgMy-9KZzZEe1G-WWGjiW6e61iKnOCtZzIj9E5ekRF0uF24I-P29yt_omyP0xSvA-S7CElp3xETLQ%3D%3D), including many companies that Citrini mentioned by name:

This is pretty interesting, from a finance perspective. It’s pretty normal these days to see companies’ stocks falling based on news of AI’s disruptive potential — IBM just fell when Anthropic revealed that its AI models can handle COBOL,[1](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/the-citrini-post-is-just-a-scary#footnote-1-188923924) and a bunch of cybersecurity stocks fell a few days ago when Anthropic’s models [found a bunch of security flaws](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-20/cyber-stocks-slide-as-anthropic-unveils-claude-code-security).[2](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/the-citrini-post-is-just-a-scary#footnote-2-188923924) But those were _real announcements of model capabilities_; Citrini’s post was just a _scenario_ for how some current business models could be disrupted.

Was that scenario really _news_? Did none of the analysts tasked with keeping tabs on Visa and Mastercard stock really think about the possibility of AI disruption until a blogger sketched out a sci-fi future mentioning those companies by name? I have my doubts. Instead, this smells more like a wave of sentiment — basically, a bunch of traders read the post, got spooked, and coordinated their panic-selling on the stocks that the post mentioned.

(The inclusion of DoorDash in the stocks that fell suggests this as well. DoorDash is no marvel of software engineering; it was always easily possible to clone the platform even before AI. Its profits are based on a first-mover advantage and network effect, which Claude Code can’t simply conjure up out of nothingness.)

But in any case, time will tell whether Citrini was right about those companies and those business models. I have a lot more to say about the _macroeconomic_ thesis — the idea that the rapid destruction of a bunch of American companies will cause a financial crisis and a recession. Citrini posits an unemployment rate of over 10% — Great Recession levels — as well as a drop in consumption.

Citrini doesn’t use an explicit macroeconomic model, so we can’t really see what assumptions they’re making; it’s not clear how they think the economy works in the first place, so we don’t know exactly _why_ they think the economy crashes. But we can make a couple of guesses. In fact, I see _two_ vaguely plausible ways that an AI-driven service productivity boom could actually end up crashing the economy. Neither one is very likely, though.

#### Failing business models could cause a financial crisis (but it isn’t likely)

Most of the people [trying to figure out what Citrini has in mind](https://x.com/BrianCAlbrecht/status/2025753847587565735) are focusing on AI as a technology shock. We’ll talk about this in the next section, and it actually is the more interesting case, because it would be weird for a productivity boom to hurt the economy. But people seem to be ignoring another, simpler possibility. It’s possible that the sudden disruption of a bunch of business models could cause a _financial crisis_.

This would be similar to what happened in 2008. Back in 2008 there was no big change to technology or productivity or anything “real” in the economy. A bunch of banks and other finance companies just realized that they had sold each other a bunch of housing-backed bonds and bond-like assets that might not be worth what the buyers paid for them. This sudden panic led to a freeze-up in the finance industry, the failure or near-failure of some big banks, and a pullback in lending throughout the economy.

Economists call this a “liquidity preference shock” — it basically means that finance companies suddenly want to hold more cash or Treasury bonds (i.e., liquid assets) and to make fewer loans. There’s a general consensus among macroeconomists that [this was the cause of the Great Recession](https://www.princeton.edu/~mwatson/papers/Stock_Watson_Disentangling_BPEA_2012.pdf), and after 2008, macro models evolved to make this sort of shock more prominent.

Theoretically, you could get something like this again. Suppose that:

1.  A whole bunch of U.S. financial companies have lent a ton of money to software and finance companies, and
    
2.  Suddenly those loans all go bad because of AI disruption.
    

In this scenario, even if AI didn’t hurt the real economy directly, you could get a bunch of bank failures or shadow bank failures that could result in a pullback in lending to the rest of the economy — potentially even to AI itself. All you’d need is a liquidity preference shock, caused by a ton of loans suddenly going bad (or by sudden uncertainty about which loans will go bad).

Citrini is definitely thinking along these lines (note that they write in the past tense because the post is styled as a note from the future):

> Software-backed loans began defaulting in Q3 2027. PE portfolio companies in information services and consulting followed. Several multi-billion dollar LBOs of well-known SaaS companies entered restructuring…The largest ARR-backed loan in history became the largest private credit software default in history…
> 
> Private credit is not 2008 banking. The whole architecture was explicitly designed to avoid forced selling…There are no depositors to run, no repo lines to pull…The system was such that it was supposed to bend, not break…\[But\] over the prior decade, the large alternative asset managers had acquired life insurance companies and turned them into funding vehicles…The losses hit balance sheets built to hold illiquid assets against long-duration obligations…Compared to the banking system, insurance regulators had been docile - even complacent…\[T\]hey began downgrading…these assets. That forced the insurers to either raise capital or sell assets\[.\]

Citrini also envisions a channel through which laid-off workers default on their mortgages en masse, causing a 2008-style mortgage crisis.

In principle, there’s no reason this couldn’t happen. In practice, it doesn’t seem very likely. For one thing, nonfinancial corporations haven’t actually borrowed that much money in recent years. Relative to earnings, their debt is down to the low levels of the 1960s and 1970s:

This lack of leverage means that profits would have to absolutely collapse to cause a big wave of loan defaults.

As for the banking system, it’s [much safer](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/financial-stability-report-20251107.pdf) than it was back before the Great Recession, because new regulations have essentially neutered American banks’ ability to take risks. Corporate loans to companies like Visa and DoorDash are definitely not some kind of secret hidden category of risk; regulators know that these are risky assets, and they assume that they are _not_ “money good”.

As Citrini notes, the insurance system is probably less safe; last year when [I talked about the possibility](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/will-data-centers-crash-the-economy) that a data center crash could cause a financial crisis, I focused on insurance companies and their stake in the private credit system. So an insurance-driven financial crisis is certainly on a lot of people’s minds, including [economists at the Fed](https://www.bostonfed.org/publications/current-policy-perspectives/2025/could-the-growth-of-private-credit-pose-a-risk-to-financial-system-stability.aspx).

But so far, most of the [private credit risk](https://www.wsj.com/finance/investing/u-s-insurers-are-binging-on-private-credit-moodys-says-ee10a41e) seems to be [concentrated among life insurers](https://www.moodys.com/web/en/us/insights/data-stories/private-credit-transforms-life-insurance-industry.html), who are probably less systemically important than other insurers. For the scenario Citrini paints to play out, you’d probably have to have large hidden exposure of banks and shadow banks to private credit via their holdings of life insurers’ debts and stocks. I am skeptical that life insurers are that important to our financial system, unless they’ve secretly transformed into a massive conduit through which the rest of the financial system can make risky corporate loans.

That’s worth worrying about, but regulators and watchdogs have been [sounding the alarm about this for a while](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/imf-raising-alarm-insurance-investments-private-credit), so I doubt that trouble in this sector will catch many people by surprise. So while it’s _possible_ for AI-driven disruption to crash the financial system, it doesn’t seem very likely.

#### Yes, demand collapse is a possible equilibrium

This brings us to the second possibility, and the one that most people are talking about: The possibility that AI might cause economic devastation by _reducing aggregate demand_.

Every time the discussion of AI-driven job destruction comes up, I ask a question along these lines: “If AI is so productive that it puts everyone out of a job, _who’s buying all the stuff that AI produces?_”

One answer to this is that a few super-rich AI owners are buying everything — telling their armies of superintelligent robots to colonize Mars, while normal humans starve. This is [the future of radical inequality](https://philiptrammell.substack.com/p/capital-in-the-22nd-century), which I like to call the “Robot Lords” future. It is a very real worry, and it deserves a much longer post (which I will write soon). But it doesn’t involve an economic crash — just a shifting of demand toward things that don’t benefit many human beings.

This is not what Citrini is envisioning. Instead, Citrini is envisioning a vicious cycle, in which AI productivity creates white-collar unemployment, which reduces aggregate demand, which harms the macroeconomy:

> When these workers lost their jobs, or took 50% pay cuts…the consumption hit was enormous…A 2% decline in white-collar employment translated to something like a 3-4% hit to discretionary consumer spending…By Q2 2027, the economy was in recession.

This is absolutely possible, but the reason this could happen is actually pretty subtle, and not obvious to most of the people who read Citrini’s post. In fact, my PhD advisor, Miles Kimball, [wrote a paper about this back in 1998](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/ifdp/1998/625/ifdp625.pdf) with Susanto Basu and John Fernald, entitled “Are Technology Improvements Contractionary?”.[3](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/the-citrini-post-is-just-a-scary#footnote-3-188923924) Here’s their abstract:

> Yes\[, technology improvements are contractionary\]. We construct a measure of aggregate technology change, controlling for imperfect competition, varying utilization of capital and labor, and aggregation effects. On impact, when technology improves, input use falls sharply, and output may fall slightly. With a lag of several years, inputs return to normal and output rises strongly. These results are inconsistent with frictionless dynamic general equilibrium models, which generally predict that technology improvements are expansionary, with inputs and (especially) output rising immediately. However, the results are consistent with plausible sticky-price models, which predict the results we find: When technology improves, input use generally falls in the short run, and **output itself may also fall**. \[emphasis mine\]

If you think Miles and his coauthors have measured technology levels correctly — and Fernald is generally considered the best in the business when it comes to that measurement — then here is some empirical evidence that when productivity improves, the economy takes a temporary hit.

The reason, though, is subtle: _sticky prices_. Technology improvements are _deflationary_ — they tend to make stuff cheaper. If everything was working as it should, workers would just produce more cheap stuff at cheaper prices with the better technology, and the economy would just get richer. But if prices are sticky and can’t easily fall, then an increase in productivity causes a shortage of goods — the market is going to be glutted, but for whatever reason, companies can’t sell more volume for lower prices. So they end up producing less, and laying off workers.

How realistic this is depends on how sticky you think prices (and wages) are. Economists debate this back and forth, and I won’t recap that debate here. But it’s theoretically possible.

Anyway, in Miles’ paper, a technology boom causes a short, mild recession that pretty quickly rights itself as prices adjust, followed by a surge in growth. But in the wake of 2008, [some economists realized](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/3-Eggertsson-Secular-Stagnation.pdf) that under some very special circumstances, a weakness in demand could become self-perpetuating. The reason is that the economy can get caught in a liquidity trap, due to the zero lower bound on interest rates — effectively, you just shift to a low-growth, low-demand equilibrium for a very long time. This is called “secular stagnation”. The most plausible candidate for secular stagnation was Japan’s 30 years of deflation after its asset bubble burst in 1989.

But that having been said, this almost certainly is _not_ going to happen, for two reasons. First, the secular stagnation model requires for the natural rate of interest to be negative for a long period of time. The natural rate of interest depends on many things, but one important factor is productivity growth; the reason people think Japan was in secular stagnation for three decades is that its productivity growth flatlined. But if AI is creating a huge productivity boom, there is vanishingly little chance that the natural rate of interest is below zero.

Second, even in the incredibly unlikely event that this _did_ happen, governments could just get the economy out of the liquidity trap by deploying fiscal stimulus. In the case of AI, this would just mean that governments borrowed money and either spent it on infrastructure and other public goods, or just mailed people checks — with the bill paid for by future taxes on AI-driven economic activity. In a Keynesian model, this would push the economy away from the zero lower bound, destroy secular stagnation, and restore growth. It’s not exactly rocket science; we did it during Covid, and to some degree during the Great Recession. We know how to do this.

So the kind of demand collapse that Citrini envisions is just not going to happen — at most, a productivity boom could cause a temporary, easily correctable disruption to the economy. It’s not worth worrying about. So if you read the Citrini post and panicked, please sleep a little easier — AI is not going to send the economy into long-term stagnation, and the chances of even a temporary recession due to AI being _too good at its job_ are low.

#### Panicking is easier than modeling

Now, I should note here that there is also a longer-term scenario in which _rising inequality_ causes a shortage of aggregate demand. This is the topic of a very interesting post from January by Alex Imas:

[Alex thinks](https://x.com/alexolegimas/status/2025921845770244371) that this model of his is the same as what Citrini is talking about, but actually it’s not. Alex is an economist, and he understands more about aggregate demand. His model relies on the fact that rich people consume less than middle-class and poor people, so shifting income from the middle class to the rich (i.e., owners and top employees of AI companies) could result in a sustained drop in demand:

> The idea that satiated preferences can impact AGI-driven growth is motivated by an excellent post from [Luis Garicano](https://x.com/lugaricano/status/1938953463724904506): as in the initial example, it is possible that at some point people will feel like they have enough of a product and become a lot less motivated to buy it regardless of how cheap it becomes. If broad automation shifts income from un-satiated high-spending (high MPC) workers to the satiated low-spending (low MPC) capital owners, this can trigger a Keynesian demand collapse that contracts the economy.

This is worth worrying about, and I’ll talk about it more in my upcoming post about AI-driven inequality. The short version is that it’s fixable with increased government redistribution.

But in fact, Imas’ model _isn’t_ what Citrini was imagining. Contra Imas, Citrini thinks that an AI-driven recession would be unusually bad because it would be concentrated among wealthy owners and employees of software and finance businesses:

> In a normal recession, job losses are broadly distributed. Blue-collar and white-collar workers share the pain…The consumption hit is also broadly distributed, and it shows up quickly in the data because lower-income workers have higher marginal propensities to consume…
> 
> In this cycle, the job losses have been concentrated in the upper deciles of the income distribution. They are a relatively small share of total employment, but they drive a wildly disproportionate share of consumer spending. The top 10% of earners account for more than 50% of all consumer spending in the United States. The top 20% account for roughly 65%. These are the people who buy the houses, the cars, the vacations, the restaurant meals, the private school tuition, the home renovations. They are the demand base for the entire consumer discretionary economy.
> 
> When these workers lost their jobs, or took 50% pay cuts to move into available roles, the consumption hit was enormous relative to the number of jobs lost. A 2% decline in white-collar employment translated to something like a 3-4% hit to discretionary consumer spending.

Citrini knows that white-collar workers have a lower marginal propensity to consume. He should realize that this means that drops in white-collar income will actually _cushion_ the blow of any income loss among the upper class.

The point here is that panicking — spinning impressionistic, dire scenarios of economic doom — is easier than creating a coherent mental model of the economy. But if you want to think carefully about how likely doom is, you’ve got to have an idea of what you think will actually _cause_ the doom, so that you can decide how likely you think those causes are to materialize. Citrini’s post is great at painting a vivid, impressionistic picture of an AI-driven economic crash — it combines our memories of 2008 with our fears of technological unemployment. But it ultimately doesn’t tell us much about _why_ that crash might happen, and thus it doesn’t tell us how we might reduce the likelihood of such a crash — or how we might deal with it if it happens. It’s really just a scary bedtime story.
