# Anysphere CEO Michael Truell on Cursor and the race to adopt AI coding | The Verge

> The head of AI coding platform Cursor on what's next for programming, whether AGI is imminent, and competing with Anthropic and OpenAI.

Casey Newton, guest-hosting Decoder while Nilay Patel is on parental leave, interviews Michael Truell, cofounder and CEO of Anysphere, the parent company of Cursor. Cursor is an automated programming platform that integrates with generative AI models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and others to help write code, built into a standard IDE with technology like Cursor Tab, which autocompletes lines of code as you write. Anysphere was cofounded by Truell three years earlier, just after graduating MIT, and is now one of the fastest-growing startups in the post-ChatGPT era.

**What is Cursor?** For nontechnical people, Truell describes it as a souped-up word processor in which engineers build software by editing millions of lines of logic. Cursor works two ways: an autocomplete form factor that predicts a programmer's next set of actions (highly predictable in programming compared to writing), and a delegation mode where users hand off small tasks to Cursor like a pair programmer.

**Origins.** Truell and his cofounders had programmed and worked on AI for years before founding Anysphere. They first worked on computer-aided design (CAD) software for mechanical engineers — an "ill-fitted decision" since none of them were mechanical engineers — before pivoting back to programming, the domain they loved, after concluding existing coding-AI efforts weren't approaching the space with enough ambition.

**GitHub Copilot as inspiration.** Copilot, released about a year before ChatGPT, was the first AI product the founders found genuinely useful — proof it was time to work on these systems in the real world rather than pursue academia.

**Cursor vs. terminal agents (Claude Code, Codex).** Truell says both are useful tools, but Cursor's ambition is a world where programming has changed completely — where you specify minimal intent and the computer fills in the gaps. Cursor invests heavily in the UI layer (the "pane of glass" where programmers work) alongside the underlying model layer, building an ensemble of custom and API models. Their Tab model does over one billion model calls per day.

**On the "wrapper" critique.** Truell argues the wrapper critique is dated — even API-based products can be very deep. Cursor's scale lets them see where AI helps and doesn't, feeding back into model improvement; the Tab model is on its fourth or fifth generation, built with help from Jacob Jackson, who built GitHub Copilot's predecessor TabNine.

**Why programming is well-suited to AI progress.** It's text-based, has abundant open-source training data, and is highly verifiable — you can run code and check the output, which suits reinforcement learning approaches similar to how models were trained to master Go and Dota.

**Productivity gains, but not shorter hours.** Truell estimates real productivity gains (an 8-hour task now taking 5-6 hours in some companies) but doesn't think programmers are working fewer hours — there's too much elasticity in the amount of software work available, especially at companies like Salesforce with tens of millions of lines of existing logic.

**Vibe coding.** Not Cursor's main use case or revenue driver, but Truell sees it growing — split between an "entertainment" bucket (personal enjoyment, hobbies) and a "professional" bucket (designers doing prototypes, non-coders contributing small patches). He believes most use cases will still be served by the roughly 5% of people who deeply care about building tools, even as access broadens.

**Path to full automation.** Truell estimates the field could automate more than half of programming as it exists today within a year in the bull case, but cites real blockers: continual learning (models don't persist new information the way human memory does — candidate solutions are longer context windows or retraining), multimodal capability (playing with software, clicking buttons, using GUI tools like Datadog), and models working coherently over "weeks" of human-equivalent time rather than hours.

**Company size and culture.** Anysphere is roughly 150 people, aiming to stay nimble (maybe capping around 2,000, similar in scale to The New York Times). Truell spends significant time on hiring, particularly individual contributors, believing the best ICs disengaging from a company is how tech companies die. The culture is process-skeptical and hierarchy-skeptical, intellectually honest and curious.

**On Meta's hiring spree and OpenAI's acquisition interest.** Truell says Meta's recent hiring push hasn't made recruiting noticeably harder, and declines to comment on reported early acquisition talks with OpenAI, calling Cursor "life's work territory." Two former Claude Code leads (Cat Wu and Boris Cherny) who joined Cursor left after a couple of weeks to return to Anthropic; Truell says they still had "a lot left to do" on Claude Code, which is their creation.

**Pricing controversy.** Cursor's July 2025 shift from a request-based subscription allotment to a compute-based usage model drew user backlash after people ran over limits without realizing it. Truell attributes this to poor communication of the underlying shift (AI now working for much longer per request, changing costs) rather than the pricing model itself. He says the vast majority of $20 Pro plan users don't hit their monthly limits.

**On AGI timelines.** Truell places himself in a "messy middle" — expects it to take decades but still be a transformational technological shift, skeptical of a single big training run producing a "machine god." He points to the low rate of genuinely new consequential ideas in machine learning over the past decade and a half despite fast overall progress.

**Two-year outlook.** Truell hopes to build an experience for orchestrating work delegated to fast AI helpers, and expects programming languages to evolve to become higher-level and more compressed (hundreds of thousands of lines instead of millions) — a way to look at, point to, and edit exactly how software works at a fine-grained level, since he doesn't think chat-box-only interfaces can be the end state for professional software.
