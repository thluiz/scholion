What to Know About Money in Your Late 30s and 40s

Your 20s are behind you and now you’ve got a spouse, kids and maybe even a home to show for it. Ideally, you’ve [saved a bit for retirement](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/how-much-should-you-have-saved-at-every-age-1821498621), you have a [debt repayment plan](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/what-20-somethings-need-to-know-about-getting-their-fin-1826004711) that works for you and you’ve been ramping up your savings over the years to [accomplish your financial goals](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/whats-your-2018-money-goal-1821064068). You’re comfortable, or getting there at least, but you still want to make sure you’re not missing anything from your financial life. There’s still much more to learn.

This is the third article in TwoCents’s latest series, What to Know About Money at Every Age. You can also read about [what you should know before you’re 20](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/what-to-know-about-money-before-youre-20-1828189748) and [how to manage your money in your 20s and 30s](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/what-to-know-about-money-in-your-20s-and-early-30s-1828308837). Stay tuned for more tips for your 50s, 60s and beyond. 

Here’s what to know about money as you leave you enter into the second half of your career.

Keep Investing in Yourself

Statistically these are your [peak earning years](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/08/17/when-youll-earn-the-most-in-your-career.html), but that doesn’t mean that you’re guaranteed to make increasingly more money. You need to continue to invest in yourself and your skill set so you remain attractive in the job market.

If you’re in your mid-30s or early 40s, your career could span another 25-30 years. Really consider what you’ll want to do in that time, and what skills you’ll need to do it.

Think: Are you surrounded by people smarter than you, who you can learn from? Could you use your skills in a range of jobs and professions? Do you push yourself beyond your comfort zone? Do you participate in meaningful work, projects, volunteering, etc.? Are you still energized at work? If you answered no to many of those questions, you have plenty of time to fix that and accomplish your long-term career goals.

There are plenty of ways to gain more skills, if that’s what you’re missing, some of which are pretty cheap. Your local community colleges and universities likely offer continuing education courses, and there are a number of workshops you can find online at places like SkillShare.

Remember to Network

For many workers, new jobs and opportunities come from who you know, rather than what your cover letter says, particularly in your mid- to late-career. So keep up your network of old colleagues and work acquaintances. You never know who could help you out in the future.

And as we discussed in an earlier installment of this series, this can be especially true for women if they leave the workforce to care for children. Monica Dwyer, a [Certified Financial Planner](http://www.harvestadvisors.com/) and wealth advisor, says a lot changed in the 10 years she stayed home with her children, and she wished she had kept in closer touch with people in her industry.

“When I went back to work, I wasn’t able to slip back into the director-level position that I left, so I had to start over,” says Dwyer. “I was naïve enough to think that I could just go back to doing what I was successfully doing before, but no one wanted to take me at that level since I no longer had a track record.”

“I do not regret spending that time with my children during their most vulnerable years,” she adds, “but I do regret not keeping my toe in the water with former colleagues.”

Supercharge Your Investments

You’re earning more money, and now’s the time to try to contribute the max to your retirement accounts and [focus on fees, not returns](http://time.com/money/collection-post/3425819/4-ways-smarter-investor/). These are crucial years in the buildup to retirement (or, more simply, [financial freedom](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/save-for-freedom-not-retirement-1828384520))—you can still catch up if you didn’t make a habit of investing at the beginning of your career.

Consider being a bit more bullish than you might normally be, especially in your 30s—if you’re retiring around 65, you still have 20 to 30 years to invest. That’s a pretty long time horizon to rebound from any dips (but as always, invest according to [your risk tolerance](https://lifehacker.com/determine-your-risk-tolerance-level-before-investing-1599228268)). People who are wary of stocks because of the financial crisis or the early-2000s dot com bust “may be missing out on potentially higher returns afforded by stocks over the course of the next two to three decades,” says Kevin Ta, Senior Wealth Strategist at [PNC Wealth Management](https://www.pnc.com/en/personal-banking/wealth-management/pnc-wealth-management.html). “Those more conservative investments in money market, CDs, treasuries and bonds may not keep up with the rising costs of goods over time.”

Robo-advisors like Wealthfront and Betterment offer [asset allocation advice at low rates](https://www.nerdwallet.com/blog/investing/wealthfront-vs-betterment/), as does Vanguard’s [Personal Advisor Service](https://www.nerdwallet.com/blog/investing/vanguard-personal-advisor-services-review/). Above all, make sure you’re investing for the longterm in a diverse array of low-cost options. (And [keep tabs on old retirement accounts](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/keep-tabs-on-old-retirement-accounts-1825712142).) Don’t [forget about dividends](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/a-beginner-s-guide-to-dividend-investing-1822211017).

If you’re juggling demands, the usual thinking is that you can borrow for college and a mortgage, but not for a better retirement, so you should prioritize yourself. That’s not incorrect, but remember that once you hit 50, you can start making catch-up contributions to your retirement accounts (that’s an extra [$6,000 for your 401(k)](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-catch-up-contributions) this year and [$1,000 more in your IRA](https://www.rothira.com/roth-ira-contribution-limits)). So diverting some money to a 529 or down payment could be a prudent move, if you’re able to contribute to all of them. Sitting down with [financial planner](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/what-to-look-for-in-a-financial-advisor-1821869335) could help you gain clarity on what makes sense for you and your family.

[LearnVest suggests](https://learnvest.com/) goal-oriented investing, meaning “every investment has a purpose or goal associated with it, enabling you to invest each account according to your time horizon and your risk tolerance for each goal.”

It’s also a good time to open a Roth, if you haven’t yet and you’re not over the earnings limits (of course, there’s always the [backdoor option](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/what-to-know-about-roth-ira-conversions-1825654230)). Because you contribute to this investment vehicle post-tax, it will help to minimize your tax headache in retirement. You may also consider opening a [taxable investment account](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/how-to-invest-beyond-your-401-k-or-ira-1822600075), which you can use to offset costs in your first few years of retirement while your tax-deferred accounts (401(k)s and IRAs) keep growing.

Pay Off Your Debt

You don’t want to go into retirement with debt hanging over your head, although an increasing number of people do. Experts say you should be close to paying off your student loans at the tail-end of your 30s, but if you’re not, there’s no need to panic. Reassess your repayment plan and make changes as needed.

At the very least, you don’t want to be adding to your debt once you’re nearing 50, even if that means contributing less to your investments. Debt pay-off should be a top priority.

Get a Handle on Your Health Care Costs

Remember that you are your most valuable asset. Take care of yourself, and pay attention to your health. You’re not a kid anymore: Take advantage of [“free” preventive services](https://vitals.lifehacker.com/all-the-free-health-care-you-can-get-without-using-your-1776252651#_ga=2.50597098.2105579250.1534164266-594046802.1524762060) each year . As I [wrote here](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/boost-your-retirement-fund-with-these-minor-lifestyle-c-1826082093), simply following your doctor’s orders and taking prescriptions as described can save you a bundle.

If you have a Health Savings Account at work, use it to build up funds in a tax-efficient way. Contributions to HSAs are deducted from your taxable income when they’re made; the investment earnings aren’t taxed; and the money you withdraw to use for qualified medical expenses isn’t taxed. It’s a win-win-win. But watch out: These are paired with [high-deductible health plans](https://vitals.lifehacker.com/how-to-survive-a-high-deductible-health-insurance-plan-1792436777), which can be prohibitively expensive. You may find that a plan with lower out-of-pocket costs and a [Flexible Spending Account](https://lifehacker.com/5855970/how-to-use-your-flexible-spending-account-to-save-money-and-avoid-wasting-benefits) is right for you.

Otherwise, familiarize yourself with your insurance’s terms:

- Deductible: How much you will have to pay out-of-pocket before your insurance kicks in (this doesn’t include preventive services, which you are entitled to without a co-pay or any other charge). The [average HDHP’s deductible](https://healthpayerintelligence.com/news/consumer-directed-care-high-deductibles-popular-for-employees) was $4,133 per year for family coverage and $2,166 for single coverage in 2018, while PPO deductibles averaged $2,198 per year for family coverage and $1,012 for single coverage.
- Copay/Coinsurance: A share of the cost of a visit or prescription even after the deductible is met. A copay is a fixed amount (like $10 for a visit) and coinsurance is a percentage of the services you received.
- Out-of-Pocket Maximum: How much you will have to pay for in-network services before insurance picks everything else up.

Keep an eye on in-network and [out-of-network providers and facilities](https://www.brookings.edu/blog/usc-brookings-schaeffer-on-health-policy/2017/08/08/receive-a-surprise-medical-bill-here-are-three-federal-actions-that-may-address-surprise-bills/). In some cases, you won’t be able to help whether an out-of-network doctor sees you, [particularly in the ER](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/brief/an-analysis-of-out-of-network-claims-in-large-employer-health-plans/) (oh, the joys of the American health care system!). But for routine procedures, surgeries, etc., make sure your doctor and the hospital or clinic is in your insurance network, suggests [NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/blog/health/provider-network-best-bet-saving-money-health-care/):

- Go online to your insurer’s physician locator or directory tool, enter your plan information and search for your physician. This should tell you if the doctor you want to see not only accepts your insurance but is part of your plan’s network.
- Verify your findings with the insurance company by phone, ensuring that the physician’s name and location match the details your insurer has. In some cases, providers work out of multiple locations that aren’t covered in the same network or two physicians have similar names.
- When scheduling your appointment, confirm that the office participates in your plan and doesn’t merely “accept” your insurance. Be sure to ask them specifically: “Does every provider I might see at your facility participate in this insurance plan?”﻿

If you don’t have employer-sponsored insurance and are shopping around on the individual market, “look at the deductibles and co-pays, the services offered, any limits on the doctors that you can visit and what additional benefits the plans offer,” says Lisa Hayes, Senior Wealth Strategist at [PNC Wealth Management](https://www.pnc.com/en/personal-banking/wealth-management/pnc-wealth-management.html). “Depending on your income, you may qualify for a subsidy from a state plan, but only if you choose” the [silver-level plan](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/the-health-care-terms-you-need-to-know-1828084354).

And remember to p[lan your visits with care](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/how-to-maximize-your-high-deductible-health-plan-1827453032#_ga=2.51971690.2105579250.1534164266-594046802.1524762060).

Teach Your Kids the Value of a Dollar

As you know by now, living means making sacrifices. Some things will be prioritized over others, though you might not be explaining to your kids why you’re making the decisions you’re making. But doing so can be a great way to start teaching them about money and being responsible with their finances. (Also, they’ll hopefully start to understand why you can’t buy them every toy or on-trend clothing item they want at any given time.)

Talk to Your Parents About Their Finances

This is one of two potentially uncomfortable financial tasks you’ll want to get a handle on before it’s too late. Talk to your parents about their finances, and see what shape they’re in in terms of their retirement, health care costs, house, etc.

To do this as painlessly as possible, as Kathleen Burns Kingsbury, a wealth psychology expert, told me for [this article](https://twocents.lifehacker.com/how-to-handle-loaning-money-to-your-parents-1824092037), you want to try to take emotion out of the conversation, while allowing them to feel in control.

“You want to make sure that you allow them to feel in control, to maintain some dignity,” she says. “Sometimes you ask your parents and they’re actually relieved and you have a conversation.”

As I wrote before, here’s what Kingsbury suggests:

- First, feel your feelings. “We can feel all different sorts of emotions about our parents getting older, so you can talk to a friend or a counselor, and by doing that first, by the time you approach your parent you’ve thought it through.”
- Then, lead with loving intent. “Let’s say you’re frustrated and scared, and you walk in and you’re not prepared—no one’s going to be receptive,” she says. “So plan...say you’re starting to get concerned and you’d love to help them.”
- Finally, give them space and time to decide. “You may have been thinking about it for three months, but it may be new to our parents,” she says. “Sometimes parents just need a little time to think about it.”﻿

Make a Will

Bleak? Sure. But should anything happen to you, your family will be grateful you had a plan in place, and you can make sure your assets are allocated how you’d like them to be. This doesn’t have to be an expensive process either; you can make one online at a place like [LegalZoom](https://www.legalzoom.com/sem/index.html) for under $100.

You’ll want to consider these questions specifically, per [Money Magazine](http://time.com/money/collection-post/2791069/10-steps-to-painless-estate-planning/):

- Whom do you want to inherit your assets?
- Whom do you want to name as guardians for your children in the event that you and their other parent dies?
- Whom do you want responsible for executing your will?
- Whom do you want handling your financial affairs if you’re ever incapacitated? Whom do you want making medical decisions for you if you become unable to make them yourself?﻿

At the very least, you and your spouse should be on the same page about your finances. You should know how much the other makes, how the bills are paid, what the investment accounts are, etc. Open communication is key, and can make any potential disaster slightly easier to bear.

And look into life insurance options (likely term life insurance, not whole), especially if you have children. In [Get a Financial Life](https://www.amazon.com/Get-Financial-Life-Personal-Twenties/dp/1476782385?ascsubtag=218a447fc994f62af9738cfd4d98049431a238b1&tag=lifehackeramzn-20), personal finance expert Beth Kobliner suggests shopping around on “individual, direct-to-consumer firms like TIAA and Ameritas,” as well as on comparison sites like Term4Sale.com.

“One rough rule of thumb says you should buy a policy that will pay seven to ten times your annual before-tax income if you die, but many people need more than that,” writes Kobliner.

Enjoy Yourself

Now that you’ve had the tough conversations and spent a lot of time focused on your goals, goals, goals, it’s time to just enjoy yourself. Not every dollar needs to be maximized or squirreled away for a rainy day. Go on vacations with your family, take off work early to go to your daughter’s soccer game and splurge on a few nice meals out every now and then.

Avoiding the “must haves,” like a big house and nice car, can help here. If you don’t want a lavish wedding or a house in the suburbs, embrace that. Spend your money on things that are important to you and your spouse.

That might even mean taking a mini-retirement (or, you know, some time off) now, rather than waiting until your 60s and 70s. You’ve put in the work, and you deserve to enjoy yourself. (For some inspiration, check out [this subreddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/financialindependence/).)
