Question (score 18, asked Nov 3, tag: research-process)

To me, it is one thing to know that you don't know something. For example, you read some paper and it says "we used method you have never heard of to obtain the result". Then you can look up the method you have never heard of and try to read more about it.

But the more serious issue for me is when I don't even know that I am missing important information.

Think for example thinking that the only method of transportation is to run somewhere, never having heard of a car, you might become the best person in the entire world at running, but even a medicore person who has heard of cars will outdo you in transportation.

It's a bit of a vague question but I feel like this applies to really anything in academia, and it's very annoying to discover you went in depth and tried your best, at something that had a much simpler and easier solution if you had only heard of it. So, not just a question on "how to deal with wasting time on an approach that ended up not working out"...

Answer 1 (JF Meier, score 18)

The problem you describe is real and it is mostly an issue of isolation.

The best methods are:

Talk to as many senior people about the problem as possible.

Try to read across many books and papers to see what other people find simple or difficult.

Answer 2 (Christian Hennig, score 6)

It's good that you are aware that most likely there is stuff relevant to you that you haven't heard of. You can do no better than taking steps to try to find out about it.

I have a list of important journals in my field where when I was younger (unfortunately this has become much more patchy - it was for sure a good thing) I'd regularly go through the tables of content and have at least a quick look at all the papers that treated stuff related to my research or of potential interest (depending on time of course). The list needs to be updated from time to time. I know somebody else who gets (or at least got, at some point) a daily list of new papers in their field on arxiv. Chances are even somewhat older important ideas and developments in your research area are mentioned in recent papers. There are also review papers, often a good resource. I would occasionally also follow older references in recent papers. Conferences and seminars offer similar opportunities - use them to learn! Be curious!

With some experience you may also get a feel for things that probably exist even if you haven't come across them yet. If you're facing a problem, ask yourself how you'd go on about solving it and whether existing literature makes it likely that somebody else may have had this idea already. Furthermore, with some knowledge of the literature, ask whether something you have already seen could somehow be applied to the problem you're thinking about and whether it seems "natural" that somebody else may have had this thought before. If so, try looking specifically for it (this may well involve asking AI).

Answer 3 (Allure, score 5)

Ask an AI. They have read far more books/papers etc. than you ever could. Try questions like "is there another way to do this?" or "this is what my goal is, what is the best approach?". Be sure to critically think about their output. It is possible they hallucinate, but you can ask them for sources.

- Anyon: I think that really depends on how you draw the Rumsfeldian line between known unknowns and unknown unknowns. LLMs seem to be quite useful for open-ended literature search questions like "is there another way to do this?" or "has something similar been done in another field with different terminology?". I.e. imaginable possibilities can be known unknowns. You may have to wade through a lot of irrelevant stuff, but the fact that it's less constrained than typical keyword searches is a positive. Of course, neither approach can demonstrate that there aren't additional unknown unknowns.
