This is my wild prediction for the job market from now on. I think there will be 2 things happening at the same time.

**First**, hiring as we know it today will eventually collapse. I think the time is already ticking and we’re already seeing strong signals. Companies cannot find the candidates, and candidates cannot find the jobs. For me, that already sounds like hiring doesn’t work.

**Second**, we will see less jobs, but only because it takes time until all kinds of other businesses actually need a person who comes in with AI and improves the business. So I think there will be a redistribution of software engineers. Big companies needed a lot of engineers, they will not need so many anymore, and they will continue laying them off. But AI and this technology will become more and more accessible over time, and this workforce will be redistributed to smaller and smaller businesses, for them to actually survive. Any personally owned shop, a jewellery store or whatever, might actually have a developer who does something specifically for them. More software engineers will become self-employed, solving problems for different businesses using AI. I think this path will last a bit longer, but eventually I think even there they will not be needed so much.

Elizabeth Stone runs product and technology at Netflix. This summer Lenny Rachitsky asked her on his podcast what she’s hiring more of now. Her answer:

> “We need more systems thinkers.”

And what she’s looking for less:

> “The days of very narrow, deep specialization feel more limited to me.”

All these skills that they want in companies are getting you a job. They also are getting you out of a need to have one.

And I think you will need that.

Elizabeth also gave a small trick for how to learn systems thinking:

> “Each problem you’re trying to solve, step out one click to, what am I assuming is true about the broader space.”

What are the things I’m assuming are true, and do they really need to be true? This is a really profound question, because it’s a questioning of everything. I’ve seen in many organizations this being done right, and I’ve seen it being done very, very poorly.

Leadership would often have this mindset of questioning everything, and the less experienced ones would say it without a lot of examples or context. They would just say, question everything!

Then there are engineers who come in and they will basically be terrorizing every decision. What they hear is, leadership made a decision, and now I have to give them a hard time to explain to me why they made it, and I will come up with an extreme amount of edge cases just to find this 1 hole where they might have taken the wrong assumption. Like taking a stick and poking it until it breaks. I have seen this often, even in interviews.

When this takes some momentum in an organization, it becomes some kind of mental masturbation about who can come up with more extreme edge cases and make it waterproof. And of course sometimes they create these waterproof solutions, and they’re just not marketable, or unrealistic in some other way.

Elizabeth said it herself: “I wouldn’t spend too long in the questioning state because then you’re stuck.”

The systems thinkers I found understood what this question really means. It’s more in the direction of: _I’m trying to understand why_.

One of them was a software engineer I promoted to EM in 6 months. That’s the kind of behaviour that gives you a fast track promotion.

We had a legal opt-in. The lawyers had been aware of the legal risk for a long time, but so far everybody took that risk. Then there was a refresh of leadership, they looked at some numbers, there were laws coming into effect, and they suddenly had a different appetite. It became a priority, and it needed to be done in a week or so. Impossible, right?

Most engineers said you cannot do it like this. They adopted this mindset of questioning: why do we need it? We didn’t need it for a long time, so 1 more week, what does it matter? They were poking.

And he comes in and looks at what’s the outcome. The outcome is that we want this in the shortest possible amount of time. I commit to the decision that this takes higher priority than everything, so you have to be fine that I’m dropping the other work. We need to reliably track whether they actually clicked or not. It needs to show up on every device, on every version, and we need to release it simultaneously. Plus we have this other system that we had just recently developed that lets us show pop-ups remotely from the backend. Okay, so this is what we can reuse. We just need to tweak it instead of recreating the apps and so this gives us the benefit that we can take it down if it doesn’t work, with 1 line of code.

Before, it was more a conversation about who should do it and who should not, because now we have other things to do and we have plans, and the lawyers cannot get hold of anybody. Long exchanges of emails, and no owner. And this person comes in and says, let me just fully understand, first of all, what you want. Let’s sit down and write it up. Tell me what actually needs to happen. Because the lawyers would come in and say we need this text on the screen. Okay, but details matter. Is it for logged-in users? Is it also for anonymous users? Oh no, not anonymous users. Suddenly it gets more concrete, the solution becomes focused, and suddenly it’s not such a big deal.

He took away many of the assumptions that many developers were taking, and it was actually 1 week of work for 2 people, because finally we understood what we needed to do. We deployed it. On a day before Xmas when some numbers came in and there was a bug, he was even able to revert it.

The systems thinker disassembles the entire system, understands it, throws away half of the things, and also knows how to react to change. This flexibility, this full understanding instead of bureaucracy, is what I think makes a huge difference.

Mo Gawdat, the former Chief Business Officer of Google X, is building an AI app that helps people find and keep love. In “Ex-Google Exec: How to Position Yourself Now Before the Next AI Phase (2026–2027) | Mo Gawdat“ (spring 2026) interview he talked about how a very small team of very senior engineers rebuilt the entire app 4 times in a month. It’s such a luxury to be able to do this today.

I don’t think most engineers would do this. They would start, fixate on a specific idea and just keep building, making it better and making assumptions.

Psychologists have a name for this, the Einstellung effect. You stick with the solution you already know, even when a better one is right in front of you.

Why did they pivot 4 times? I think they found out 4 times, or 3 at least, that it somehow was not solving the right problem in the right way. They want to solve this relationship problem, make better matches. They are prepared to throw away the entire application if it doesn’t serve the purpose, instead of fixating on the solution and almost just hoping. Because for engineers, the problem is always the users, not that the thing they’re building is wrong.

If you ask engineers how to get a job right now, it’s a numbers game. Be a little bit lucky, sure, but also be the most senior engineer with FAANG experience, be the best at LeetCode, have an edge in some framework, be the first developer working with something. And if you don’t get it, you find the reason: it’s the 1 thing you’re missing. There will always be whatever is missing for you to be the “complete engineer”.

He won on this 1 particular thing: he’s into research, he’s studying it, and that’s what they need. You need to match your strengths.

So yes, you can still get a job. And the same skills are what you’ll need when hiring stops working the way it does now.

I have done the opposite. With side projects, I would create this app, and then I thought I’m going to compete on price. If I just make it cheaper and cheaper and cheaper, it must be that it’s too expensive. I didn’t feel good about charging much.

That’s the problematic mindset. It’s exactly like, oh, I would take any job. That’s the problem. Not any job, not a lower price. A particular job and a higher price, because it’s very focused, very concentrated. Concentrated things have higher value than things spread through everything. Nobody needs a generic app that does most of the things badly, they need a very focused one. Besides, the app itself is anyway not enough anymore.

When I started helping software engineers, I thought this is a general thing. I can help any engineer who’s trying to get a job and cannot get one. I can identify them very quickly, they have the #OPENTOWORK badge. And then I found out there are people who have had it open for a year.

They’re really struggling, because the longer they’re open without a job, they’re making up all kinds of stuff they’re doing, instead of actually building their projects and trying to see, or contributing to known open source repositories. Those are obviously the best things they can be doing. Instead they turn sour and they rant all over LinkedIn, and it really works against them. I would not want to hire someone who’s coming with this mindset even though as candidate I have been there more often than people I know and understand the insane level of frustration. It just doesn’t help you sell your skills.

Then I thought the best would be engineers who have a job and are looking for a better one, because they obviously have income to pay. But there’s no urgency for them to buy. They have not felt the pain of not having a job and running out of money.

I had a conversation with IT support guy who told me he realized he’s in this codependent relationship with his job. It’s semi-toxic, but not painful enough for him to go through the big pain of looking for a new job, so he rather stays there. And then, totally exhausted at the end of the day, he still pushes himself to work on some app on the side, hoping that one day he can end his job and he doesn’t have to go through this stupid hiring process again.

But he’s just building, because he has energy for that, that’s what he knows. He doesn’t have a lot of time to think about it because he’s so drained. He has no urgency, because it’s not bad enough, and he’s investing his time in the wrong thing, which is just building. That is not going to give you any competitive advantage. Knowing how to find out if you’re solving the right problem, and proving that you can sell it before you even start building, that is. He’s in this locked state.

Psychologists have a name for this too, self-handicapping. You set up the obstacle yourself, so if it doesn’t work out, you have your excuse.

I had 4 failed startups because every time I was just building instead of trying to sell it.

```
Do you have a job?
│
├── No → How long until rent and bills hurt?
│        ├── A couple of months → Get a job. Spear, not net. No automation.
│        ├── Some traction → Explore the weak signal before anything else.
│        └── Long runway → The dangerous one. Come back when it runs out.
│
└── Yes → How much energy at the end of the day?
         ├── Zero → Change the job, the role or the level. Get help.
         ├── Energy + happy → Enjoy it. A layoff is still coming.
         ├── Energy + not happy → Put it in the right direction, before the layoff.
         └── Time + energy → Stop the hobby projects. Find out if it sells.
```

**If you don’t have a job,** ask yourself how long you have before you start struggling to pay rent and bills.

**A couple of months.** No audience, you’re unknown and you don’t have any real traction. Then I really recommend getting a job as soon as possible. The longer you’re out of a job, the worse everything gets for you, and the gap on your resume keeps extending. People underestimate how important it is to get to the next job as soon as possible in this market. That means you should not automate your way in. Your targeting needs to be very, very sharp and precise, because you’re competing against the wave of layoffs that are coming as well.

**Some traction.** It means you have some weak signal. Instead of dismissing it because there’s not enough data, you should really explore it.

**A long runway.** That’s the danger, because you feel like, okay, this might be working, I just need to make it better. The runway gives you a cushion, it gives you all kinds of reasons that you can just keep building and do whatever you want.

Look at me. My job ended on 31 December 2025 and I never wanted a job again, so the only thing I can be building is a business.

I cannot convince you about this. It’s only going to work for people who realize that the runway is temporary. So probably, come back to this article when you run out of runway. That’s all I can tell you, and you will see it with different eyes then.

**If you do have a job,** ask yourself how much energy you have at the end of the day.

**Zero.** You have nothing left and you are completely dependent on the job. You should be looking for ways to reduce that, and maybe you’ll find out the only way is to change the job, change the role you’re doing, or level up. Often to a manager, if you know what you’re doing. For me, the job actually became harder for a certain period of time. But after learning how to manage my time, not other people but myself, it became easier. I would not recommend trying to do this yourself all alone, because it will take years. Let’s be honest, if you’re already not feeling like pouring your heart into finding a new job and being very targeted, alone you’re not going to get there, because you don’t have enough energy. So you need help.

**Energy, and you’re happy where you are.** Then be happy for a while. But know that eventually there’s a layoff coming. There’s no job safety. You have to realize that and remember it.

**Energy, and you’re not happy where you are.** That’s great. You still have pay and you have energy, so put it in the right direction. And do it before there’s a layoff, or before your runway gets short.

**And if you have time and energy,** stop f\*king around with hobby projects. Seriously, you need to build something for yourself and for your family that will outlast jobs. There is still a value creation process, and you better start creating value instead of exchanging your time for money. Especially in software engineering. I don’t think we have as much time as we think.

Building is super cheap now, so the only thing you need to find out is whether you can sell it, and whether it’s specific enough. AI is very good in many places, better than humans, but in many places it’s much worse, especially deep down in a few verticals and domains. So build on your strengths. Find the domain where you have a particular advantage, because maybe you’ve been working in health for 20 years and you have insights that are not documented or available online for the models to be trained on.  
Use that.

You can still build. But if you start with the building, you’re always going to lose this competition to AI, if not right now, eventually. That is not a front to compete on in the long term at all.
