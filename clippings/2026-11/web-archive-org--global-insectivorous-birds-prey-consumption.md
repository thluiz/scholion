## Abstract

In this paper, we present an estimate of the predation impact of the global population of insectivorous birds based on 103 (for the most part) published studies of prey consumption (kg ha−1 season−1) of insectivorous birds in seven biome types. By extrapolation—taking into account the global land cover of the various biomes—an estimate of the annual prey consumption of the world’s insectivorous birds was obtained. We estimate the prey biomass consumed by the world’s insectivorous birds to be somewhere between 400 and 500 million metric tons year−1, but most likely at the lower end of this range (corresponding to an energy consumption of ≈ 2.7 × 1018 J year−1 or ≈ 0.15% of the global terrestrial net primary production). Birds in forests account for > 70% of the global annual prey consumption of insectivorous birds (≥ 300 million tons year−1), whereas birds in other biomes (savannas and grasslands, croplands, deserts, and Arctic tundra) are less significant contributors (≥ 100 million tons year−1). Especially during the breeding season, when adult birds feed their nestlings protein-rich prey, large numbers of herbivorous insects (i.e., primarily in the orders Coleoptera, Diptera, Hemiptera, Hymenoptera, Lepidoptera, and Orthoptera) supplemented by spiders are captured. The estimates presented in this paper emphasize the ecological and economic importance of insectivorous birds in suppressing potentially harmful insect pests on a global scale—especially in forested areas.

## Introduction

Birds, represented by nearly 10,700 species, are found across the world in all major terrestrial biomes. Accordingly, they exhibit a large variety of life styles and foraging behaviors (see Wiens [1989](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR179 "Wiens JA (1989) The ecology of bird communities, Vol. 1. Cambridge University Press, Cambridge, UK")). While some birds depend predominantly on plant diets, such as seeds, fruits, and nectar, others feed as carnivores on animal prey, or as omnivores on a mixed diet of plant/animal matter. Most bird species are insectivores that depend for the most part on insects as prey (Losey and Vaughan [2006](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR97 "Losey JE, Vaughan M (2006) The economic value of ecological services provided by insects. Bioscience 56:311–323"); Şekercioğlu [2006a](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR142 "Şekercioğlu CH (2006a) Ecological significance of bird populations. Handbook of the birds of the world 11:15-51. Lynx Edicions, Barcelona 
http://www.hbw.com/
(accessed 16 June 2017)")). In this paper, “insectivorous birds” are defined in a wider sense as the total of all bird groups that include, at least temporarily, a considerable percentage of arthropods (in particular insects and spiders) in their diets (Lopes et al. [2016](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR96 "Lopes LE, Fernandes AM, Medeiros MC, Marini MÂ (2016) A classification scheme for avian diet types. J Field Ornithol 87:309–322")). Included in this definition are also omnivorous birds such as starlings (Sturnidae) and thrushes (Turdidae) that consume large amounts of arthropods in addition to other types of food (Del Hoyo et al. [2016](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR29 "Del Hoyo J, Elliott A, Sargatal J, Christie DA, de Juana E (2016) Handbook of the birds of the world alive. Lynx Edicions, Barcelona 
http://www.hbw.com/
(accessed 16 June 2017)")). The predominance of insectivory as a feeding style among birds might be explained by the fact that insects (dominating the land biota in terms of numbers, biomass, and diversity) constitute the largest food base for terrestrial carnivorous animals. So, for instance, social insects alone are assumed to have a standing biomass of > 700 million tons globally (compare Hölldobler and Wilson [1994](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR61 "Hölldobler B, Wilson EO (1994) Journey to the ants: a story of scientific exploration. Harvard University Press, Cambridge"); Sanderson [1996](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR137 "Sanderson MG (1996) Biomass of termites and their emissions of methane and carbon dioxide: a global database. Global Biogeochem Cy 10:543–557")).

Şekercioğlu ([2006b](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR143 "Şekercioğlu CH (2006b) Increasing awareness of avian ecological function. Trends Ecol Evol 21:464–471")) states that birds are “important but ecologically little known actors in many ecosystems.” Likewise, Wenny et al. ([2011](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR172 "Wenny DG, Devault TL, Johnson MD, Kelly D, Şekercioğlu CH, Tomback DF, Whelan CJ (2011) The need to quantify ecosystem services provided by birds. Auk 128:1–14")) state “Birds provide many ecosystem services, which by and large are invisible and underappreciated.” It has thus been suggested that “quantifying the services provided by birds is crucial to understand their importance for ecosystems and for the people that benefit from them” (Whelan et al. [2015](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR175 "Whelan CJ, Şekercioğlu ÇH, Wenny DG (2015) Why birds matter: from economic ornithology to ecosystem services. J Ornithol 156:227–238")). While several attempts had been undertaken to quantify the food consumption of marine birds and shorebirds on a global scale (e.g., Wiens [1989](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR179 "Wiens JA (1989) The ecology of bird communities, Vol. 1. Cambridge University Press, Cambridge, UK"); Brooke [2004](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR16 "Brooke MDL (2004) The food consumption of the world’s seabirds. P Roy Soc Lond B Bio (Supp) 271:S246–S248")), the combined predation impact of the world’s insectivorous birds is still unknown.

Here, we provide estimates for the annual biomass of prey that is consumed by the global population of insectivorous birds in individual biome types and worldwide based on data from the literature. Furthermore, we present an estimate of the standing biomass of the global population of insectivorous birds. This study is intended as a continuation of the papers by Nyffeler ([2000](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR113 "Nyffeler M (2000) Ecological impact of spider predation: a critical assessment of Bristowe‘s and Turnbull‘s estimates. Bull Br Arachnol Soc 11:367–373")) and Nyffeler and Birkhofer ([2017](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR114 "Nyffeler M, Birkhofer K (2017) An estimated 400–800 million tons of prey are annually killed by the global spider community. Sci Nat 104:30"))—who were studying spiders—to get a better understanding of the global extent to which potentially harmful herbivorous insects are suppressed by major natural enemies.

## Methods

### Estimate of the standing biomass of the global population of insectivorous birds

For each of seven terrestrial biome types, the bird biomass across the entire biome was assessed by calculating the product of (_D_) × (_W_) × (_Y_), whereby _D_ = mean bird density (birds ha−1), _W_ = mean bird body mass (kg fresh weight bird−1), and _Y_ = area size of the entire biome type (ha). The mean breeding bird densities (representing global averages) for the various biome types were extracted from a world literature review by Gaston et al. ([2003](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR45 "Gaston KJ, Blackburn TM, Goldewijk KK (2003) Habitat conversion and global avian biodiversity loss. Proc R Soc Lond B Biol Sci 270:1293–1300")) and are largely in agreement with North American breeding bird densities compiled by Terborgh ([1989](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR157 "Terborgh J (1989) Where have all the birds gone? Princeton University Press, Princeton"), page 71). Area sizes for the various biomes were taken from Saugier et al. ([2001](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR138 "Saugier B, Roy J, Mooney HA (2001) Estimations of global terrestrial productivity: converging toward a single number? In: Roy J, Saugier B, Mooney HA (eds) Terrestrial global productivity. Academic Press, San Diego, pp 543–557")); essentially, these values do not differ very much from the more up to date 2010 land cover distribution data provided by FAO ([https://ourworldindata.org/land-cover](https://ourworldindata.org/land-cover)) but are more suitable for our purposes because they are broken down into more detailed cover classes than the latter ones allowing a more rigorous assessment. To obtain a mean body mass for Arctic tundra birds, an overall mean for 18 tundra-inhabiting species (see Sokolov et al. [2012](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR151 "Sokolov V, Ehrich D, Yoccoz NG, Sokolov A, Lecomte N (2012) Bird communities of the Arctic shrub tundra of Yamal: habitat specialists and generalists. PLoS One 7:e50335")) was calculated based on data from Del Hoyo et al. ([2016](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR29 "Del Hoyo J, Elliott A, Sargatal J, Christie DA, de Juana E (2016) Handbook of the birds of the world alive. Lynx Edicions, Barcelona 
http://www.hbw.com/
(accessed 16 June 2017)")). An overall mean body mass for desert birds was calculated based on weight data for 26 species occurring in Chihuahuan deserts (Gutzwiller and Barrow [2002](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR56 "Gutzwiller KJ, Barrow WC (2002) Does bird community structure vary with landscape patchiness? A Chihuahuan Desert perspective. Oikos 98:284–298")). Mean bird body mass values for the remaining six biome types were gathered from the following literature sources: Howell ([1971](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR70 "Howell TR (1971) An ecological study of the birds of the lowland pine savanna adjacent to rain forest in northeastern Nicaragua. Living Bird 10:185–242")); Karr ([1971](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR81 "Karr JR (1971) Structure of avian communities in selected Panama and Illinois habitats. Ecol Monogr 41:207–233")); Wiens ([1973](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR177 "Wiens JA (1973) Pattern and process in grassland bird communities. Ecol Monogr 43:237–270")); Holmes and Sturges ([1975](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR65 "Holmes RT, Sturges FW (1975) Bird community dynamics and energetics in a northern hardwoods ecosystem. J Anim Ecol 44:175–200")); Wiens and Nussbaum ([1975](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR182 "Wiens JA, Nussbaum RA (1975) Model estimation of energy flow in northwestern coniferous forest bird communities. Ecology 56:547–561")); Kartanas ([1989](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR83 "Kartanas E (1989) Energy flow through breeding bird communities of selected environments of the rural landscape. Ekol Pol 37:157–178")); and Terborgh et al. ([1990](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR158 "Terborgh J, Robinson SK, Parker TA, Munn CA, Pierpont N (1990) Structure and organization of an Amazonian forest bird community. Ecol Monogr 60:213–238")).

Summing up the seven subtotals produced an estimate of the standing biomass of the global terrestrial avifauna. From this, an estimate of the standing biomass of the global population of insectivorous birds was deduced, assuming that ≈ 90% of the terrestrial bird individuals in the temperate, boreal, and arctic zones and ≈ 60% in the tropics are arthropod-eaters (see Assumption 1, “[Methods](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Sec2)” section).

### Estimate of the annual prey consumption of the global population of insectivorous birds

We used a simple model involving few assumptions as is advised in cases where a field of study is still largely undeveloped (Weathers [1983](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR170 "Weathers WW (1983) Birds of Southern California’s Deep Canyon. University of California Press, Berkeley"); Nyffeler and Birkhofer [2017](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR114 "Nyffeler M, Birkhofer K (2017) An estimated 400–800 million tons of prey are annually killed by the global spider community. Sci Nat 104:30")). Our estimate is based on mean values of prey consumption ha−1 year−1 in the various biome types, which subsequently were extrapolated on a global scale. To retrieve comparable data, all values obtained from the literature were converted to kg fresh weight ha−1 year−1. A total of 103 prey consumption values were gathered from three different information sources:

*   Source 1: In 26 cases, published values of prey consumption were used (see [Supplementary material](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#MOESM1)).
    
*   Source 2: In 53 cases, energy demand estimates for bird communities extracted from the scientific literature (see [Supplementary material](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#MOESM1)) were converted into food consumption measures. The conversions are based on an overall average water content of arthropod prey of ≈ 70% (Zandt [1997](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR188 "Zandt HS (1997) Water content of prey of nestling blue tits in a Corsican habitat. Neth J Zool 47:125–131"); Brodmann and Reyer [1999](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR15 "Brodmann PA, Reyer HU (1999) Nestling provisioning in water pipits (Anthus spinoletta): do parents go for specific nutrients or profitable prey? Oecologia 120:506–514"); Bureš and Weidinger [2000](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR20 "Bureš S, Weidinger K (2000) Estimation of calcium intake by Meadow Pipit nestlings in an acidified area. J Avian Biol 31:426–429")), an energy density of animal matter of 22.5 kJ g−1 dry weight (Schaefer [1990](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR140 "Schaefer M (1990) The soil fauna of a beech forest on limestone: trophic structure and energy budget. Oecologia 82:128–136")), and 75% assimilation efficiency (Wiens [1989](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR179 "Wiens JA (1989) The ecology of bird communities, Vol. 1. Cambridge University Press, Cambridge, UK")). For details see [Supplementary material](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#MOESM1).
    
*   Source 3: There is a lack of data regarding the food consumption rates of bird communities in desert and Arctic tundra biomes. We thus calculated food consumption rates for bird communities in these two biome types based on estimates of daily energy expenditure and breeding bird densities. Energy expended for standard metabolism (_M_, in kcal day−1) was calculated with the equation _M_ = 129 _W_0.724 of Lasiewski and Dawson ([1967](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR92 "Lasiewski RC, Dawson WR (1967) A re-examination of the relation between standard metabolic rate and body weight in birds. Condor 69:13–23")), whereby _W_ equals the weight of an average sized bird in kg. Energy expended under field conditions equals approximately 2.5 times standard metabolism (Holmes and Sturges [1975](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR65 "Holmes RT, Sturges FW (1975) Bird community dynamics and energetics in a northern hardwoods ecosystem. J Anim Ecol 44:175–200")). For the calculation of the desert biome values, cactus wren (_Campylorhynchus brunneicapillus_, average body mass = 38.9 g; Dunning [2007](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR34 "Dunning JB (2007) CRC handbook of avian body masses, 2nd edn. CRC Press, Boca Raton")) was chosen as a standard bird representing this biome type, assuming a breeding season length of 90–180 days (mean = 135 days) for deserts (Wiens [1991](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR180 "Wiens JA (1991) Ecological similarity of shrub-desert avifaunas of Australia and North America. Ecology 72:479–495")). In the case of the Arctic tundra biome, snow bunting (_Plectrophenax nivalis_, average body mass = 42.2 g; Dunning [2007](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR34 "Dunning JB (2007) CRC handbook of avian body masses, 2nd edn. CRC Press, Boca Raton")) was used as a standard tundra bird, whereby a breeding season length of ≈ 100 days for the Arctic tundra biome was assumed (Weiner and Głowaciński [1975](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR171 "Weiner J, Głowaciński Z (1975) Energy flow through a bird community in a deciduous forest in southern Poland. Condor 77:233–242")). By multiplying the resulting energy consumption value for a standard bird with corresponding breeding density values taken from the literature (deserts: Austin [1970](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR6 "Austin GT (1970) Breeding birds of desert riparian habitat in southern Nevada. Condor 72:431–436"); Arctic tundra: Watson [1963](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR169 "Watson A (1963) Bird numbers on tundra in Baffin Island. Arctic 16:101–108"); James and Rathbun [1981](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR72 "James FC, Rathbun S (1981) Rarefaction, relative abundance, and diversity of avian communities. Auk 98:785–800"); Montgomerie et al. [1983](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR107 "Montgomerie RD, Cartar RV, McLaughlin RL, Lyon B (1983) Birds of Sarcpa Lake, Melville Peninsula, Northwest Territories: breeding phenologies, densities and biogeography. Arctic 36:65–75"); Sokolov et al. [2012](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR151 "Sokolov V, Ehrich D, Yoccoz NG, Sokolov A, Lecomte N (2012) Bird communities of the Arctic shrub tundra of Yamal: habitat specialists and generalists. PLoS One 7:e50335")), rough estimates of the energy consumption for desert and Arctic tundra bird communities, respectively, during the breeding season were obtained. Subsequently, these energy consumption values were converted into food consumption rates (the same conversion factors being applied as in the previous paragraph), which yielded 18 values for desert and 6 values for Arctic tundra sites. For details see [Supplementary material](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#MOESM1).
    

The 103 prey consumption values were assigned to the following seven groups of terrestrial biomes: (1) tropical forests, (2) temperate and boreal forests, (3) tropical grasslands and savannas/Mediterranean shrubland, (4) temperate grasslands (incl. meadows, pastures, old fields), (5) cropland, (6) deserts, and (7) Arctic tundra. The data were pooled by computing an average prey consumption value (_x̅_ kg ha−1 year−1) for each biome type. By multiplying the average prey consumption ha−1 year−1 with the corresponding area size of each biome type (based on Saugier et al. [2001](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR138 "Saugier B, Roy J, Mooney HA (2001) Estimations of global terrestrial productivity: converging toward a single number? In: Roy J, Saugier B, Mooney HA (eds) Terrestrial global productivity. Academic Press, San Diego, pp 543–557")), a prey consumption subtotal for each biome type was derived. Summing up the seven subtotals produced an estimate of the global annual prey consumption by the insectivorous avifauna (Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2)). This figure refers exclusively to arthropod prey, whereas other types of invertebrates, such as earthworms, slugs, and snails, are not included.

Our assessment is based on the following assumptions:

*   Assumption 1: Prey consumption measures presented in the literature for land bird communities were downsized to the corresponding values for insectivorous birds, taking into account that an estimated 90% of all land bird individuals (and about two thirds of all species) in the temperate, boreal, and arctic zones are insectivores during the breeding season, whereas ≈ 60% of all individuals (and 62% of all species) in the tropics are insectivores. The figure of 60% has also been chosen for non-tropical desert habitats (see [Supplementary material](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#MOESM1)). The figure of 90% for the Palearctic birds has been calculated based on population size/diet composition data for 422 bird species presented in the data base “Birds of Switzerland” of the Swiss Ornithological Institute Sempach; it can be considered to be representative for the European temperate/cold regions (see [http://www.vogelwarte.ch/en/birds/birds-of-switzerland/](http://www.vogelwarte.ch/en/birds/birds-of-switzerland/)). A similarly high proportion of all breeding land bird individuals in the Nearctic realm are insectivores (calculated based on data presented by Wiens [1973](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR177 "Wiens JA (1973) Pattern and process in grassland bird communities. Ecol Monogr 43:237–270"); Wiens and Nussbaum [1975](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR182 "Wiens JA, Nussbaum RA (1975) Model estimation of energy flow in northwestern coniferous forest bird communities. Ecology 56:547–561"), Holmes et al. [1986](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR67 "Holmes RT, Sherry TW, Sturges FW (1986) Bird community dynamics in a temperate deciduous forest: long-term trends at Hubbard Brook. Ecol Monogr 56:201–220"); and others). The figure of ≈ 60% for tropical birds is a rough estimate based on various sources (see Karr [1971](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR81 "Karr JR (1971) Structure of avian communities in selected Panama and Illinois habitats. Ecol Monogr 41:207–233"), [1975](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR82 "Karr JR (1975) Production, energy pathways, and community diversity in forest birds. In: Golley EB, Medina E (eds) Tropical ecological systems: trends in terrestrial and aquatic research. Springer, New York, pp 161–176"); Poulin et al. [1994](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR123 "Poulin B, Lefebvre G, McNeil R (1994) Diets of land birds from northeastern Venezuela. Condor 96:354–367"); Poulin and Lefebvre [1996](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR122 "Poulin B, Lefebvre G (1996) Dietary relationships of migrant and resident birds from a humid forest in central Panama. Auk 113:277–287"); Leigh [1999](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR93 "Leigh EG (1999) Tropical forest ecology: a view from Barro Colorado Island. Oxford University Press, New York"); Sakai [2002](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR135 "Sakai S (2002) General flowering in lowland mixed dipterocarp forests of South-East Asia. Biol J Linn Soc 75:233–247"); Tscharntke et al. [2008](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR162 "Tscharntke T, Şekercioğlu ÇH, Dietsch TV, Sodhi NS, Hoehn P, Tylianakis JM (2008) Landscape constraints on functional diversity of birds and insects in tropical agroecosystems. Ecology 89:944–951"); Maas et al. [2015](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR99 "Maas B, Tscharntke T, Saleh S, Dwi Putra D, Clough Y (2015) Avian species identity drives predation success in tropical cacao agroforestry. J Appl Ecol 52:735–743"); Sam et al. [2017](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR136 "Sam K, Koane B, Jeppy S, Sykorova J, Novotny V (2017) Diet of land birds along an elevational gradient in Papua New Guinea. Sci Rep 7:44018")).
    
*   Assumption 2: The breeding season diets of the avifauna in temperate forests and in some temperate grasslands are composed of ≥ 75% arthropods (Głowaciński et al. [1984](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR51 "Głowaciński Z, Kozlowski J, Weiner J (1984) Energy and matter flow through bird populations. ### Statistical analysis of annual prey consumption in the various biomes

To determine whether prey consumption rates (kg arthropods ha−1 year−1) differed among biomes, we first determined that the consumption data among biomes were not normally distributed using normal probability plots. Rather than using a normalizing transformation, we instead performed a Kruskal-Wallis one-way analysis of variance by ranks test. The omnibus test was followed with a pairwise multiple comparison using Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons of independent samples corrected for ties (Pohlert [2018](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR121 "Pohlert T (2018) PMCMRplus: calculate pairwise multiple comparisons of mean rank sums extended. R package version 1.0.1. 
https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=PMCMRplus
(accessed 30 Mar 2018)")). Analyses were performed with R, the programming language (R Core Team [2018](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR126 "R Core Team (2018) R: a language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. URL 
https://www.R-project.org/
(accessed 30 Mar 2018)")).

## Results

### Standing biomass of the global population of insectivorous birds

Based on estimates of avian standing biomass in various terrestrial biomes, we estimate the total standing biomass of the global terrestrial avifauna to be 3981 × 106 kg fresh weight (= roughly 4 million metric tons; Table [1](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab1)). This value is similar to an estimate of 5 million tons for the global terrestrial avifauna calculated using a different approach by Alerstam ([1993](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR2 "Alerstam T (1993) Bird migration. Cambridge University Press, Cambridge, UK")). Because it is assumed that ≈ 90% of all land bird individuals in the temperate, boreal, and arctic zones and ≈ 60% in the tropics are insectivorous foragers (see Assumption 1, “[Methods](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Sec2)” section), it follows that the standing biomass of the global community of insectivorous birds might be on the order of ≈ 3 million tons (Table [1](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab1)). This value is a small fraction of the global standing biomass of other predaceous animal taxa such as spiders (≈ 25 million tons; Nyffeler and Birkhofer [2017](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR114 "Nyffeler M, Birkhofer K (2017) An estimated 400–800 million tons of prey are annually killed by the global spider community. Sci Nat 104:30")), ants (≈ 280 million tons; Hölldobler and Wilson [1994](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR61 "Hölldobler B, Wilson EO (1994) Journey to the ants: a story of scientific exploration. Harvard University Press, Cambridge")), or whales (16–103 million tons; Pershing et al. [2010](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR120 "Pershing AJ, Christensen LB, Record NR, Sherwood GD, Stetson PB (2010) The impact of whaling on the ocean carbon cycle: why bigger was better. PLoS One 5:e12444")). The comparatively low value of the global standing biomass of wild birds is partially explained by the fact that birds have a very low production efficiency (i.e., low P/A-ratio). With other words, in birds, the vast majority of the assimilated energy is lost in respiration and only ≈ 1–2% is converted to biomass (see Golley [1968](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR52 "Golley FB (1968) Secondary productivity in terrestrial communities. Am Zool 8:53–59"); Holmes and Sturges [1975](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR65 "Holmes RT, Sturges FW (1975) Bird community dynamics and energetics in a northern hardwoods ecosystem. J Anim Ecol 44:175–200"); Humphreys [1979](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR71 "Humphreys WF (1979) Production and respiration in animal populations. J Anim Ecol 48:427–453")).

### Prey consumption rates of insectivorous birds in the various biomes

Prey consumption rates (kg arthropods ha−1 year−1) varied significantly among biomes (Kruskal-Wallis chi-squared = 51.179, df = 6, _P_ < 0.001; Fig. [1](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Fig1)). A Dunn’s post hoc multiple comparison test revealed that prey consumption in tropical forests was greater than in all other biomes (all _P_ ≤ 0.022). Prey consumption in temperate-boreal forests was greater than in tundra (_P_ < 0.001), desert (_P_ < 0.001), and temperate grasslands (_P_ = 0.009), but did not differ from tropical grasslands and croplands. Prey consumption was greater in tropical grassland and savanna than in desert (_P_ = 0.004) and tundra (_P_ = 0.024). Finally, prey consumption was greater in cropland than in desert (_P_ = 0.044). Prey consumption did not differ significantly among any of the remaining biomes. Annual prey consumption correlated positively with net primary production among biomes, using NPP values from Chapin et al. ([2011](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR22 "Chapin FS, Matson PA, Vitousek PM (2011) Principles of terrestrial ecosystem ecology, 2nd edn. Springer, New York")).

### Estimate of the global annual prey consumption by the insectivorous birds

Our calculation of the annual prey consumption by the global population of insectivorous birds produced an estimate of 404,586 × 106 kg year−1 (= 404.6 million tons; Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2)), which corresponds to an energy consumption of ≈ 2.7 × 1018 J year−1 (= 0.15% of the global terrestrial net primary production of 1.782 × 1021 J year−1 (see Lieth [1973](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR95 "Lieth H (1973) Primary production: terrestrial ecosystems. Hum Ecol 1:303–332"))).

This estimate (Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2)) does not include the amounts of food consumed at stopover sites during the fall and spring migrations. Currently, no quantitative assessments of the prey biomass consumed at stopover sites have been published (also see Lott et al. [2006](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR98 "Lott CA, Langan BE, Mulrooney MB, Grau RT, Miller KE (2006) Stopover ecology of nearctic-neotropical migrant songbirds in hardwood hammocks of the Florida Keys. Final Report, Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission, Tallahassee")); but considering the energy costs of approximately 10 to 20 billion birds migrating annually (see Hahn et al. [2009](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR57 "Hahn S, Bauer S, Liechti F (2009) The natural link between Europe and Africa—2.1 billion birds on migration. Oikos 118:624–626"); Berthold [2001](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR10 "Berthold P (2001) Bird migration: a general survey. Oxford University Press"); Wikelski et al. [2003](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR183 "Wikelski M, Tarlow EM, Raim A, Diehl RH, Larkin RP, Visser GH (2003) Avian metabolism: costs of migration in free-flying songbirds. Nature 423:704–704"); Fristoe [2015](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR42 "Fristoe TS (2015) Energy use by migrants and residents in North American breeding bird communities. Glob Ecol Biogeogr 24:406–415")) and taking into account that the birds resting at stopover sites only partially depend on arthropod food (Schaub and Jenni [2000](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR141 "Schaub M, Jenni L (2000) Fuel deposition of three passerine bird species along the migration route. Oecologia 122:306–317"); Suthers et al. [2000](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR155 "Suthers HB, Bickal JM, Rodewald PG (2000) Use of successional habitat and fruit resources by songbirds during autumn migration in central New Jersey. Wilson Bull 112:249–260")), we estimate that the amount of arthropod food they consume globally at stopover sites may be on the order of 3–5 million tons year−1. Thus, arthropod consumption during migratory stopovers is around 1% of the total amount of prey biomass consumed by the global population of insectivorous birds (see Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2)).

Regarding the temperate, sub-polar, and polar climates, our calculations (Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2)) assume that the arthropod consumption in these climates during the non-breeding season is reduced to a small fraction (≈ 5–10%) of the breeding season value (see “[Methods](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Sec2)”). However, there are some studies which indicate that the insectivorous activities of birds during the non-breeding season may not always be reduced so drastically—at least in some parts of the temperate/cold climate zones (see Askenmo et al. [1977](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR4 "Askenmo C, Von Brömssen A, Ekman J, Jansson C (1977) Impact of some wintering birds on spider abundance in spruce. Oikos 28:90–94"); Gunnarsson [1996](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR55 "Gunnarsson B (1996) Bird predation and vegetation structure affecting spruce-living arthropods in a temperate forest. J Anim Ecol 65:389–397"); Kirk et al. [1996](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR88 "Kirk DA, Evenden MD, Mineau P (1996) Past and current attempts to evaluate the role of birds as predators of insect pests in temperate agriculture. Curr Ornithol 13:175–269"); Vel’ký et al. [2011](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR167 "Vel’ký M, Kanuch P, Krištín A (2011) Food composition of wintering great tits (Parus major): habitat and seasonal aspects. Folia Zool 60:228–236")) —and it could therefore be argued our calculations underestimate the contribution of birds as consumers of arthropod prey during the non-breeding period (Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2)). To address this issue, we considered two extreme scenarios. In scenario 1, a minimum estimate was assessed based on the assumption that the birds’ diets in temperate/cold climates contain no arthropods during the non-breeding season; in scenario 2, a maximum estimate was assessed by assuming that the birds’ contribution as arthropod consumers during the non-breeding season in temperate/cold climates is 50% of the breeding season value. With these assumptions, the annual prey consumption of the world’s insectivorous birds was recalculated, producing a minimum estimate of 396,041 million tons year−1 and a maximum estimate of 472,145 million tons year−1. Thus, the true value of insect consumption presumably is somewhere in between approximately 400 and 500 million tons year−1, but most likely at the lower end of this range as indicated in Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2), because the availability of arthropod prey during the non-breeding season is greatly reduced in most areas of the temperate/cold climates.

For comparison, Alerstam ([1993](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR2 "Alerstam T (1993) Bird migration. Cambridge University Press, Cambridge, UK")), using a different method, estimated the total energy consumption of the world’s land birds (including arthropods, plant matter, and other food sources) to be ≈ 7.5 × 1018 J year−1. Our estimate for the world’s insectivorous birds is consistent with this broader estimate. The difference of 4.6 × 1018 J year−1 between the two estimates is mainly explained by the fact that in our estimate exclusively feeding on arthropod prey is considered, whereas in Alerstam’s estimate, feeding on additional food sources was assumed. Especially during the non-breeding season, when the availability of arthropod prey is strongly reduced in many places of the globe, land birds consume large amounts of plant matter (Clements and Shelford [1939](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR24 "Clements F, Shelford V (1939) Bio-ecology. John Wiley and Sons, New York"); Brown et al. [1979](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR17 "Brown JH, Reichman OJ, Davidson DW (1979) Granivory in desert ecosystems. Annu Rev Ecol Evol S 10:201–227"); Robinson and Sutherland [1997](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR130 "Robinson RA, Sutherland WJ (1997) The feeding ecology of seed-eating birds on farmland in winter. Joint Nature Conservation Committee, Peterborough, pp 162–169"); Buckingham et al. [1999](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR19 "Buckingham DL, Evans AD, Morris AJ, Orsman CJ, Yaxley R (1999) Use of set-aside land in winter by declining farmland bird species in the UK. ## Discussion

### Experimental evidence supporting our theory of high global predation impact by insectivorous birds

Our calculations presented in Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2) imply that insectivorous birds exert substantial predation pressure on insects and other arthropods, especially in tropical and temperate/boreal forest ecosystems. This is supported by a large number of experimental studies conducted in a variety of habitats in different parts of the world (see Şekercioğlu [2006a](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR142 "Şekercioğlu CH (2006a) Ecological significance of bird populations. ### Relative contribution of different biome categories to the global annual prey consumption

Birds in forests account for 75% of the annual prey consumption of the world’s insectivorous birds (≈ 300 million tons year−1; Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2)). Forests cover a large portion of the global terrestrial surface area (41.6 million km2; Saugier et al. [2001](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR138 "Saugier B, Roy J, Mooney HA (2001) Estimations of global terrestrial productivity: converging toward a single number? In: Roy J, Saugier B, Mooney HA (eds) Terrestrial global productivity. Academic Press, San Diego, pp 543–557")), and in these productive and vegetatively complex habitats, birds usually reach higher diversities (Willson [1974](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR185 "Willson MF (1974) Avian community organization and habitat structure. Ecology 55:1017–1029")) and numbers ha−1 compared to non-forested areas (Gaston et al. [2003](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR45 "Gaston KJ, Blackburn TM, Goldewijk KK (2003) Habitat conversion and global avian biodiversity loss. Proc R Soc Lond B Biol Sci 270:1293–1300")). A similar trend of highest predation impact occurring in forested areas has been reported for spiders (Nyffeler and Birkhofer [2017](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR114 "Nyffeler M, Birkhofer K (2017) An estimated 400–800 million tons of prey are annually killed by the global spider community. Sci Nat 104:30")). Forest birds feed frequently on potentially harmful caterpillar and beetle pests (Holmes et al. [1979](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR66 "Holmes RT, Schultz JC, Nothnagle P (1979) Bird predation on forest insects: an exclosure experiment. Science 206:462–463"); Fayt et al. [2005](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR39 "Fayt P, Machmer MM, Steeger C (2005) Regulation of spruce bark beetles by woodpeckers—a literature review. Forest Ecol Manag 206:1–14"); Moorman et al. [2007](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR108 "Moorman CE, Bowen LT, Kilgo JC, Sorenson CE, Hanula JL, Horn S, Ulyshen MD (2007) Seasonal diets of insectivorous birds using canopy gaps in a bottomland forest. J Field Ornithol 78:11–20")). This is especially true during the breeding season, when passerines (song birds) catch large numbers of leaf-eating caterpillars to feed them to their nestlings (Gibb and Betts [1963](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR46 "Gibb JA, Betts MM (1963) Food and food supply of nestling tits (Paridae) in Breckland pine. J Anim Ecol 32:489–533"); Holmes et al. [1979](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR66 "Holmes RT, Schultz JC, Nothnagle P (1979) Bird predation on forest insects: an exclosure experiment. Science 206:462–463"); Gajdoš and Krištín [1997](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR43 "Gajdoš P, Krištín A (1997) Spiders (Araneae) as bird food. In: Zabka M (ed) Proceedings of the 16th European Colloquium of Arachnology, Siedlce, pp 91–105"); Mols and Visser [2002](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR106 "Mols CM, Visser ME (2002) Great tits can reduce caterpillar damage in apple orchards. J Appl Ecol 39:888–899")). At this time of the year, caterpillars make up 20–90% of the nestling diets of many species of insectivorous birds (Gibb and Betts [1963](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR46 "Gibb JA, Betts MM (1963) Food and food supply of nestling tits (Paridae) in Breckland pine. J Anim Ecol 32:489–533"); Pravosudov and Pravosudova [1996](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR124 "Pravosudov VV, Pravosudova EV (1996) The breeding biology of the willow tit in northeastern Siberia. Wilson Bull 108:80–93"); Gajdoš and Krištín [1997](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR43 "Gajdoš P, Krištín A (1997) Spiders (Araneae) as bird food. In: Zabka M (ed) Proceedings of the 16th European Colloquium of Arachnology, Siedlce, pp 91–105"); Török and Tóth [1999](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR160 "Török J, Tóth L (1999) Asymmetric competition between two tit species: a reciprocal removal experiment. J Anim Ecol 68:338–345"); Pagani-Núñez et al. [2017](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR116 "Pagani-Núñez E, Renom M, Mateos-Gonzalez F, Cotín J, Senar JC (2017) The diet of great tit nestlings: comparing observation records and stable isotope analyses. Basic Appl Ecol 18:57–66")). Due to high protein content and easy digestibility, caterpillars comprise an optimal diet for nestling birds (Tremblay et al. [2005](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR161 "Tremblay I, Thomas D, Blondel J, Perret P, Lambrechts MM (2005) The effect of habitat quality on foraging patterns, provisioning rate and nestling growth in Corsican blue tits Parus caeruleus. Ibis 147:17–24")). Data suggest that forest birds exert considerable predation pressure on lepidopteran pests, such as the eastern spruce budworm (_Choristoneura fumiferana_; Holmes et al. [1979](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR66 "Holmes RT, Schultz JC, Nothnagle P (1979) Bird predation on forest insects: an exclosure experiment. Science 206:462–463"); Şekercioğlu [2006a](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR142 "Şekercioğlu CH (2006a) Ecological significance of bird populations. Handbook of the birds of the world 11:15-51. Lynx Edicions, Barcelona 
http://www.hbw.com/
(accessed 16 June 2017)")). Crawford and Jennings ([1989](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR25 "Crawford HS, Jennings DT (1989) Predation by birds on spruce budworm Choristoneura fumiferana: functional, numerical, and total responses. Ecology 70:152–163")) found that birds destroyed 84% of larval and pupal eastern spruce budworms at low densities of this pest. The birds are most effective as natural enemies at endemic pest densities (Holmes et al. [1979](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR66 "Holmes RT, Schultz JC, Nothnagle P (1979) Bird predation on forest insects: an exclosure experiment. Science 206:462–463"); Holmes [1990](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR63 "Holmes RT (1990) Ecological and evolutionary impacts of bird predation on forest insects: an overview. Stud Avian Biol 13:6–13")). Fayt et al. ([2005](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR39 "Fayt P, Machmer MM, Steeger C (2005) Regulation of spruce bark beetles by woodpeckers—a literature review. Forest Ecol Manag 206:1–14")) pointed out that woodpeckers (Picidae) suppress the abundance of bark beetles (Curculionidae) in coniferous forest landscapes. Furthermore, forest birds at times feed heavily on spiders, especially during the breeding season (Naef-Daenzer et al. [2000](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR111 "Naef-Daenzer L, Naef-Daenzer B, Nager RG (2000) Prey selection and foraging performance of breeding Great Tits Parus major in relation to food availability. J Avian Biol 31:206–214"); Pagani-Núñez et al. [2017](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR116 "Pagani-Núñez E, Renom M, Mateos-Gonzalez F, Cotín J, Senar JC (2017) The diet of great tit nestlings: comparing observation records and stable isotope analyses. Basic Appl Ecol 18:57–66")). In Scandinavian boreal forests, spiders are a major diet for overwintering tits (_Parus_ spp.), treecreepers (_Certhia familiaris_), and goldcrests (_Regulus regulus_) (Askenmo et al. [1977](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR4 "Askenmo C, Von Brömssen A, Ekman J, Jansson C (1977) Impact of some wintering birds on spider abundance in spruce. Oikos 28:90–94"); Gunnarsson [1996](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR55 "Gunnarsson B (1996) Bird predation and vegetation structure affecting spruce-living arthropods in a temperate forest. J Anim Ecol 65:389–397")). Spiders are an important food source for birds because of their high content of taurine, an amino acid that plays a vital role in the early development of many types of passerine birds (Ramsay and Houston [2003](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR125 "Ramsay SL, Houston DC (2003) Amino acid composition of some woodland arthropods and its implications for breeding tits and other passerines. Ibis 145:227–232"); Arnold et al. [2007](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR3 "Arnold KE, Ramsay SL, Donaldson C, Adam A (2007) Parental prey selection affects risk-taking behaviour and spatial learning in avian offspring. P Roy Soc Lond B Bio 274:2563–2569")). The propensity for birds to feed on spiders can reduce some positive economic impact of avian insectivory because spiders themselves are highly beneficial natural enemies of insects (Nyffeler [2000](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR113 "Nyffeler M (2000) Ecological impact of spider predation: a critical assessment of Bristowe‘s and Turnbull‘s estimates. Bull Br Arachnol Soc 11:367–373"); Nyffeler and Birkhofer [2017](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR114 "Nyffeler M, Birkhofer K (2017) An estimated 400–800 million tons of prey are annually killed by the global spider community. Sci Nat 104:30")). The same is true when birds feed on large numbers of predaceous ants or odonates, as is sometimes the case in purple martins (_Progne subis_) and house martins (_Delichon urbicum_) (Kelly et al. [2013](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR86 "Kelly JF, Bridge ES, Frick WF, Chilson PB (2013) Ecological energetics of an abundant aerial insectivore, the Purple Martin. PLoS One 8:e76616"); Orłowski et al. [2014](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR115 "Orłowski G, Karg J, Karg G (2014) Functional invertebrate prey groups reflect dietary responses to phenology and farming activity and pest control services in three sympatric species of aerially foraging insectivorous birds. PLoS One 9:e114906"); Helms et al. [2016](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR60 "Helms JA, Godfrey AP, Ames T, Bridge ES (2016) Predator foraging altitudes reveal the structure of aerial insect communities. Sci Rep 6:28670")).

Birds in grasslands and savannas contributed 15% (i.e., ≈ 60 million tons year−1; Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2)) to the global annual prey biomass. Grasslands and savannas cover a vast area of the globe (45.4 million km2; Saugier et al. [2001](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR138 "Saugier B, Roy J, Mooney HA (2001) Estimations of global terrestrial productivity: converging toward a single number? In: Roy J, Saugier B, Mooney HA (eds) Terrestrial global productivity. Academic Press, San Diego, pp 543–557")). Included in this figure are 2.8 million km2 Mediterranean shrublands. The prey biomass ha−1 year−1 of bird communities in the grassland biome is considerably lower than that in forests (Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2); Ford and Bell [1981](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR41 "Ford HA, Bell H (1981) Density of birds in eucalypt woodland affected to varying degrees by dieback. Emu 81:202–208"); Wiens [1989](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR179 "Wiens JA (1989) The ecology of bird communities, Vol. 1. Cambridge University Press, Cambridge, UK")). Notwithstanding that, North American studies have shown that grassland birds at times exert noticeable predation pressure on grasshopper populations (Joern [1986](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR75 "Joern A (1986) Experimental study of avian predation on coexisting grasshopper populations (Orthoptera: Acrididae) in a sandhills grassland. Oikos 46:243–249"); Belovsky et al. [1990](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR8 "Belovsky GE, Slade JB, Stockhoff BA (1990) Susceptibility to predation for different grasshoppers: an experimental study. Ecology 71:624–634"); Bock et al. [1992](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR13 "Bock CE, Bock JH, Grant MC (1992) Effects of bird predation on grasshopper densities in an Arizona grassland. Ecology 73:1706–1717")).

Bird communities associated with agricultural areas contributed roughly 7% (i.e., ≈ 28 million tons year−1; Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2)). Cropland covers an area of 13.5 million km2 (Saugier et al., [2001](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR138 "Saugier B, Roy J, Mooney HA (2001) Estimations of global terrestrial productivity: converging toward a single number? In: Roy J, Saugier B, Mooney HA (eds) Terrestrial global productivity. Academic Press, San Diego, pp 543–557")) Agricultural landscapes are mosaics of crop fields, shelterbelts, and tree-lined field roads (Kartanas [1989](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR83 "Kartanas E (1989) Energy flow through breeding bird communities of selected environments of the rural landscape. Ekol Pol 37:157–178"); Gámez-Virués et al. [2007](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR44 "Gámez-Virués S, Bonifacio RS, Gurr GM, Kinross C, Raman A, Nicol HI (2007) Arthropod prey of shelterbelt-associated birds: linking faecal samples with biological control of agricultural pests. Aust J Entomol 46:325–331")). In our estimates of prey biomass ha−1 year−1 for croplands (Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2)), birds associated with tree-lined field roads have been taken into account as well (compare Kartanas [1989](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR83 "Kartanas E (1989) Energy flow through breeding bird communities of selected environments of the rural landscape. Ekol Pol 37:157–178")). Although birds in the agricultural landscape are known to feed at times heavily on potentially harmful lepidopteran and coleopteran pests (Woronecki and Dolbeer [1980](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR187 "Woronecki PP, Dolbeer RA (1980) The influence of insects in bird damage control. In: Clark JP (ed) Proceedings 9th vertebrate pest control conference. University of California, Davis, pp 53–59")), examples of farmland birds substantially suppressing crop pests are few, which may be explained by the fact that crop fields are usually inhabited/visited by birds in rather low numbers (Gaston et al. [2003](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR45 "Gaston KJ, Blackburn TM, Goldewijk KK (2003) Habitat conversion and global avian biodiversity loss. Proc R Soc Lond B Biol Sci 270:1293–1300")), at least in temperate regions. Reports of birds suppressing agricultural pests refer for the most part to studies in tropical plantations (e.g., Hooks et al. [2003](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR68 "Hooks CR, Pandey RR, Johnson MW (2003) Impact of avian and arthropod predation on lepidopteran caterpillar densities and plant productivity in an ephemeral agroecosystem. Ecol Entomol 28:522–532"); Koh [2008](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR90 "Koh LP (2008) Birds defend oil palms from herbivorous insects. Ecol Appl 18:821–825")). A classic example of the successful avian control of a pest species comes from tropical coffee plantations in Costa Rica, Guatemala, Jamaica, Mexico, Panama, and Puerto Rico, where the coffee berry borer _Hypothenemus hampei_—considered to be the world’s most damaging insect pest in coffee—is successfully controlled by insectivorous avian communities often largely composed of wood-warblers (Parulidae) (Greenberg et al. [2000](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR54 "Greenberg R, Bichier P, Angon AC, MacVean C, Perez R, Cano E (2000) The impact of avian insectivory on arthropods and leaf damage in some Guatemalan coffee plantations. Ecology 81:1750–1755"); Perfecto et al. [2004](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR119 "Perfecto I, Vandermeer JH, Bautista GL, Nunñez GI, Greenberg R, Bichier P, Langridge S (2004) Greater predation in shaded coffee farms: the role of resident neotropical birds. Ecology 85:2677–2681"); Kellermann et al. [2008](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR85 "Kellermann JL, Johnson MD, Stercho AM, Hackett SC (2008) Ecological and economic services provided by birds on Jamaican Blue Mountain coffee farms. Conserv Biol 22:1177–1185"); Johnson et al. [2010](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR76 "Johnson MD, Kellermann JL, Stercho AM (2010) Pest reduction services by birds in shade and sun coffee in Jamaica. Anim Conserv 13:140–147"); Wenny et al. [2011](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR172 "Wenny DG, Devault TL, Johnson MD, Kelly D, Şekercioğlu CH, Tomback DF, Whelan CJ (2011) The need to quantify ecosystem services provided by birds. Auk 128:1–14"); Karp et al. [2013](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR80 "Karp DS, Mendenhall CD, Sandí RF, Chaumont N, Ehrlich PR, Hadly EA, Daily GC (2013) Forest bolsters bird abundance, pest control and coffee yield. Ecol Lett 16:1339–1347")).

Birds associated with desert and tundra biomes account for only a small percentage (each < 4%) of the global annual prey biomass (Table [2](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab2)). The low prey biomass ha−1 year−1 of birds in these biome types reflects that such habitats are covered by a sparse vegetation of low productivity supporting only low densities of birds (see Gaston et al. [2003](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR45 "Gaston KJ, Blackburn TM, Goldewijk KK (2003) Habitat conversion and global avian biodiversity loss. Proc R Soc Lond B Biol Sci 270:1293–1300")). Birds in desert and tundra habitats prey exclusively on non-pest arthropods during their occurrence in these biomes which renders them insignificant from the perspective of economic ornithology (Holmes [1966](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR62 "Holmes RT (1966) Feeding ecology of the red-backed sandpiper (Calidris alpina) in Arctic Alaska. Ecology 47:32–45"); Custer and Pitelka [1978](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR26 "Custer TW, Pitelka FA (1978) Seasonal trends in summer diet of the Lapland Longspur near Barrow, Alaska. Condor 80:295–301"); Maclean [2013](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR101 "Maclean GL (2013) Ecophysiology of desert birds. Springer, Berlin")).

## Concluding remarks

For the first time, the predation impact of the insectivorous birds has been quantified on a global scale. The global energy consumption by the insectivorous birds in the form of arthropod prey is substantial (an estimated ≈ 2.7 × 1018 J year−1). Annually, the global population of insectivorous birds consumes as much energy as a megacity the size of New York (≈ 2.8 × 1018 J year−1, in 2011; Kennedy et al. [2015](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR87 "Kennedy CA, Stewart I, Facchini A, Cersosimo I, Mele R, Chen B, Uda M, Kansal A, Chiu A, Kim KG, Dubeux C, Lebre La Rovere E, Cunha B, Pincetl S, Keirstead J, Barles S, Pusaka S, Gunawan J, Adegbile M, Nazariha M, Hoque S, Marcotullio PJ, González Otharán F, Genena T, Ibrahim N, Farooqui R, Cervantes G, Sahin AD (2015) Energy and material flows of megacities. Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A 112:5985–5990")).

To fulfill these huge energy requirements, the insectivorous birds capture billions of potentially harmful herbivorous insects and other arthropods. Only few other predator groups, such as spiders and entomophagous insects, can keep up with the insectivorous birds in their capacity to suppress herbivorous insect populations in a variety of biomes (Table [3](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab3); DeBach and Rosen [1991](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR28 "DeBach P, Rosen D (1991) Biological control by natural enemies, 2nd edn. Cambridge University Press, Cambridge, UK"); Nyffeler and Birkhofer [2017](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR114 "Nyffeler M, Birkhofer K (2017) An estimated 400–800 million tons of prey are annually killed by the global spider community. Sci Nat 104:30")). Other predator groups like bats, primates, shrews, hedgehogs, frogs, salamanders, and lizards apparently are less effective natural enemies of herbivorous insects (Table [3](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#Tab3)). Although some of these latter predator groups may exert high predation pressure in a particular biome type (e.g., lizards on tropical islands; see Bennett and Gorman [1979](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR9 "Bennett AF, Gorman GC (1979) Population density and energetics of lizards on a tropical island. Oecologia 42:339–358")), these same groups are much less effective in other biomes so that their global impact cannot compare to that of spiders, entomophagous insects, or insectivorous birds. The global predation impact of the insectivorous birds (between 400 and 500 million tons year−1) is approximately of the same order of magnitude as that of the spiders (between 400 and 800 million tons year−1; see Nyffeler and Birkhofer [2017](https://web.archive.org/article/10.1007/s00114-018-1571-z?shared-article-renderer#ref-CR114 "Nyffeler M, Birkhofer K (2017) An estimated 400–800 million tons of prey are annually killed by the global spider community. Sci Nat 104:30")).
