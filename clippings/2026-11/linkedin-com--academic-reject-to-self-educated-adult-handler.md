My journey from an academic reject to a semi-respectable, self-educated adult

Chelsea Handler. Published May 19, 2016 (LinkedIn Pulse). Header image credit: Getty/Michael Tran.

The late Pulitzer-Prize-Presidential-Medal-of-Freedom-winning writer and historian Will Durant wrote that “education is a progressive discovery of our own ignorance.”

I agree.

When we are young, we all begin as idiots.

At some point, we must ask ourselves: Do we continue as idiots out of childish, idiotic pride by pretending to know it all when we don’t? Or are we willing to humbly admit that we very often don’t know the answers, and are willing to listen, to learn, to question and to self-educate?

I’m a high-school graduate…..barely….and I never missed a single homework assignment in my Modern Cannabis Inhalation Strategy class as I proceeded to not go to college.

However, what I’ve learned since high school is that not only do I love to learn and educate myself, albeit at my own, very individual pace, but I also learned that there’s really no choice in the matter if one wants to become a better person, to succeed, and to simply survive.

Education is the ultimate survival tool, and while primary education comes from the classroom and we owe all children a first-rate education, secondary education more often comes from the classroom of life and we also owe ourselves a never-ending adult education.

What all educated humans have in common is a rapacious hunger for elevating one’s mind – to understand something not just new, but also to understand something we had no idea we knew nothing about, and more important, to learn about things that don’t naturally interest us. In doing so, we shrink our vast ignorance ever so slightly in order to produce new insight.

Some of you know me through television, while I know you through telepathy. My old junior high school talk show called Chelsea Lately on E! focused on celebrity gossip, humor and silly skits intended to simply amuse myself and hopefully, some viewers.

Stupidity can be a lot of fun, but ultimately we all owe it to ourselves to lift our collective heads out of the gutter and start flashing our IQs at each other and exposing our brains to smarter people, smarter ideas, smarter thinking and a smarter species.

I intend for my new NETFLIX talk show Chelsea to be educational college-credit material.

While I did not go to college, I read as many books as I can about history, civilization, society, civil rights, politics, the Greeks, the Romans, the Zulus, the Maoris and how the Sumerians liked to drink beer 5000 years ago.

I read newspapers because I want to know about the world and who’s mismanaging it and I want to be prepared as a citizen to ask just the right perspicacious question to one of those mismanagers should our divine paths cross.

I read the dictionary from cover to cover because a rich vocabulary is a beautiful piece of mental jewelry and a fading ancient art form that gives you the equivalent of a college degree when you finish …and because it’s gratifying to be able to speak English in all its splendiferous (First Known Use Per Merriam-Webster: 1843) glory.

In short, I self-educate...and self-medicate…often at the same time.

My journey from a youthful academic reject to a semi-respectable, semi-responsible and overcompensated adult was only made possible from a combination of my curiosity and constant question-asking about things I had no idea about.

And as it turns out in life, questions are often more important than the answers, so if you can teach yourself (and your children) to ask good questions, you will have already impressed your boss, your friend, your significant other or your dog with your own healthy intellect.

And if you don’t believe me about the importance of asking questions as the educational solution for our very human inborn ignorance, then take the same exact advice from the words of one of the smartest men in the world and his very loving mother.

Dr. Isidor Rabi - the Nobel laureate in physics who died in 1988 and the discoverer of nuclear magnetic resonance that made the modern medical miracle of MRIs commonplace - was once asked, ''Why did you become a scientist, rather than a doctor or lawyer or businessman, like the other immigrant kids in your neighborhood?''

Dr. Rabi's answer?

''My mother made me a scientist without ever intending it. Every other Jewish mother in Brooklyn would ask her child after school: 'So, did you learn anything today?' But not my mother. She always asked me a different question. 'Izzy,' she would say, 'did you ask a good question today?' That difference - asking good questions - made me become a scientist!''

Questions?
