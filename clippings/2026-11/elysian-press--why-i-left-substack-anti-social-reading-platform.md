# Why I left Substack

The world doesn't need another social media platform. It needs the anti-social media platform.

I left Substack because it’s social media now and that distracts people from reading. And I'm extremely against social media and extremely bullish on reading.

It's just that the app has become _noisy_—the home feed is full of social media posts and the article feed shows only five things by the people you subscribe to before devolving into algorithmic recommendations. My own husband, who only subscribes to my newsletter and one other, downloaded the Substack app to read us both only to never see a single one of our posts.

This to me is a tragedy. We spent 20 years building social media platforms—we didn't need another one. We needed the _anti_-social media platform. We need quiet places for long-form reading where we can spend 30 minutes absorbed in an essay without distraction, where we can foster long attention spans, and a life spent offline.

Substack _was_ that, once. Three years ago there was nothing to do on the app but read long-form essays and leave comments on them—I loved it. "Take back your attention," was Substack's mantra back then—now we have little control over it. I have to hunt through the app and change a bunch of settings to find the writers I subscribe to in the order they were published. Mostly I see a bunch of people I don't subscribe to at all. I see short-form posts and notifications and whatever is algorithmically recommended to me.

Because of the "social mediafication" of Substack I stopped reading in the app more than a year ago. But once the reading feed turned algorithmic too, I realized it was no longer a good place to write either. The app has chosen to emphasize discovery at the expense of reading. I can see it in my numbers: My subscribers have grown from 8,300 to 22,700 in the last three years, up 175%. But readers per essay went from 3,500 to only 5,400, up just 55%.

In 2023, 1 in 2.4 of my subscribers read each essay. Today it's 1 in 4.2.

![](https://elysian.press/storage/essays/200/S6qjprLpW21rCfRLNrghYNEBFewL9fqGLIPgpI8E.png.webp)

In Substack stats, “opens” include everyone who opened the essay both in email and the app.

And "followers" are just there to follow social media posts. Only 1 in 8 reads any given essay.

![](https://elysian.press/storage/essays/200/3wam5gk0QoYSZegfT2t6sPgj3SazKL1dQFJ68yTL.png.webp)

Substack debuted the ability to "follow" people without subscribing in August 2023.

And app users aren't seeing essays. 5,500 of my subscribers have emails turned off—that was supposed to mean they are reading in the app instead, but they aren’t. Instead, article titles pass them by as if they are Twitter posts.

I’ve tested it now several times: On Substack, "opens" count everyone who opens a post via email _and_ everyone who opens a post in the app. Yet whenever I send something as an email (something _all_ subscribers get) it reaches, on average, 3,700 more people and 1,700 more opens than when I publish it as a post (something you have to have emails turned on to get).

That means thousands of people who subscribe to my newsletter only see my writing if it lands in their inbox, or don't see it at all if it lands in the app.

![](https://elysian.press/storage/essays/200/AA3U0NLzCoWdifZK5byBzSvlKxlPvnRgEjURUqv3.png.webp)

The platform keeps touting that the network has driven [80 million](https://on.substack.com/p/you-told-us-you-wanted-more-control) free subscriptions in the last six months, and [more than half](https://on.substack.com/p/switch-newsletter) of all Substack subscriptions. But as a writer, what is the point of having subscribers if they don't read?

This is not “owning my audience"—another mantra of the early Substack days. Instead the platform has realized it can drive more subscriptions with social media posts than it can with writing, and we've wound up with another social media platform instead of a reading platform.

I don't think Substack hatched some evil plan to destroy reading, it's just happening naturally because they have to reach a mass market. The company has investors and expenses and needs to drive millions of users to make them all money. Becoming a pure social media platform is definitely their best bet to do that.

Most people, after all, are not readers. There are quite a lot of scrollers though.

As a result, Substack has created yet another place for scrollers.

And that means those of us still catering to a reading audience need nearly twice as many free subscribers to have the same number of readers we had three years ago.

Writing _can't_ compete with social media posts—these are two very different activities that require very different levels of attention. One involves scrolling dozens of posts mindlessly, the other requires focusing on one piece for a long time. These activities can't happen in the same place—they are fundamentally incompatible.

I recently wrote an article titled “[If we fix the phone, we fix society](https://elysian.press/essays/fix-the-phone-fix-society),” and I think that’s true. I think that if we fix the technologies we are addicted to every day—if we get rid of the infinite scrolling and the algorithms and the notifications—then we can also eliminate the anti-social behaviors we've developed from them. But that means creating media platforms that are not social media.

That means creating reading platforms.

It didn’t occur to me that I could fix this problem because, until recently, I had been at the mercy of whatever the tech gods built for us—I was just grateful to finally have one that included writing. But then I went on a backpacking trip with a bunch of writers who are much more successful than me, none of whom are on Substack, and I realized another way was possible.

They talked about custom-built platforms that worked the way Substack used to: Readers subscribe because they read an essay they liked, not because they accidentally subscribed while reading something else. Readers read because they get the essays directly to their inbox, rather than missing them amidst a feed of social media content. Readers get essays chronologically because email still works like that, rather than filtered through feeds and algorithms. Discussion still happens through comments, not through hot takes and fly-by social media posts.

At the same time, Claude became much more capable of building literally whatever I wanted. Earlier in the year I used it to build a reading app for myself, then I built a shop for my pamphlets. By the time I got home from my backpacking trip I was able to build the writing and reading platform of my dreams. I was able to think through what a platform might look like that was optimized solely for reading, for long attention spans, for conversation and discourse between members. What would it look like to have an Enlightenment-era literary salon on the internet?

This has been a bit like when my fridge died. I wasn’t going to replace something that worked, but when it finally stopped working I was thrilled to have an excuse to upgrade to the retro fridge of my dreams. And my new site is absolutely the retro upgrade of my writing dreams.

Not only does it do all of the normal Substack things—it is a newsletter with a website and paid subscriptions—but it also allows me to do things Substack never could. Like have a fully analog [print pamphlet subscription](https://elysian.press/shop/pamphlet-subscription) with no email subscription necessary. Like have subscriptions that [come at a sliding scale](https://elysian.press/shop/pamphlet-subscription) or allow readers to subscribe to just one series, rather than the whole year. Like host [the Commons](https://elysian.press/commons), a new discussion space for subscribers, and redesign commenting—readers can now comment at the sentence level, and gift up to three “laurels” to other commenters, letting the most salient ideas sort to the top of discussions.

And like a true social club, we now have a membership program! Subscribers get points for every dollar they spend, every comment they leave, and every referral they make, with points multiplied depending on the number of years they've been a subscriber. In exchange for contributing to the press, membership tiers come with merch, the ability to float to the top of the comments sections, permanent access to my paid posts, and even board member seats and profit sharing in my business. (Warning, there are no profits yet—all dollars are reinvested in the business so this is a bet for the future!)

I’ve also added a lot of geeky things I’ve always wanted—the backend of my website completely replaces Google Docs with the most organized and beautiful place to write and research!

There are, of course, major risks to leaving Substack—namely that all of my new subscribers come in through Substack and some people still prefer to read there. But this is a personal passion project designed around my love of writing and thinking and printing pamphlets. I want to research deeply and think slowly and reimagine the world for our benefit. It is not a commercial enterprise that requires a mass market and social media and me churning out three essays a week about modern politics.

And I've taken great care to mitigate both challenges. First, if Substack is social media now, then I will use it solely as social media. I will continue to share my posts there as Substack notes. I upload my Substack export to my new site every day so new subscribers can still come in through Substack, even as they will only ever receive emails from elysian.press. I removed the custom domain from my Substack (which used to live at [www.elysian.press](http://www.elysian.press/) and now lives at ellegriffin.substack.com) and rerouted every URL that used to go to Substack at [www.elysian.press](http://www.elysian.press/) to the equivalent [elysian.press](http://elysian.press/) page.

Second, I am being very careful about the move. One of the most critical risks in leaving Substack is losing our subscribers in the process. This is because, though we can export our whole subscriber list from Substack, the first email from the new platform comes from a different email and email providers immediately block them as spam. I’ve read articles [by Molly White](https://www.citationneeded.news/substack-to-self-hosted-ghost/), whose first email missed a huge chunk of her list because of an incorrectly set up domain, [and Today in Tabs](https://www.todayintabs.com/p/migration-report) which saw opens go from 39% to 35-38% because of spam filters. Today in Tabs also lost about 1.5% of its list in the first four weeks after leaving Substack, [and Garbage Day](https://www.garbageday.email/p/excuse-navelgazing) lost 4% over four to five months. 

While I’m happy with an adjustment period, I also didn't want to lose subscribers because of spam filters. I created a “warming” campaign so that email filters could get used to my new domain and mark my emails as quality rather than spam before I started sending essays. This involved, first, letting you know on Substack that I was leaving—so you knew to check your spam folders—then, sending my first email from the new platform to small groups of people at a time, asking everyone to reply to the email (which trains the spam filters for everyone!).

Claude determined the order people should be emailed in, with the most active and engaged first. There was, however, one group of people who were not engaged at all—6,865 of my Substack subscribers hadn't opened a single email, clicked a single link, or otherwise shown any sign of life in the past year, or ever. These people could have marked me as spam years ago and never seen another email. Emailing them cold from a new domain would be a huge risk—that's a large chunk of people who could instantly mark me as spam and then my emails wouldn't reach anyone. But they also could just be using private emails that don't share data. So I paid $95 to run those addresses through ZeroBounce and check.

It determined that 5,604 of the emails were valid, split as follows:

- 5,357 valid emails (might have just turned off emails in the Substack app and then never seen a post)
    
- 53 read-it-later and newsletter app emails: Readwise Reader, Matter, Mailbrew, Feedly, Feedbin, Meco, Slick Inbox
    
- 63 privacy aliases: DuckDuckGo, Proton Pass/SimpleLogin, Firefox Relay, AnonAddy.
    
- 61 role addresses like hello@ or info@
    
- 70 others ZeroBounce couldn’t fully verify: 52 catch-all domains, 10 unknown, and 8 forwarding addresses
    

I kept those. But it also discovered 1,261 email addresses which were entirely defunct:

- 620 mailboxes no longer exist
    
- 248 full mailboxes nobody empties
    
- 148 domains that no longer exist, including 15 at Omnivore, a reading app that shut down
    
- 46 servers that don’t accept mail
    
- 12 typos like “[gnail.com](http://gnail.com/)”, which never received a single email
    
- 123 emails known for marking emails as spam
    
- 46 throwaway addresses from temporary-inbox sites
    
- 18 others: 6 likely spam traps, 3 flagged “toxic,” and 9 on global suppression lists
    

I removed these 1,261 people from my newsletter list when I moved over to the new platform, and put the other 5,604 non-responsive folks into the sixth and final wave of my warming campaign with a custom email reminding them that they subscribed through Substack, telling them that I was moving, and inviting them to stay subscribed if they wished. This is an incredibly good start—my overall open rate was 43% and more than 1,000 people replied to emails. And this with only six people marking emails as spam, and losing only 172 readers to unsubscribes and 149 to bounced emails. We're starting out with a very healthy baseline of 21,793 subscribers.

I cannot thank you enough for replying to emails and clicking links during my warming period, as well as rescuing my emails from your spam folders! And I really can't thank you enough for all of the nice emails you sent me. As a writer I don't often get feedback about my work, so it was incredibly affirming to have a thousand of you emailing me notes about how you've been here for years, how my work has resonated with you, and even writing me poems inspired by my work. I feel very affirmed and like I'm doing a good job at my work. Thank you, truly.

I'm also very excited to see how things progress from here, and with our own custom platform where we can do whatever we like. Claude has ushered in a new era for writers who before, had to rely on whatever the coders built for us, but can now build all of the worlds we imagine in our work. For the first time, the creative types can build the software, and it's going to be really interesting to see what we all create for the internet.

Personally, I remain bullish on writing and reading, for words printed into pamphlets and books, for a world with long attention spans, where we can sit quietly and read and think. Where we can engage with one another in long thoughtful comment sections, and create a community that continues to influence the world we can create.

This is a renaissance Substack started—for a brief moment they proved there was still a market for reading, and that people would pay to follow their favorite writers. Substack has evolved to meet a much wider market, but we don't have to. We can build for the readers only. For the more analog world we want. We can experiment with media that does something different, and I'm excited to explore what that might look like with all of you.

We _should_ "take back our attention." And we _should_ "own our own work." But no one is going to provide that for us. We have to build it ourselves. I'm excited to have the opportunity to do so.

Thank you so much for reading and supporting what we're creating with The Elysian,

Elle Griffin
