The pressure starts to arrive as soon as you’re past a two-pizza team. Planning is often where you feel it first. Nobody can say with any confidence what people will be working on in eight weeks, and the planning process is painful. Every. Single. Time.

Meanwhile, ownership is forming anyway—there’s the person who always picks up the billing bugs, the person everyone routes integration questions to. Standup has gotten long. Nobody understands the whole backlog.

This is usually the time when someone asks: Should we split into teams? Doing so could drive more ownership, visibility, and shard the planning process.

**So the organization reaches for the most obvious model: cell division.** One group of twelve becomes two groups of six, each with a lead, a charter, and a roadmap. Later those groups become four, then eight. The org chart branches.

That’s clean, but generally the wrong approach for most startups.

I’ve seen it play out both ways: teams that split at twelve and spent the next year negotiating across a boundary they’d invented, and teams that stayed unified well past the point of comfort until they effectively became fifteen teams instead of one. **A better model is budding.** When yeast reproduces, the parent cell doesn’t neatly split in half. A small daughter cell forms on the parent’s surface, grows until it can survive alone, and pinches off. The parent carries on being the parent.

Startup teams can grow the same way. Instead of dividing an existing team evenly, you keep one large, fluid body of engineers working on short-lived initiatives, and when a workstream stabilizes enough, you bud a small team off around it. Three or four people, a clear ongoing obligation, its own operational load.

The main, core team loses those people but stays otherwise intact.

Cell division assumes the work is already partitioned and stable. At a dozen people it usually isn’t. You’re still finding out what the product is, so any permanent boundary you draw is a bet placed when you know least about the thing you’re betting on.

Multiple backlogs, different sets of quarterly goals, a coordination tax on everything that crosses the line, and a lead on each side whose job is now partly to defend a scope. Conway’s law does the rest: the split you drew in the org shows up in the architecture, and architecture is much harder to unsplit than a team is.

The other cost is what you give up. The main advantage a 15-person engineering org has over a 200-person org is that it can be flexible and agile. When you split into separate missions, things inevitably start to fall through the cracks and emergent needs end up unstaffed unless you declare an emergency.

Budding gives you a test. A team should exist when there’s a stable, ongoing obligation to attach it to. Something with a pager. A recurring stream of requests. Its own backlog of P2s. A surface other people build on and file bugs against.

If there isn’t an ongoing obligation behind it, it’s probably still a project, and projects live with the rest of the team.

The implicit ownership from earlier is the best signal you have. The person who always picks up the billing bugs is telling you that billing has already stabilized into an ongoing obligation.

When a company makes a serious bet on something new, the reflex is to give it a dedicated team, because that’s how you signal it matters. But for startups, the new bet is the thing most likely to change shape or die. Four people can usually be redirected pretty easily. Once they’ve become a named team with a lead, OKRs, and six months of identity behind them, changing direction gets much harder.

In my experience, the boring work often stabilizes first. Think billing, integrations, data pipelines, enterprise features.

Budded teams should also be allowed to stay small. Two or three people owning billing is a healthy end state, not a stepping stone on the way to a twelve-person billing org.

It can feel chaotic. Ad hoc work can read as an absence of strategy even when there’s a coherent thesis behind the sequencing. “We’re staying flexible” is hard to distinguish from “we don’t know what we’re doing” unless someone keeps explaining why this week’s work builds on last week’s. But this is the way of startups, and the people already there mostly signed up for it.

Hiring is where it can bite. Candidates want to know what they’ll be working on, and a grab bag of short-lived initiatives is a much harder pitch than “you’ll own the data platform.” A team with a named surface is a recruiting asset. A core product team isn’t. This is why it’s important to hire people early on who are comfortable with ambiguity.

The same illegibility shows up internally at review time (if you’re doing them). Ownership stories are easy to write for someone on a budded team and hard for someone who spent two years on nine wildly different things.

And budding can turn into slow-motion cell division if you do it too eagerly. The first bud is the expensive one. At twelve engineers, three people leaving the main team is a quarter of the flexibility you were trying to protect. Four buds later, the parent is empty and you’ve arrived at the org you were avoiding, eighteen months later.

Of course, leaving the structure untouched indefinitely doesn’t work either. A body that never buds doesn’t stay one team. It becomes a dozen teams of one, with all of the implicit ownership and none of the structure, and that failure can be harder to spot because the org chart still looks flat and healthy.

The end-game for this process isn’t based on headcount; it’s more of a condition or a state. The core team exists to find the shape of the work, such that it stops being necessary once the shape is known: which typically aligns with PMF being found. At this point, the surface area is mapped, and a team is sitting on each part of it.

The core team is useful while the work is still taking shape; it shouldn’t survive once that stops being true. Meaning, you end up with roughly the org chart you might have drawn on day one, but the boundaries emerged from the work rather than being chosen up front. And you kept the ability to move sideways during the period when the company needed it most.
