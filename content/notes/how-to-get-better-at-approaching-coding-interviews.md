The article argues that coding interviews are easier to handle when you treat them as a process. It starts with mindset, then moves through clarifying the prompt, testing small examples, describing a brute-force solution, and improving it with patterns, data structures, or algorithm techniques.

## Reading notes

- Nerves come from uncertainty, and the article says interviewers usually want to see how you think, not just whether you already know the answer.
- If you blank out, it suggests linking the question to something you have done or being honest about what you do not know while showing interest in learning.
- Whiteboard algorithm interviews are presented as common because they reveal clarity, preparation, logic, and CS fundamentals, and they scale well for hiring.
- Good interview performance depends on strong fundamentals and lots of practice, so patterns become easier to recognize over time.
- The recommended workflow is to try a few examples, explain a brute-force approach, connect it to a pattern or technique, optimize it, and then check edge cases.
- Communication matters throughout the interview, so the candidate should keep talking, ask clarifying questions, and say when more context or a hint is needed.
- The article uses moving zeros to the end of an array as the main example for clarifying requirements, building test cases, and describing a brute-force solution.
- It recommends starting from the input and output, then using pseudocode to make the solution easier to adjust before writing code.
- Optimization comes from identifying the bottleneck and choosing a suitable data structure or algorithmic technique, such as a counter, a hash table, divide and conquer, recursion, memoization, or greedy choice.
- After a working solution, the article says to run through examples again by hand to catch mistakes and confirm the logic before coding it out fully.
