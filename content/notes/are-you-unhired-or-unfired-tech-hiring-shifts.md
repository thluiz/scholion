The article introduces two concepts: being "unfired," when someone still holds and performs a role that the company no longer truly needs, and being "unhired," when roles that would once have existed are never created. Using the Unhired Index, it shows that by late 2025 nine large software firms employed about 185,000 fewer people than their historical revenue–headcount relationship would predict, even as business kept growing. The text reviews competing explanations for this divergence, including AI, uncertainty, rate hikes, and the unwinding of 2021 over‑hiring, and argues that shocks such as the pandemic or AI investment spur firms to finally realize productivity gains made possible by earlier waves of technology and organizational change.

It highlights how remote work followed this pattern: the capacity existed for years but only became widespread when COVID forced adoption. AI is framed primarily as a shock that pushes companies to test how much they can do with existing staff, with its own further productivity gains likely to surface during future shocks. Meta is used as an example of a company that grew revenue and margins while reducing headcount, confirming that a much larger operation can run with fewer employees. Leaders like Mark Zuckerberg and Elon Musk embraced leaner organizations early, and Microsoft has now offered voluntary exits to tens of thousands of employees. At the same time, major tech firms pour money into capital expenditure rather than payroll, underscoring that industry success no longer guarantees job security. For people early in their careers, the main threat is being unhired when openings never appear; for those in comfortable corporate roles, it is becoming unfired without realizing it, which makes it crucial to assess whether one’s work would be replaced, redistributed, or simply vanish without affecting the company’s growth.
