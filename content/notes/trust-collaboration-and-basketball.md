The post argues that trust on teams means giving someone work and believing it will be done well enough that you do not need to check it. Trust does not mean working alone; it also includes expecting people to collaborate, ask for help, seek feedback, and change course when needed.

Basketball is used as the main analogy. Passing the ball is like handing off work, while checks and approval gates are compared to taking the ball back. The post says leaders should match work to the right person, accept results that differ from their own approach, and help teammates become more trustable through coaching rather than constant oversight.

## Reading notes

- Trust means handing off work and not needing to check it before it is done.
- Trust also includes collaboration, asking for help, and changing one’s mind when needed.
- Checking work or requiring approval signals a lack of trust.
- The basketball analogy frames handoffs as passing the ball and collaboration as being open to receive it.
- A checkpoint or gate is compared to taking the ball back from the person who was given the work.
- Leaders should ask how much they can trust a specific person with a specific piece of work right now.
- Giving someone work they are not ready for can create a trap for both the person and the manager.
- Repeated checks can lead teams to build systems that enforce low trust.
- Approval processes reduce ownership and engagement because people do not fully own work that must be checked by someone else.
- Leaders need to accept work that is different from how they would have done it.
- Mistakes can be part of learning, and leaders are sometimes simply wrong.
- If someone cannot be trusted with the work, the options are to remove them from the team or coach them until they can be trusted.
- A leader’s job includes making teammates more trustable.
- Trust has to come first, because new teammates cannot earn it otherwise.
- High-trust teams keep the ball moving through constant collaboration and shared learning.
