The text revisits the “monkey” metaphor of delegation and argues that the usual advice about keeping responsibility on subordinates’ shoulders fails in practice. Through an example of a startup co‑founder helping her marketing team upload videos, the author shows how employees can successfully push responsibility back to managers when they themselves have little to lose. The core claim is that leaders often delegate work but not the consequences, which leaves them worrying more than their staff and absorbing the real risks.

The author defines “worry” as a combination of rewards and punishments tied to outcomes. In the story, the co‑founder has strong personal stakes in the expo’s success, while the two marketers receive mostly fixed salaries and only small bonuses, so they care less about the result. The piece argues that professional employees deliberately try to move monkeys back up the hierarchy and that managers must ensure subordinates suffer or benefit proportionally from project outcomes; otherwise responsibility slides upward and the team structure is hollow.

- The article references “Who’s Got the Monkey?” and its idea that good managers keep responsibility with subordinates instead of accepting excuses.
- The author claims this principle usually fails because most managers still end up carrying the responsibility their subordinates try to return.

- She feels that if she does not help, their expo mission will fail, which she reads as her own failure as a co‑founder.
- Reframed in “monkey” terms, she initially delegated the expo work, but the team sent the monkey back by asking for help, and she kept it on her shoulders.
- The expo outcome would then depend on her remembering the task, giving the team a ready excuse if things went wrong.
- The author notes that experienced employees usually try hard to send monkeys back to bosses, while only junior or incompetent workers simply follow orders without defending themselves.
- Seasoned experts are described as knowing how to manage their managers by pushing responsibility upward.
- In this situation, the author argues the co‑founder’s mistake was failing to make her employees worry enough about the expo results.
- She had clearly communicated that the expo outcome was important and hired them with the expectation of bringing back leads or similar results.
- Despite this, the marketers worried less than she did, shown by their lack of constant follow‑up calls and poor planning of the video uploads.
- “Worry” is defined as the combined effect of rewards and punishments that follow success or failure, regardless of the terminology used.
- The co‑founder knew the personal rewards of a successful expo and the personal risks of failure but did not transfer these stakes proportionally to her team.
- According to her description, the marketers received a monthly salary and some per‑lead bonuses, with bonuses much smaller than salary.
- The author questions how employees can be expected to care deeply about leads when the pay structure signals that steady salary matters more than performance.
- The text states that leaders typically break down big objectives into smaller ones and delegate them, but they often omit delegating the related consequences.

- The proposed goal is to make every team member gain and lose in proportion to their part in the outcome.
- The author concludes that without this proportional sharing of consequences, a group cannot function as a real team.
