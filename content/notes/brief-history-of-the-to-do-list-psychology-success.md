The article uses John Tierney and Roy F. Baumeister’s *Willpower* to show how the to-do list can produce both fulfillment and frustration. It links the history of lists to cultural examples, psychological research, and the problem of setting goals that compete with one another.

## Reading notes

- The book’s third chapter follows the to-do list from the Bible’s creation story to Benjamin Franklin’s virtue lists and Drew Carey’s productivity methods.
- Franklin’s attempt to manage several virtues at once shows how goals can collide and create unhappiness instead of action.
- A Pentagon exercise with generals suggests that many people struggle to state priorities in a short, clear form.
- The example of the lone woman general turns into a simple rule: list priorities, then cross out the lower ones.
- Drew Carey’s approach, through David Allen, stresses next actions rather than abstract goals.
- The Zeigarnik effect is described as the mind’s tendency to keep unfinished tasks active until a specific plan is made.
- Recent research in the article says the unconscious wants the conscious mind to define time, place, and opportunity.
- The closing advice is to keep the list short, specific, actionable, and non-conflicting.
