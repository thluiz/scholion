Loukides argues that a full-stack developer should have broad familiarity across the systems that shape an application: front end, design, hardware, networking, cloud services, data, and tooling. The point is not mastery of everything, but enough range to understand tradeoffs and work effectively with specialists.

## Reading notes

- The usual definitions of the full stack are too narrow or too grandiose, and they miss things like source control, data infrastructure, and distributed computing.
- The stack starts with the older LAMP model, but the components have changed, with new servers, databases, and languages taking part.
- JavaScript and CSS are treated as serious parts of the platform, and the MEAN stack shows how the browser and server can now share a JavaScript-based environment.
- Developers should understand design and user experience well enough to discuss what can be implemented effectively with designers.
- Performance depends on how software interacts with hardware, including SQL queries, CPU execution, and disk caching.
- Networking matters because it affects what happens in real applications and has a large impact on performance.
- Cloud development requires understanding Amazon, its APIs, billing, services, and resilience in the face of outages.
- Data-driven applications often involve Hadoop and machine-learning libraries such as Mahout or Scikit-learn.
- Modern tooling includes source control, automated testing, and continuous deployment, and Git or SVN should not be optional.
- The author prefers the T-shaped developer model: broad knowledge across areas, with deep expertise in one.
- The goal is not to replace all specializations, but to work across them and collaborate productively with other teams.
- Developers who understand more disciplines can build better applications and communicate better with designers, front-end and back-end colleagues, and others.
