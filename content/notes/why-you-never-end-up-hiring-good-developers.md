The piece argues that many technical interviews overvalue current knowledge, whiteboard performance, degrees, famous employers, and personal familiarity. Instead, it says hiring should focus on whether a candidate can learn the job, keep improving, communicate clearly, and work well with others without bias.

## Reading notes

- It says interviewers often test irrelevant skills and should instead assess growth potential.
- It rejects obscure syntax questions, whiteboard coding, and memorized algorithms as poor hiring signals.
- It says degrees and brand-name employers are weak predictors of engineering ability.
- It warns against hiring friends and family because relationships create bias and obligation.
- It proposes two core questions: can the person do the job, and will they get better at it?
- It treats relevant experience as helpful but not decisive, and values learning speed and increasing responsibility.
- It highlights “smart and gets things done” as a useful hiring standard.
- It says engineers need both technical understanding and clear communication.
- It values honesty about what a candidate does not know and sees confident guessing as a bad sign.
- It frames the interview as a conversation, not an interrogation, and says stress makes answers less useful.
- It allows exceptions for very junior candidates, career changers, and highly specialized large teams.
- It says the real question is whether you want to work with the person, while warning against bias.
- It separates productive work relationships from friendship.
- It rejects jerks and says no amount of brilliance can justify poisoning team morale.
- It rejects “team fit” because it often disguises bias and sameness.
- It says lack of diversity is bad for a company because it usually means hiring has become narrow and self-reinforcing.
- It closes by saying better hiring is hard, slow, and worth the effort.
