The post argues that group chat should be a limited tool, not the default way an organization communicates. Used for quick back-and-forth, urgent alerts, fun, and a sense of belonging, it can help. Used all day as the main channel, it pushes people into fatigue, anxiety, interruptions, and shallow discussion.

## Reading notes

- Group chat is presented as useful for quick idea exchange, urgent alerts, fun at work, and helping remote people feel included.
- Using chat as the primary communication method leads to mental fatigue and exhaustion.
- Real-time chat encourages an ASAP culture and makes ordinary matters feel urgent.
- People may fear missing out or miss their chance to speak if they are not watching constantly.
- One-line-at-a-time conversation makes it harder to develop full thoughts and make decisions.
- Chat can create implied consensus when people assume everyone saw and agreed with what was discussed.
- The format encourages knee-jerk replies, pile-ons, rambling, and repetition.
- Piping in frequent updates can over-inform everyone in real time and interrupt work many times a day.
- Constant unread counts make people feel behind and create return anxiety.
- Large unread numbers and broad room badges hide what the conversation is actually about.
- Keeping chat open all day produces constant context switching and attention residue.
- It is difficult to review and reference important chat conversations later.
- Conversations in chat lack context because they are detached from the source material.
- Presence indicators pressure people to stay online and suggest that being unavailable means not working.
- Chat works poorly across time zones because it is built around now, while different people’s now is different.
- The post recommends “real-time sometimes, asynchronous most of the time.”
- Important conversations, announcements, and feedback should usually move to asynchronous formats.
- The author advises small chat groups, short use, summaries instead of drip updates, and long uninterrupted stretches for focused work.
- The broader claim is that communication tools shape behavior and can cause stress if their design encourages constant interruption.
