## Reading notes

- A traveler puzzled by the high cost of European paper notebooks decides to make his own in the US, despite paper being a commoditized, difficult market where mills have closed and profits are hard to find.
- Many notebook makers lean on new task‑management systems or digital syncing, but Aaron Draplin’s project focused on making simple paper notebooks rather than promoting a methodology or cloud features.
- Research by Dr. Constantine Sedikides and Dr. Tim Wildschut at the University of Southampton links nostalgia triggers (old songs, photos) to heightened feelings of social connectedness, even when the remembered people are distant in place or time.
- Nostalgia historically was seen as sickness or homesickness, but recent work suggests it can be emotionally beneficial and counter isolation by emotionally reconnecting people across time.
- Field Notes notebooks emerged from Draplin’s love of “junk”: estate‑sale finds, old stickers, matchbooks, large signs, and especially vintage farmer notebooks given out by seed or insurance companies.
- Draplin has amassed hundreds or thousands of agricultural memo books, which directly inspired Field Notes’ design, typography, and voice, evoking mid‑20th‑century American ephemera and relatives who carried pocket notebooks.
- The author connects Field Notes’ aesthetic to memories of a grandfather who hated his paper‑mill job but tinkered and invented at home, and says the brand calls up a vanished feeling of “America” and personal connections.
- Field Notes’ success is attributed to Draplin’s reputation as a designer, Jim Coudal’s marketing skills, charming product videos, an innovative subscription model bringing recurring revenue to stationery, and its deliberate use of nostalgia as a design tool.
- In another post, the author notes discovering Prettier, a code formatter that automatically fixes style issues rather than blocking commits with lint errors, contrasting it with linters that refuse to proceed until developers conform.
- The writer expresses discomfort when tools dictate behavior (“You need to do x before I’ll do what you want”), preferring tools that serve the programmer by handling style corrections instead of nagging.

- Pings on Android are improved: each one‑to‑one Ping gets its own color and profile picture, group Pings have a colored toolbar listing participants, and tapping an avatar shows that person’s time zone and details.
- The Android app also adds gestures like swiping right to go back, shows to‑do list descriptions, and smooths drafting and saving messages and comments.

- Claire Lew promotes a “Don’t Be the Last to Know” workshop at Basecamp HQ, built from three years of work with almost 300 companies and 15,000 employees using Know Your Company.
- She argues software alone can’t create an open, honest environment; leaders must change daily behavior and learn specific practices to avoid blindspots and encourage real employee candor.
- The workshop promises a previously unshared, end‑to‑end playbook on not being “the last to know,” based on CEO interviews and aggregated data from companies in over 15 countries.
- Attendees will practice techniques with live coaching, with more time than a typical 30‑minute talk allows to address individual situations and implementation challenges.

- In a separate essay, the author describes his two‑year‑old daughter’s frustration with coloring inside the lines and links it to Robert Sapolsky’s discussion of the “winner effect,” where winning increases chances of future wins and losing leads to withdrawal.
- Research cited shows even artificially induced wins (e.g., a sedated opponent mouse) boost future winning odds, while repeated losses discourage further competition and can redirect aggression toward weaker targets.
- The toy company Melissa & Doug is highlighted for products like Water Wow, a reusable coloring book with water‑activated pages that reveal preprinted colors, giving toddlers the experience of successfully “painting” without real skill.
- Water Wow is framed as a way to hack the winner effect for children: by faking a small win, it builds confidence to keep working on real coloring tasks despite limited motor skills.
- The author generalizes this idea to adults learning skills, running businesses, or tackling tools like video editing, arguing that they often feel like his daughter failing to color within the lines.
- He suggests product designers ask where customers currently feel repeated failure, and consider creating “Water Wow”‑like experiences by handling hard parts for users to give them early wins.
- At Highrise, onboarding emails were changed from generic tips to a direct offer to import new customers’ contact files, recognizing that importing data is a major pain point for many.
- The Highrise team invites new users to simply reply with a file so they can help or fully perform the import, using their experience with diverse data formats to make the process feel almost effortless.
- Internally, the Highrise team used Slack channels like Buzz (for positive tweets) and Signup (for new accounts) to surface small successes and counterbalance stress from earlier customer churn and rumors of shutdown.
- These visible “little wins” are described as important boosts to morale when other areas of the business were struggling, another way of harnessing the winner effect.
- The post ends by asking readers how they’ve hacked the winner effect in their own lives or for customers, and again points to the author’s YouTube channel for related content.
- Another piece introduces the Chicago Printmakers Collaborative (CPC) and its founder, printmaker Deborah Maris Lader, via a transcript of The Distance podcast.
- Deborah recounts always identifying as an artist, switching from sculpture to printmaking at Cornell after one class, and loving printmaking’s technical, process‑oriented nature.
- After teaching and heading the printmaking department at Indiana University–Fort Wayne, she moved to Chicago for personal reasons and found there was no community print shop where artists could work together.
- Seeing printmakers as social and supportive by nature, she decided to start a shared workshop and gallery in 1989 by buying a defunct print shop advertised for $3,000, exactly what she had saved.
- Deborah salvaged one combination press from that shop and named a later lithography press “Edith” after her grandmother, whose small inheritance happened to match the price of the press.
- She structured CPC as a business rather than a nonprofit to avoid board‑driven censorship during an era of high‑profile arts controversies (Mapplethorpe, Serrano’s “Piss Christ,” and a painting of Chicago mayor Harold Washington).
- Rejecting grants meant CPC had to survive on earned revenue: affordable but paid studio memberships, competitive‑priced classes, and a gallery, forcing constant experimentation with what worked financially.
- Over time, other organizations began asking her how she sustained a print shop without donations, and she emphasized that every participant pays something for the space or services.
- A decade in, CPC moved to a larger, trendier neighborhood with a dedicated upstairs gallery, but higher costs led to losses until Deborah converted that gallery space into four lockable artist studios with 6–12 month leases.
- Those private studios created steady, predictable income, and Deborah reframed the main workshop as both studio and gallery, improving the business model.
- Still dissatisfied with the fit, she later found a foreclosed antique store called the Penn Dutchman nearby, in poor condition with water damage, burst pipes, and unsafe floors.
- Buying a foreclosure required nearly a year and substantial legal work; Deborah benefited from her husband’s experience as a developer and architect familiar with Chicago permits and inspections.
- She secured city small business improvement funds to help with renovation and personally obsessed over redesigning the space, eventually owning a properly coded facility with a new roof, skylight, better equipment layout, accessible bathroom, and even a drinking fountain.
- The build‑out involved dealing with frustrating bureaucratic demands, such as a wrongly required street tree and punching an unnecessary window into a storage room mislabelled as a bedroom on plans.
- Deborah describes moments of wanting to quit under these burdens, including her husband suggesting she could just keep a press somewhere and focus on her own work rather than managing a community.
- After weighing pros and cons, she realized the printmakers’ community nourished her and that running a small business requires strong passion beyond a spreadsheet model.
- She likens herself at times to set builder or janitor, spending much time on emotional support and logistics for others, but still likes the role despite its difficulty.
- CPC’s business model lets it fund community events like a free printmaking day where people printed images of hope or protest on shirts, which Deborah says was expensive but deeply rewarding.
- The story is framed in the context of potential federal cuts to the NEA and NEH, presenting CPC’s self‑sustaining model and Deborah’s commitment as particularly important.
- Another short essay urges product makers to ask what people will stop doing when they adopt a new product, not just what new capabilities they gain.
- Habits, familiarity, and anxiety make existing workflows hard to abandon, so products must overcome those bonds by addressing the switch from previous tools directly.
- The author emphasizes identifying the jobs and behaviors a product replaces, understanding how hard it is to leave those behind, and designing ways to ease that transition.
- A longer critique examines Silicon Valley’s fixation on exponential growth and “potential,” where revenue and profits are secondary to steep curves and viral spread.
- Companies like Salesforce are given as examples of firms growing large on the promise of future potential while showing little profit, modeling virus‑like expansion and acquisition.
- The piece argues that growth targets and shareholder expectations lead to gradual erosion of product integrity, illustrated by Angry Birds’ evolution into an in‑app‑purchase‑heavy “swamp of dark patterns.”
- The “split‑pea soup” analogy is used: removing three peas per can seems harmless but repeated quarter after quarter eventually leaves no peas, mapping to incremental degradations driven by continuous growth quotas.
- Corporate slogans like Google’s “Don’t be evil” are portrayed as brittle against long‑term market pressures, accumulating exceptions until they lose force as guiding principles.
- Incentives such as favorable tax rates on capital gains over income are cited as pushing founders and boards toward big exit payoffs rather than sustainable profits (e.g., preferring a huge event taxed at 15% to steady $10M/year at higher rates).
- The author notes that when 37signals trimmed its product line to focus solely on Basecamp, commentators insisted it was irrational to kill any revenue‑producing business, treating constant extraction and growth as a fiduciary duty.
- Venture capital diversification is described as treating startups as a fund “basket” where failures are expected and desired, driving incubators and accelerators to push founders into extreme, viral growth strategies.
- Engagement metrics are highlighted as modern euphemisms for capturing eyeballs, with the ecosystem quick to adopt sanitized language that obscures extractive motives.
- The essay asks what future iterations of this system will look like if current trends continue and worries about everyone being pushed through funnels into servile consumer roles.
- As a partial analogy, it points to pharmaceuticals, where patent monopolies eventually expire and generics spread benefits more broadly, and asks what a “generic” Twitter or Facebook might look like.
- Open source is cited as one way closed‑source advances have been turned into generics, enabling many new projects to build on previous work rather than preserving monopoly control.
- The piece argues that risk for founders has been softened by abundant capital and a culture that celebrates failure and restarts, which can weaken the sense of responsibility for each specific venture.
- It suggests reintroducing some personal liability (short of total ruin) so decision‑makers feel real consequences and act more responsibly.
- The author criticizes startup cultures that hide ethical questions under perks and distractions (foosball tables, game rooms), encouraging people not to dwell on the implications of their work.
- Uber is used as an example of how algorithm‑driven rating systems treat drivers like tightly spec’d machine parts, where falling below a rating threshold brings penalties with little human context.
- The essay notes the way some customers, including the author previously, valued frictionless, faceless interactions but later recognized that removing human contact has social costs and contributes to isolation.
- It challenges the narrative that gig workers are “independent business owners,” pointing out that many must drive 60–80 hours to get by, and that their “choice” is constrained.
- Algorithmic decision‑making (e.g., Facebook’s newsfeed) is portrayed as extracting engagement through outrage and fake news, and as hard to resist given platform monopolies on communication and social life.
- Tech philanthropy (like large foundations by wealthy founders) is described as an old pattern of “giving back” that doesn’t replace the need for a system where society isn’t dependent on the generosity of a few winners.
- The piece criticizes “too big to be conscientious” tech conglomerates whose scale makes it unrealistic for slogans like “Don’t be evil” to permeate every division’s decisions.
- It calls out justifications like “natural monopolies” and “network effects” as rhetorical covers for entrenching power and avoiding real competition at the top while workers compete in a “race to the bottom.”
- The author argues that ad‑driven, engagement‑maximizing products can become exploitative in pennies‑for‑workers, billions‑for‑founders patterns and that consumers enable this by continuing to buy and use these services.
- Citing Douglas Rushkoff’s phrase “Throwing Rocks at the Google Bus,” the essay calls for a new operating system for startups that normalizes leaving money and growth on the table.
- It advocates a strong counterculture, echoing 1960s spirit, to build ethical, non‑exponential companies that benefit more than a small elite and push the world in a healthier direction.
- Another post examines the phrase “full stack developer,” arguing it has become so vague and overloaded that it can hurt developers who apply it to themselves, especially those early in their careers.
- The author questions whether someone with only a few years of experience can truly do front‑to‑back work well, as opposed to merely cobbling together basics, and suggests such claims invite skepticism.
- The enormous range of possible “stacks” in 2017 (front‑end languages, back‑end frameworks, databases, mobile platforms, etc.) makes “full stack” increasingly ill‑defined.
- Knowing and articulating one’s limitations is framed as a sign of honesty and a professional strength, not impostor syndrome or weakness.
- The piece suggests describing oneself in concrete, opinionated terms (e.g., “a proud, productive Ruby on Rails programmer”) rather than generic buzzwords that communicate no preferences or focus.
- Overly long skills lists paired with “full stack” labels are portrayed as “quantity over quality” cover stories that dilute the impact of a few real strengths.
- The advice is to keep skill presentations short and focused, which shows clear thinking and conviction about what matters and can lead to a more satisfying long‑term programming career.
- The last essay tells of a company that helps car dealers offload used vehicles by buying them directly from lots and redistributing them, offering a reliable alternative to wholesale auctions.
- Dealers send cars to auction when models don’t match regional demand (e.g., Subarus performing better in Colorado than Georgia), making the used‑car distribution system inefficient and somewhat random.
- The new company promises less hassle and more predictable outcomes than auctions, but faces resistance from sales managers who would be the decision‑makers.
- The post argues that these managers value auction trips as quasi‑vacations: time away from the lot, chances to socialize with peers, and a break from routine.
- Because the financial gains from the new system go to owners or shareholders rather than the managers personally, the “perks” of auction trips outweigh the appeal of saving the dealership money.
- The key point is that the company’s true competition is not auctions as institutions but the auction experience and habits around it.
- More broadly, the essay observes that products often compete with existing behaviors, rituals, and experiences, not just with direct rival products, so understanding those forces is crucial when trying to change how people work.
