Chuck Palahniuk uses friends’ real-life stories and examples from books and films to show how people cope with fear and grief, and how writers can use those patterns. He describes strategies like imagining multiple versions of oneself across time, displacing fear onto vulnerable objects, and using singing, repetition, or even screams when language fails. These behaviors both reveal character and heighten emotional impact in narrative.

## Reading notes

- Suzy Vitello conceives of herself as three Suzys: present, three days in the past, and three days in the future, which gives her distance from immediate crises.
- By choosing to inhabit past or future Suzy, she reminds herself that problems didn’t exist days ago and will likely ease soon, which curbs impulsive reactions.
- Palahniuk recalls learning this from Suzy in Tom Spanbauer’s 1990s workshop, where she was also writing about a woman with alter-ego advisors.
- He notes that Suzy’s concept may have influenced his creation of Tyler Durden in *Fight Club*, linking the idea to fractured personalities.
- Trapped at home in a snowstorm with wood for two days, he uses Suzy’s strategy by picturing himself three days earlier at lunch with an editor and three days later mailing fake severed hands.
- He frames Suzy’s idea as what a strong story should offer: a concept or strategy the reader can use to cope.
- A smoke jumper friend trained with a Hostess cherry pie he had to carry constantly and eat at graduation, turning it into something precious.
- The pie tradition shifts trainees’ fear away from their own bodies toward protecting the fragile pie, even while jumping from planes.
- Another friend, as a child crossing a dark cemetery with a coconut candle for her mother, fixated on protecting the candle from a pursuing stranger.
- She ran through the graveyard, convinced the man wanted to harm her candle, and escaped through a fence while still cradling it.
- A different friend crashed her car onto a power tower’s guy wire, ending up nearly vertical, yet her main concern was for the bedding plants on the backseat.
- Palahniuk generalizes that in real danger, people often transfer fear onto a more vulnerable object whose survival becomes the priority.
- He argues that such displacement is a useful fiction detail: readers may care more about a character who is protecting a pet, object, or token while in danger.
- He cites his novel *Rant*, where a girl focuses on hiding a gold coin during a rape, convincing herself the attacker only wants the coin.
- He admires Amy Hempel’s “The Harvest,” where a man reassures an injured woman but laments his ruined sweater, showing displacement of concern.
- Palahniuk summarizes a comic principle: truth is funny, and ugly truth combines greater humor with heartbreak.
- Another coping strategy he highlights is singing, which occupies the mind and diverts overwhelming emotion in moments of terror or sadness.
- In *2001: A Space Odyssey*, HAL’s death scene gains power when the computer sings “Daisy Bell,” a simple love song that clashes with its fear and shutdown.
- He points to the final episode of *The Mary Tyler Moore Show*, where the cast copes with ending the series by singing “It’s a Long Way to Tipperary.”
- The seemingly arbitrary, absurd songs in these scenes intensify the terror or pathos rather than undercutting it.
- He recalls a dying friend whose aunt asked everyone to sing “Take Me Out to the Ball Game,” said to be the man’s childhood favorite.
- The group repeated the lighthearted song softly, over and over by the hospital bed, creating an emotionally devastating contrast he remembers decades later.
- Palahniuk labels both the rescue of trivial objects and the singing of silly songs as examples of what Chelsea Cain calls “self-comforting behavior.”
- He advises writers to show self-comforting behavior at a story’s emotional high points, when fear or sadness feels unbearable.
- He notes that this can be intensified by a breakdown in language, such as stutters or repeated phrases.
- In his story "Romance," the narrator cycles through “life goes around” multiple times before stating an emotional core belief.
- He compares this to Bill Withers repeating “I know” before delivering the central line about leaving “the young thing” alone.
- Repetition, he argues, builds tension by delaying a key statement and suggests emotion too strong for smooth articulation.
- He urges writers to try such repetition in stories, saying it works powerfully to depict overwhelming feeling.
- The final conflict in *Alien* interests him because of how the dialogue degrades from full sentences to a scream.
- He notes Ripley’s premature line “I got you, you son-of-a-bitch,” followed by her singing “You Are My Lucky Star,” as a kind of protective chant.
- The song itself shrinks into the word “lucky” repeated mantra-like, marking a slide from sentence to chorus to single word.
- The last vocalization in the scene is a scream, which he reads as language dissolving completely into an animal sound before action finishes the story.
- Later, Ripley’s recorded log uses formal language to bridge into the future and echoes the mechanical text that opened the film.
- He points out an inaccuracy in the log: Ripley says she’ll be rescued within weeks, but in the series timeline she is found over fifty years later.
- From this he concludes that spoken language is an inaccurate way to communicate, that singing might be more accurate, and that a scream is the most honest.
- He enjoys punk songs that move from clever lyrics toward screaming, singling out Jim Carroll’s “People Who Died” as a witty patter song punctuated by screamed “Died!”
- He mentions Margaret Atwood’s essay about happy endings, which includes the repeated line “John and Mary die,” as another use of stark repetition.
- He describes his own short story “The Collector” (later called "The Merchant" in the collection *Make Something Up*), about a man who kills to record and sell screams as sound effects.
- The killer builds a huge digital archive of screams and is valued by film foley artists for his ability to discern subtle differences among them.
- Palahniuk suggests, without confirming, that the story could allegorize how news media profit from recorded suffering.
- He promotes Suzy Vitello’s novel *The Moment Before* and an event where he, Suzy, and Chelsea Cain will read “adult bedtime stories” in Seattle.
- As homework, he asks readers to find narratives where language devolves into singing or single-word repetition.
- He also encourages identifying scenes where characters project their danger onto objects, and where self-comforting behaviors appear.
- He offers examples like Jane Fonda’s character in *Klute* singing an unlikely religious hymn while stoned and alone, hinting at her background.
- He cites Nick Carraway’s consoling flashback after Gatsby’s grave scene as another narrative self-comforting move.
- He closes wishing readers warmth and expressing gratitude that they read his work.
