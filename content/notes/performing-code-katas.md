The post describes katas as a discipline that helps software craftspeople build muscle memory, practice logical thought, and try familiar ideas from a different angle. At 8th Light, kata work can be a warm-up before the day’s work or a way to regain clarity on a hard problem, and polished katas may be performed live for peers as entertainment.

## Reading notes

- Katas are presented as a practice for preserving and refining skills through repetition.
- In software, they are used to build muscle memory, exercise reasoning, and approach familiar ideas differently.
- 8th Light treats katas as part of craftsmanship, and Mike Ebert uses them to warm up or regain clarity.
- A kata performance is described as entertainment, not as a test of mastery.
- The suggested length is five to ten minutes, with seven or more minutes seen as more realistic.
- Performances should begin and end with an introduction and a closing that states the performer’s name and kata.
- A large font, minimal scrolling, and a quick projector check are recommended.
- White background with black text is preferred, and a custom presentation theme can reduce distractions.
- Fast typing matters, but accuracy still matters, and copy-paste should be avoided during the performance.
- Keyboard shortcuts should replace mouse use whenever possible.
- Each kata should have one clear theme that gives the audience a small lesson to take away.
- The performer should keep code, tests, and test runs visible at all times.
- A kata can start from scratch or from an existing codebase.
- The performer must choose between speaking, music, or silence, with each option used intentionally.
- The author says technique should be flawless, especially when using test-driven design or a new language.
- The post uses Sandro Padin’s Fizzbuzz kata in Clojure as an example of theme and music working together.
- The closing compares kata performances to songs and says the goal is to raise standards and encourage performances worth remembering.
