The article says an MVP works when teams balance speed and depth. It stresses early feedback from knowledgeable people, user analytics, and A/B testing to validate choices, while staying alert to market shifts and competitor moves.

## Reading notes

- Early input from stakeholders who understand the space helps define the MVP before development gets too far.
- User feedback and behavior tracking are central to validated learning, but teams should focus on the metrics that matter most.
- A/B testing lets teams compare product behaviors in real time and iterate without shipping a full new version.
- The product should stay flexible because the market can shift before launch, and competitors may gain traction too.
- The MVP can be broken into smaller layers, starting with wireframes and then moving to click dummies and core features.
- Each release should add the fewest changes needed to reach a viable next version.
- Feature creep and last-minute requests can drain morale and make the product unstable.
- Managers and executives should protect priorities and keep teams focused on shipping and feedback.
- Developers should avoid spending too much time on non-mission-critical refactoring before release.
- Third-party components can speed up delivery and be replaced later if needed.
- Hiring experienced developers early can be cheaper than relying on interns when rework and delays are counted.
- The article says there is no exact science to deciding when to ship, so teams need judgment and confidence in their vision.
