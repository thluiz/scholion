Psychogeography is presented as the study of how cities shape feeling and conduct, with Guy Debord’s definition tying it to the effects of geographical environments on individuals. The page traces it from the Lettrist International and Situationist International to later literary, artistic, activist, and academic uses, while stressing that practice matters more than theory alone.

## Reading notes

- It focuses on urban environments, interpersonal connections to places, and arbitrary routes.
- It was developed by the Lettrist International and the Situationist International, under Marxist and anarchist influence.
- Debord defines it as the study of the effects of geographical environment on emotions and behavior.
- The dérive is the main tactic, described as drifting through the city and following its attractions.
- Détournement is linked to psychogeography through the use of pre-found elements that are subverted.
- Early discussions appear in Potlatch and in Chtcheglov’s “Formulary for a New Urbanism.”
- Chtcheglov’s text and Debord’s later writings connect psychogeography to unitary urbanism and criticism of contemporary architecture.
- Debord and Asger Jorn produced psychogeographic maps of Paris by cutting and rearranging existing maps.
- The page says psychogeography later spread into contemporary art, literature, urban exploration, and mobile applications.
- British writers such as Iain Sinclair and Peter Ackroyd are presented as major later references.
- The page lists groups, noted psychogeographers, and mobile apps associated with dérives.
