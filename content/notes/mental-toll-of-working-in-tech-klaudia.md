## Reading notes

- The article follows a previous, very personal burnout post that unexpectedly became the author’s most popular piece and drew many comments from people with similar struggles.
- The strong response made her realize how widespread burnout is in the tech community and motivated her to start a mental health series.
- Her goal is to describe common mental threats in tech without feeding doomscrolling, and to normalize these struggles as something many people share.

- During her first tech internship as a writer, she constantly doubted her abilities, overchecked tasks, and felt she did not deserve to work among highly intelligent colleagues.
- She was afraid to ask questions for fear of looking silly, until she saw that even a respected PM was improvising and did not always know the answers.
- This realization showed her that others also lack complete certainty and sometimes stay silent to avoid embarrassment.
- She describes an updated "impostor syndrome 2.0" in the AI era, where people compare themselves not only to peers but also to AI tools.
- Inner doubts shift to fears that AI could do one’s work, make a role unnecessary, or reveal that companies might prefer using AI instead of paying a human.
- She admits she does not yet have a solution for this AI-flavored impostor feeling and sees it as an old fear in a new form that people are still learning to handle.

- She references another author’s post about developers’ fears and focuses on FOMO as it appears in tech.
- In this context, FOMO comes from the constant stream of new tools, approaches, and skills that seem essential to learn each year.
- Social feeds amplify this by showing others building impressive projects and mastering unfamiliar technologies, prompting endless scrolling and bookmarking.
- People often accumulate long lists of courses they suspect they will never start or finish.
- This state feeds into learning paralysis: information is abundant and accessible, but the sheer number of choices can freeze action.
- She recalls spending hours comparing courses and trying to design the perfect learning path and skill set as a tech writer instead of actually learning or practicing.

- Perfectionism kept this very article in draft for months, because she wanted it to be helpful, relatable, well-written, and engaging all at once.
- She set very high standards for herself and became extremely self-critical, which made it harder to finish and publish.
- The longer she delayed, the more pressure she felt to return with something exceptionally good, turning postponement into a reinforcing loop.
- She notes how perfectionism can drive people to over-polish work instead of sharing it early for feedback.
- It can also cause people to abandon activities they enjoy because they believe they will never reach a high enough level.
- As an example, she took up painting again after years of avoiding it, partly because she felt she would never be as talented as famous artists.
- She reflects that this is a sad outcome, since enjoying the process should matter even if the result is not extraordinary.
- She invites readers to consider what they are delaying or denying themselves due to fears of not being good enough.

- She defines mental overload as having too many open tabs, both digitally and mentally.
- A typical tech workday might include code reviews, Slack messages, multiple meetings, production alerts, half-finished tasks, and a constantly growing backlog.
- After work, everyday chores still demand attention, which collectively can be very draining.
- This accumulation of responsibilities makes people feel like they are running on low battery for extended periods.

- She distinguishes burnout, which comes from prolonged high pressure, heavy workload, and insufficient rest, from boreout, which arises from chronic under-stimulation.
- Boreout involves boredom, lack of challenge, and feeling stuck with tasks that seem meaningless.
- From the outside, burnout and boreout can appear similar in symptoms such as tiredness, disengagement, and lost motivation.
- Their roots differ: burnout stems from too much demand, while boreout stems from too little.
- She links her earlier thoughts about staying in Iceland herding sheep to boreout, which emerged as she imagined offloading most of her work to AI.
- This leads to questions about where her expertise fits, what challenges remain, and whether her role could shrink to managing AI agents.

- The AI section opens with her admitting she worries about her job security because of AI on an ongoing basis.
- She wonders whether AI will replace her, reshape her profession, or cause it to disappear, and how she can adapt and stay ahead.
- She assumes many people in tech now occasionally have similar concerns.
- Under the umbrella of AI-related threats, she names AI anxiety and an identity crisis about who one is if no longer defined by a tech role.
- She highlights AI fatigue as an under-discussed issue: feeling tired of adopting new tools, tired of hype, and tired of pressure to keep up.
- This fatigue also comes from being bombarded with AI-generated promotional materials and dense visuals.
- She notes that other writers have explored these feelings more broadly and that their posts resonated with her.

- In the closing section, she suggests readers may recognize some of these patterns in themselves and reassures them that this is normal, especially in a fast-moving field.
- She frames the article’s purpose as naming these mental patterns so people can notice them rather than adding to anxiety.
- She argues that being able to name what one is facing can make it feel a bit easier to bear.
- She insists that experiencing these issues does not mean someone is broken and that many peers share similar struggles quietly.
- Problems escalate when such thoughts become constant, disturb sleep, and drain joy from previously enjoyable activities.
- She advises watching for that threshold and reaching out to someone trusted if it is crossed.
- If informal support is not enough, she encourages seeking professional help, stressing that asking for help is not weakness and that therapy is nothing to be ashamed of.
