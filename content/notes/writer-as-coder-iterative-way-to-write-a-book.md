Babauta argues that nonfiction books should be written more like iterative software than like a finished product produced in isolation. He describes using alpha testers, reader feedback, revisions, wider testing, Kickstarter support, and post-print updates to improve the book through repeated cycles.

## Reading notes

- The traditional book model leaves the work untested until launch.
- Iterative programming uses a minimum viable product, testers, and feedback to improve a product over time.
- Babauta applied that model to *Zen Habits: Mastering the Art of Change*.
- He wrote chapters for ten alpha testers who read early versions and tried the ideas in practice.
- The testers journaled about the chapters, and he used those journals as feedback.
- He rewrote responses to their questions and sometimes turned those responses into new chapters.
- He then tested the ideas with larger groups through Zen Habits and Sea Change.
- He gathered feedback through Twitter, emails, polls, and forums.
- He later rewrote the book from scratch using feedback from readers, testers, and his editor Juli.
- He plans to keep revising the book after print and to prepare a second edition online later in 2015.
- He says the method makes him more motivated, lowers the burden of writing a large book, and catches problems before print.
- He also says the process lets him create in solitude while working with a community.
