Hoy argues that a daily quota like 1,000 words is not the point of writing. What matters is the reason for writing and the effect it has on other people. She says guilt, pressure, and self-forcing can obscure that purpose and make the work feel like a battle instead of a practice.

## Reading notes

- The post begins from Chris Guillebeau’s answer that he does not motivate himself to write when he does not feel like it.
- Hoy says she cannot reliably write 1,000 words a day, but she has still written books, blog posts, lessons, scripts, workshops, and talks.
- She argues that “motivate yourself” often really means “make yourself” or “force yourself.”
- The question behind the quota treats the self as split between a cart driver and a donkey.
- In that frame, writing is lost under the struggle to force output.
- The better question is why someone cares about writing in the first place.
- Hoy says she writes because her words help people, and readers tell her so directly.
- She trusts that she will return to the work after time away and does not want arbitrary rules that become excuses to quit.
- Writing, for her, is not a system of punishments and rewards.
- She says the same logic applies to programming, product work, or marketing: the reason for doing the work has to be stronger than the days when motivation drops.
- If someone likes a 1,000-words-a-day goal, she says to use it as a tool, but not as a moral test.
- She ends by saying that guilt and self-recrimination do not work and that loving the art is a better way forward.
