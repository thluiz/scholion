A distributed team can develop a stronger culture than a co-located one when leaders act intentionally instead of assuming culture will emerge on its own. Drawing on Edgar Schein’s model, the article argues that remote organizations must make underlying assumptions and values explicit, then align hiring, onboarding, communication, recognition, and work–life balance with those foundations.

Remote teams benefit from hiring self-motivated problem solvers, designing onboarding around mission and values, structuring meetings and one-on-ones, and using clear, rich communication channels including video. Leaders are encouraged to use public recognition, be deliberate about rare in-person gatherings, and actively prevent burnout in driven remote staff.

## Reading notes

- The article challenges the belief that physical distance makes it hard to nurture a healthy culture and claims distributed teams can end up with stronger cultures than face-to-face teams.
- Toptal’s experience shows a fully remote organization can grow to hundreds of team members and a nine-figure annual revenue run rate.
- Co-located teams often assume culture will grow organically, while distributed organizations feel higher stakes and so become more deliberate about culture.
- The article uses Edgar Schein’s research-based definition of culture as shared basic assumptions and values that have proven to lead to success.
- Schein’s cultural pyramid has three layers: visible artifacts and practices, underlying values, and deeper assumptions, all combining to form culture.
- Remote teams cannot rely on office cues like dress codes or office layouts to convey assumptions, so they should articulate those assumptions clearly.
- Foundational questions for remote culture include why the team exists, how it operates, and what principle serves as a “true north” guiding decisions.
- Values may include broad concepts like integrity or quality and more specific principles such as high standards for communication, plus purpose and mission.
- There is no fixed list of values suited to all remote teams; the key is to think consciously about the cultural foundation before or while building the team.
- Once assumptions and values are defined, managers can hire for cultural fit, which is particularly important when people won’t absorb norms in an office setting.
- Remote managers should be confident that new hires already embody the team’s values, because problems from a bad fit may surface later and be harder to detect.
- The traits the authors see as universal for successful remote hires are self-motivation and strong problem-solving ability.
- Remote work means individuals spend more time solving issues independently, so self-starters who handle unstructured environments fit better.
- With the right people and training during onboarding, managers can trust that autonomous decisions will align with the company and team.
- Traditional teams often leave cultural training to osmosis, assuming new hires absorb norms by being in the office.
- Remote teams must deliberately introduce culture in onboarding because they lack passive exposure to behaviors and norms.
- Toptal emphasizes mission and values across training materials so new hires repeatedly encounter the cultural foundations.
- The company records videos of executives talking about and modeling the organization’s values, creating scalable, reusable content.
- These videos help new team members feel more personally connected to leaders and to the mission despite physical distance.
- After values are set and understood, attention turns to the visible artifacts and practices, with communication positioned as the core of remote collaboration.
- Team meetings should become central to the team’s rhythm rather than just virtual replacements of conference room gatherings.
- Managers are encouraged to involve all team members in meetings to help everyone feel like essential contributors.
- Remote meetings should maintain an honest, thorough tone so participants share enough information to keep everyone aligned.
- If meetings degrade into mere status updates, that signals managers need to examine structure and purpose more deeply.
- One-on-one communication is more easily neglected remotely because there are no casual office encounters to build rapport.
- Individual conversations should be scheduled regularly, structured, and include topics beyond strictly professional updates.
- Regular one-on-ones across levels help deepen relationships and give space for praise or concerns that may not surface in group meetings.
- Using both formal and informal chat channels lets teams separate focused business communication from social conversations and shared interests.
- Side chats about hobbies or personal topics can build trust and connection without cluttering work-focused threads.
- The article stresses clarity in written communication because sarcasm, emojis, and vague comments are more easily misunderstood without nonverbal cues.
- An example illustrates how a simple thumbs-up emoji in response to instructions can leave intentions ambiguous.
- The authors discourage relying on emojis or GIFs for core information like instructions or problem descriptions.
- They endorse using emojis to convey emotion and to celebrate or share feelings, rather than for operational clarity.
- Over time, teams may create custom emojis tied to internal jokes or memes, which become artifacts that contribute to culture.
- The article argues that video adds value beyond audio or text by making facial expressions and reactions visible.
- Video is recommended for both team meetings and one-on-ones to strengthen engagement and participation.
- Seeing colleagues on video can highlight whether participants are attentive, engaged, and actively contributing to the culture.
- Without physical perks like special parking spots, remote teams can still motivate people through public verbal recognition.
- Shout outs during team meetings publicly acknowledge strong performance and create examples for others to follow.
- When posted in team chat, shout outs invite replies or emoji reactions from colleagues, amplifying the appreciation.
- If executives see and respond to these acknowledgments in shared channels, the recognition carries additional weight.
- The authors state that strong remote teams can function at high speed for long periods without any in-person meetings.
- When organizing face-to-face gatherings, they recommend a “Rule of 10x”: aim for ten times the productivity of a normal remote meeting if the goal is work.
- For social gatherings, they suggest aiming for ten times the bonding value of typical video or voice calls.
- Examples include multi-day in-person work sessions to accelerate a new initiative or renting a ski house where work discussions mix with enjoyable activities.
- The article warns against in-person meetings that could have been handled by video or that fail to create meaningful shared experiences.
- Meeting long-time remote colleagues in person should feel like old friends coming together rather than strangers meeting for the first time.
- The timing, location, and purpose of in-person meetings should emerge naturally from the team’s culture and needs, rather than being overplanned early on.
- Contrary to stereotypes, the authors say engaged remote workers are more likely to overwork than to underperform or get distracted.
- Driven employees who share a strong mission may have trouble stepping away from work, increasing burnout risk.
- Remote managers are urged to promote balanced work habits and a “work hard, play hard” ethos that leverages geographic flexibility.
- Examples of balance include “mini vacations” or “working vacations” that let people recharge while maintaining output.
- The article also says full breaks are necessary eventually, benefiting both individuals and the team.
- Absences from vacation or leave test whether the team can function if someone is suddenly unavailable due to emergencies or illness.
- Burnout cues are harder to spot without seeing colleagues in person, since you cannot observe their physical stress or long hours in an office.
- In remote contexts, managers must rely on communication patterns, one-on-one conversations, and performance metrics as indicators of strain.
- The authors see remote work and freelancing as growing trends that will make distributed collaboration common across organizations.
- Their experience suggests that building a strong remote culture does not require unusual tools or disruptive restructuring.
- By following practices around values, hiring, onboarding, communication, recognition, and balance, remote teams can handle cultural challenges across distances.
