The post focuses on Luděk Marold’s use of black and white watercolor on gray or tan board. It points out how he separates opaque and transparent whites, lets the drawing remain visible, and uses the background tone to add warmth and fill leftover spaces.

## Reading notes

- Marold’s illustrations often use black and white watercolor on a gray or tan board.
- Black is used transparently in some areas, while opaque white is reserved for brighter passages such as windows and highlights.
- White, black, and gray appear in different levels of opacity, with the drawing showing through in the figures.
- The warm tone of the board remains visible in the empty areas.
- Foreground elements help organize the image.
- In one festive scene, dark hats sit over a light area, with light lanterns against dark above them, and the blank lower area makes the crowded detail readable.
- In another scene, the artist chooses what to show and leaves the rest to the viewer’s imagination.
- White is used selectively, while most of the modeling is done in transparent gray.
- One painting gains much of its color from black, white, gray, and the background tone alone.
- Pure white is kept for small glints, and a soft gray can take on a bluish look.
- These originals were used for woodblock printing, so speed and efficiency mattered.
- Light and dark tones arranged around a middle tone create a sense of completeness.
- Marold had a difficult and short life, with early family loss, study in Prague, later expulsion, and stronger connections to Munich and Paris.
- He worked as a poster artist and magazine illustrator and befriended Alphonse Mucha.
- His large battle mural, together with rheumatic fever, led to his death at 33.
- The post ends by noting that his limited color paintings feel more alive and remain built from a restricted palette, including black, white, burnt sienna, and sometimes blue.
