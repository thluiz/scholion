The newsletter says AI makes communication, problem definition, and human judgment more important for engineers. As AI speeds up coding and documentation, engineers should spend more time on stakeholder management, project management, domain knowledge, and understanding product and user needs.

It also argues that engineers should keep learning, stay curious, try tools carefully, and avoid depending on AI so much that their own skills weaken. The writer and John Crickett point to habits like asking better questions, checking AI output, focusing on bottlenecks, and practicing without AI to keep core engineering abilities sharp.

## Reading notes

- Clear communication matters more because most interaction with AI happens through written or spoken prompts.
- Faster coding and documentation shift more attention toward product work, stakeholder management, project management, domain knowledge, and user needs.
- Adaptability matters because engineers need to learn tools quickly, absorb new domain knowledge, and adjust to change.
- Problem-solving starts with defining the problem clearly, its constraints, and what a good solution should look like.
- Curiosity helps engineers ask better questions about what they are building, why it matters, and who cares.
- Continuous learning helps people recognize patterns, evaluate what is worth exploring, and connect new ideas to existing knowledge.
- Small routines like asking one more question or spending 10–15 minutes a day learning can build broader understanding over time.
- AI tools should be tried broadly, not only as coding agents, because many engineering tasks involve writing, research, documents, and audio or video work.
- Tool choice should be based on whether it makes work easier, faster, or better, not on hype or constant switching.
- Main misuses include vague “vibe coding,” blind trust in AI output, and letting AI replace personal skill development.
- Engineers should keep their fundamentals sharp, because tools can go down and core problem-solving ability still matters in incidents.
- Stronger engineering fundamentals give an advantage, especially as some newer engineers enter the field with weaker basics.
