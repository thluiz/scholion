The text argues that generalists should not be reduced to people with broad but shallow competence. Their real advantage is curiosity, adaptability, and the ability to combine knowledge from different fields. In AI-heavy work, that matters more than rote expertise.

## Reading notes

- Generalists are described as curious people who move across domains, enjoy uncertainty, and solve problems by combining knowledge from different fields.
- Their advantage is not only shallow skill in many areas, but also the ability and desire to adapt to new situations.
- Following David Epstein, the text says generalists do especially well in “wicked” environments, where rules are unclear, patterns are not obvious, and feedback is delayed or inaccurate.
- Experts tend to shine in “kind” environments with clear, repetitive patterns and immediate feedback.
- The text links LLMs to kind environments, saying they are strong in specialist knowledge but weak at entirely novel problems.
- In that view, AI is less of a threat to generalists than to experts, because generalists can use LLMs to move faster into new domains and connect knowledge across fields.
- In an allocation economy, where people are paid for deploying intelligence rather than just knowing things, language models become a tool for generalists.
- The text contrasts modern specialization with ancient Athens, where citizens were expected to take part in many civic roles.
- It says complexity eventually pushed Athens toward specialization, but AI may make a broader kind of citizen useful again.
- The closing claim is that the winner in an allocation economy is not the person with the exact answer, but the person who knows which questions to ask.
