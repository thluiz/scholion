Ruby is presented as still being the most programmer-friendly mainstream language the author has used. The post says that if developer joy matters, Ruby will stay competitive, and that pressure to build better implementations can also improve performance. ## Reading notes

- The author acknowledges bias because of long-term Ruby work and income from Ruby books and screencasts.
- He describes himself as a hacker rather than a Rubyist and says he values Ruby for being pragmatic and fun.
- He argues that concern about concurrency and scalability is overstated because only large organizations usually face those problems strongly enough to drive new languages.
- He says many programmers care more about development velocity than raw server capacity.
- He points out that some scaling gains come from rewriting components after the team understands the problem better, not from changing languages early.
- He compares choosing tools to using a pocket Leatherman first and bringing out the big toolbox only when needed.
- James Edward Gray’s anecdote says an all-Ruby SOA system handled a traffic spike to 40,000 requests per minute, then was being tuned further.
- The post warns against peer pressure to adopt new frameworks just because they are hot.
- It says new technologies usually solve specific problems, and similar surface traits do not mean two applications have the same problem.
