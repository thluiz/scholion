The page defines the HiPPO problem as executive opinion overriding product plans without research or validation. It says this can come from CEOs, CTOs, sales, development, or service teams, and that the damage shows up in culture, confidence, and execution when direction changes without evidence.

It argues that the best responses are to bring data into the discussion, use qualitative or quantitative evidence, keep a transparent and prioritized backlog, and deflect sudden requests so product managers can validate them later. It also notes that HiPPOs are not always harmful, since some can be swayed by facts and may sometimes be easier to follow than to fight.

## Reading notes

- HiPPO means the highest-paid person's opinion is treated as direction, even without research or validation.
- HiPPO behavior can come from executives, but also from sales, development, or service teams.
- The page says HiPPOs are subjective and unscientific, and belong more to autocratic organizations than to data-sharing cultures.
- Frequent HiPPO decisions can make teams feel micromanaged and less confident in their own judgment.
- Repeated shifts in direction can weaken trust in leadership, the product, and the company.
- Sudden changes also create practical costs for work already in progress.
- Data is presented as the main counterweight to opinion when debating product direction.
- If quantitative data is unavailable, the page says qualitative input can still help.
- Product goals should frame the discussion, so the issue becomes rationalization rather than opinion matching.
- Direct contact with customers and the market helps product managers test whether a HiPPO view is broadly valid.
- A prioritized backlog gives product managers a way to compare new ideas against existing work.
- When a HiPPO appears suddenly, deflection can buy time to validate the idea and estimate the effort.
- The page says some HiPPO decisions can be productive when the product impact is small or debate is pointless.
- It contrasts HiPPOs with ZEBRAs, who shut down debate and treat their opinion as fact.
- The closing point is that recognizing and managing these situations is part of strong product management.
