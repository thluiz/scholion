The article argues that quieter employees often have useful feedback, but they are more likely to speak up when managers make the exchange easier. It links honest feedback to preparation, clarity, smaller questions, and visible follow-through.

## Reading notes

- Introverted employees may avoid spontaneous feedback requests because they need time to reflect and prepare.
- Managers should set up the conversation in advance instead of asking on the spot.
- The manager should explain why the feedback is being requested and why it matters.
- Questions that use “what” instead of “any” can invite more detailed answers.
- “Time boxing” helps by asking about a specific period, such as the past two weeks.
- Asking about one thing instead of leaving the question open-ended can make the request less overwhelming.
- A manager can lower the pressure by sharing something they think did not go well and asking whether the employee agrees or disagrees.
- Questions about the future can feel more open and constructive than questions about past failures.
- Bringing a notebook signals attention and a willingness to listen.
- Thanking the employee for dissenting feedback helps show that candid input is welcome.
- Staying quiet and not rebutting right away can make the employee more willing to speak honestly.
- Managers should say when they will follow up and keep that promise, because employees may feel feedback is futile if nothing changes.
