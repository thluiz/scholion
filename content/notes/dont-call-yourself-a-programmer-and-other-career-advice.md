The post frames engineering careers around business value. It says most programming jobs are in internal business software, that companies hire engineers to raise revenue or cut costs, and that status, pay, and opportunities follow proximity to profit centers more than technical labels, stack choices, or academic markers.

It also argues that careers are shaped by communication, networking, negotiation, and social context. The post advises readers to describe impact rather than call themselves programmers, treat interviews and workplace interactions as transactions, and remember that startup equity, hiring, and even technical decisions depend on people, not rules.

## Reading notes

- Most software work is boring internal business software that exists to save money or make money.
- Engineers are hired to create business value, not to program things for their own sake.
- Being attached to profit centers brings higher wages, more respect, and better opportunities.
- The label "programmer" is discouraged because it frames the worker as a replaceable cost.
- People should describe themselves by the revenue they increase or the costs they reduce.
- Chosen language or stack matters less than the ability to learn what a job needs.
- Coworkers and bosses are usually not friends; work relationships are transactional.
- The average competition is weaker than many engineers assume.
- Public job ads are presented as the exception, while hiring often happens through private introductions.
- Networking means meeting people who can help you later and making a favorable impression.
- Academic signals like GPA, major, and minor matter less in industry than in school.
- Salaries vary by company type, seniority, location, perceived value, and negotiation skill.
- Salary should be discussed late, after agreement in principle, not at the interview.
- Equity grants are usually worth little, and startup success is overestimated.
- Startups are presented as a risky career path and often a lifestyle choice.
- Communication is described as the most important professional skill.
- Engineers often need to do enterprise sales by persuading others inside the company.
- Modesty is not treated as career-enhancing in American business contexts.
- Business decisions are made by people, so appearance, grooming, and social cues matter.
- Career success should not be treated as the main source of life happiness.
