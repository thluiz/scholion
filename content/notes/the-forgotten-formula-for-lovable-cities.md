Sitte argues that cities feel lovable when streets and squares are enclosed, human-scaled, and shaped to frame buildings and monuments. He treats the surrounding urban fabric as part of the experience, not as clutter.

## Reading notes

- Old city streets are loved not only for historic buildings but also for the character of the spaces between them.
- Sitte’s 1889 book argues that successful public spaces are small, enclosed, and intimate.
- Enclosed squares and streets feel calmer and more protected, like outdoor rooms.
- Squares work better when entrances are less visually intrusive, especially when streets enter at corners or are bridged over.
- Large plazas weaken enclosure and make nearby buildings look diminished.
- Curves and T-junctions can help streets feel visually closed rather than endless.
- Sitte rejects symmetry as a necessary condition of good urban space.
- Irregular shapes can still feel harmonious at pedestrian level and can create useful recesses.
- Monuments work better at the edges of squares than in the middle or on central axes.
- A plain wall or restrained backdrop can make sculpture look larger and clearer.
- Buildings also need context: freestanding monuments often look smaller and lonelier than when surrounded by ordinary fabric.
- The demolition of surrounding buildings around monuments can worsen the effect by removing the frame that gives them scale.
- Sitte’s approach favors dense, interconnected fabric and small framed squares over exposed plazas.
- His ideas were influential in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, then largely displaced by modernism.
- The article presents modernist planning as more anti-Sittesque, with space treated as leftover after buildings are placed.
- Contemporary urban design is again moving toward Sitte-like principles, including in New Urbanist projects and some mainstream practice.
