When you have to pass along bad news that was not your fault, the message should stay direct and calm. The essay says negative words can make the situation feel more dramatic, and too much explanation can blur the facts and make it sound like you are accepting blame.

It also says to give only the key information, answer follow-up questions if needed, and remind the other person that you both knew the risks or shared the decision. That helps reduce misplaced anger and keeps the focus on what to do next.

## Reading notes

- Bad news can create negative feelings toward the person who shares it, even when that person did not cause the problem.
- Small changes in word choice can shape how the audience responds.
- Heavy words like "unfortunately" can make the situation sound more dramatic than it needs to be.
- When people feel guilty, they may over-explain to try to ease that feeling.
- Too much detail can make it harder to tell where the facts end and personal narrative begins.
- The recipient deserves the key information needed to make a decision.
- If the messenger is responsible, responsibility should be taken.
- Empathy and accepting fault are different things.
- The message should not accidentally sound like an admission of blame.
- If the audience believes you are admitting fault, that can strengthen misplaced anger and hurt credibility.
- Adding too much preface can make the listener imagine a worse outcome before hearing the actual news.
- Preface as little as possible and share the core message quickly.
- Reminding people that they had a role in the situation can make them less likely to blame only the messenger.
- A soft, respectful tone matters when explaining shared decisions or shared risk.
- These principles apply when you are only the messenger and are as surprised by the news as the recipient.
