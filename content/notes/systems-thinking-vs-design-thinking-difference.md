Systems thinking starts from the whole system and the relationships within it, while design thinking starts from people’s needs and uses prototyping to create human-centered products, services, and processes. The page also argues that combining both can lead to more effective, holistic solutions.

## Reading notes

- Systems thinking looks at wholes, interrelationships, and patterns of change rather than isolated elements.
- A system is described as more than the sum of its parts and as shaped by the interaction of its parts.
- Design thinking is framed as a human-centered approach that brings together desirability, feasibility, viability, and responsibility.
- The design thinking process is presented as iterative, with phases that can loop back and repeat.
- Systems thinking helps when a problem is large, complex, and involves many stakeholders or competing incentives.
- Its benefits include deeper understanding, broader choices, better-informed decisions, anticipation of trade-offs, and more buy-in.
- A limitation of systems thinking is getting stuck in thinking and ideation without moving into tangible implementation.
- Design thinking is valued for centering people, reducing risk, supporting faster learning, and building creative confidence.
- Its limitations include ambiguity, repeated looping through the process, and the time and practice needed to do it well.
- Human-centered systems thinking combines the analytical, holistic tools of systems thinking with the creative, human-centered process of design thinking.
- This combined approach aims to diagnose root causes, understand multiple stakeholders, and design interventions that drive behavior change and positive impact.
- The iceberg model is used to uncover behaviors, structures, and mindsets in a complex system.
- A systems map lays out stakeholder relationships and interactions to reveal opportunities for growth and change.
