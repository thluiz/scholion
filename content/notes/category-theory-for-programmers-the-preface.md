The preface frames category theory as a useful body of ideas for programmers, especially because it focuses on structure and composition. It also says the book will use code examples, including C++ and Haskell, to make the ideas accessible without assuming the reader already knows Haskell.

## Reading notes

- The book is aimed at programmers, not computer scientists, and the author wants to explain category theory in a more approachable way.
- Category theory is presented as a source of useful programming ideas that have long been used in Haskell and are spreading to other languages.
- The author argues that category theory suits programmers because it studies structure rather than particulars, and that structure is what makes programs composable.
- Composition is described as central to both category theory and programming, including structured programming, object-oriented programming, and functional programming.
- The author says he will use informal reasoning, but will ground it in solid mathematical theory.
- Computer code will illustrate the main concepts, with both functional-language and C++ examples.
- Haskell will be introduced slowly as a language for sketching and documenting ideas, not as a prerequisite.
- The preface links the growing interest in functional features, multicore concurrency, and software complexity to the need to rethink programming foundations.
- It says object-oriented programming does not handle concurrency well when combined with sharing and mutation, and that side effects become hard to manage at scale.
- The text uses the Beauvais Cathedral as an analogy for engineering done with limited foundations and many ad hoc fixes.
