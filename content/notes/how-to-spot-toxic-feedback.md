Susan DeFreitas argues that feedback can be harmful when it does not respect the work’s purpose or the writer’s vision. She frames toxic feedback as a practical problem in critique groups, mentoring, and editing rather than as proof that the writer is imagining things.

## Reading notes

- Feedback can miss the mark when the reader does not understand the writer’s intent or the kind of work being written.
- Some critiques reflect the reader’s own preferences, assumptions, or unresolved issues instead of the manuscript.
- Feedback becomes toxic when it turns personal, especially when critique is mixed with public conflict, derision, or unwanted psychoanalysis.
- An unprofessional tone can make criticism degrading even when the work itself needs revision.
- Formula-based advice can flatten a novel by forcing it into a single system or structure.
- A useful editor should think through the consequences of a change, not just point to a problem.
- The problem and the fix should stay distinct, because only the author can decide what fits the work.
- Writers should keep their vision intact while improving craft and finding readers who value that specific vision.
