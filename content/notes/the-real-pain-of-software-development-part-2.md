The author says long hours of coding left him with numb fingers, wrist, back, shoulder pain, and headaches. He describes recovery through workers’ compensation, therapy, breaks, stress reduction, and changes to his workstation and habits.

He also notes that solutions vary by person. He discusses Dvorak, chair choice, and the value of movement, but says there is too little research to prove one setup works for everyone and that pain often remains manageable rather than fully gone.

## Reading notes

- Long hours at the keyboard caused pain severe enough that even touching the keyboard hurt.
- His employer supported a workers’ compensation claim that covered doctor visits, physical therapy, occupational therapy, and an ergonomic chair.
- He says different bodies react differently, so people need to listen to what their own bodies are telling them.
- Physical therapy focused on strengthening muscles, massages, and stretches to relieve tension.
- Occupational therapy focused on habits and regular breaks because the body does not handle long periods of stillness well.
- He used a foam roller at the office for stress breaks and found it helpful.
- He switched to Dvorak to reduce finger motion, but says the evidence comparing Dvorak and QWERTY is too limited to make strong claims.
- He thinks his own experience cannot isolate Dvorak’s effect because he changed several habits at once.
- A comfortable chair matters, but the right chair depends on the person’s body type.
- He chose a chair and seat cushion that fit him better than the Aeron chair.
- He says the pain has mostly subsided, but not disappeared.
- When pain returns, he handles it with stretches, exercise, breaks, and lower stress.
- He tells readers not to treat coding pain as a personal failure and not to ignore it.
