The study tests whether a scarcity mindset changes consumer choice and the neural processes behind it. Participants completed a staged task that induced scarcity or abundance, then bid on supermarket food items while scanned with fMRI. Scarcity did not raise bids overall, but it made bidding track retail price more strongly and altered activity in orbitofrontal and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.

## Reading notes

- The authors used an experimental manipulation to induce scarcity and abundance within the same participants, avoiding simple comparisons between low- and high-income groups.
- Participants were scanned with fMRI while they completed a bidding task for familiar supermarket food items.
- A scarcity mindset did not produce a significant main effect on bid amounts.
- Participants bid more for hedonic products than for utilitarian products.
- Later willingness to pay predicted bids during scanning, but this relation did not differ between scarcity and abundance.
- Retail price predicted bidding behavior, and this relation was stronger in scarcity than in abundance.
- During product evaluation, scarcity greater than abundance was associated with increased activity in the orbitofrontal cortex.
- During bidding, higher bid amounts were associated with activity in right dlPFC, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, and left anterior insula.
- During bidding, abundance greater than scarcity showed increased activity in left dlPFC and left angular gyrus.
- The dlPFC effect depended on order: when participants experienced abundance first, dlPFC activity dropped in the later scarcity condition.
- Positive feedback versus negative feedback was associated with greater activity in OFC, angular gyrus, caudate, precuneus, middle temporal gyrus, and dACC.
- The authors argue that scarcity affects goal-directed value-based decision processes and that transitions from abundance to scarcity may be especially salient.
