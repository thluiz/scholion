The post argues that many business problems are self-induced and that Basecamp has lasted by avoiding them before they appear. Its decisions are shaped by future cost, keeping the company small, and favoring smaller projects that are easier to correct.

## Reading notes

- Many business problems are self-induced, and entrepreneurs often create the difficulties they later have to solve.
- Basecamp says it builds its business around avoiding problems instead of tackling hard problems head on.
- The company keeps itself intentionally small, with more than 100,000 paying customers and a staff of just over 50.
- Smaller companies avoid layers, management, bottlenecks, and formal processes that slow people down.
- Most projects are done by teams of three people or less, often two or even one person.
- Smaller teams are presented as a way to take on work in smaller chunks and course correct more easily.
- Most work fits into six-week cycles or shorter projects, which limits the cost of a bad decision.
- The post warns that long-running projects with no end in sight drain morale.
- The company rarely makes promises about the future because future promises often become sources of headache and conflict.
- Basecamp avoids things that require scale to succeed and looks for things that work at any size.
- It distinguishes deadlines from dreadlines, describing dreadlines as deadlines that create rushed work and negative attitudes.
- The post says companies usually have miscommunication problems, and that adding people increases the odds of information being lost or distorted.
- It also says partnerships with large companies often become dead ends and waste time, especially when the relationship is uneven.
- The company avoids bottlenecks by rejecting permission-based structures and letting people move forward unless an action would destroy the company.
- The closing metaphor compares this approach to washing dishes as you go, so work does not pile up later.
