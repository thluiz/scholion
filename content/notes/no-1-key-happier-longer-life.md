The piece presents older adults as unusually credible guides to a happier and longer life because they have lived through loss, illness, and other difficulties while still feeling fulfilled. Their main lesson is that time is finite, so people should use it on what matters instead of regretting things or worrying about what never happens.

## Reading notes

- Karl Pillemer’s research began with the observation that many people in their 70s, 80s, and beyond seem far happier than younger people.
- Older respondents said life passes quickly, so time should be spent on what matters.
- Their biggest regret was worrying about things that never happened.
- They valued saying important things to loved ones now, spending time with parents and children, savoring daily pleasures, working in a job they love, and choosing a mate carefully.
- They did not see money, status, career earning potential, or getting even with others as worth prioritizing for happiness.
- One participant said happiness is a choice, not a condition.
- Pillemer argues that happiness requires a daily shift in outlook toward optimism and hope.
- The text connects this view to Marcus Aurelius’s idea that distress comes from judgment of events, not the events themselves.
