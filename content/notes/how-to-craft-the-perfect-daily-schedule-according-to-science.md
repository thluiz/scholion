The article says the best daily schedule is built around energy, not time. It uses timing research from Daniel Pink to explain why performance changes across the day and why breaks matter for focus, memory, creativity, and motivation.

## Reading notes

- Performance varies by time of day, and the article treats that as the main reason to plan around peak, trough, and rebound periods.
- Peak time is for analytical work that needs focus and vigilance, such as coding or writing.
- Trough time is for low-focus tasks, breaks, email, expense reports, or a nap.
- Rebound time is for creative or insight-driven work, such as brainstorming, problem-solving, or learning.
- The article recommends tracking your own energy for at least a week to find your personal pattern.
- It suggests labeling tasks by energy level and using filters so the right work appears at the right time.
- Breaks are presented as part of productivity, not as time wasted.
- The article divides breaks into micro breaks, moving breaks, nature breaks, social breaks, and mental gear-shifting breaks.
- Naps are described as especially useful when they are short and timed to the trough period.
- It also suggests day theming if weekly energy patterns are consistent.
- The schedule should stay flexible, with margin for interruptions and a smaller definition of a productive day.
- If concentrated work is needed during off-peak hours, the article suggests using restorative breaks and the Pomodoro Technique.
