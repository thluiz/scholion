Thread by Thiago Ghisi summarizing Dan Heller’s ten principles for growing as an engineer. The focus is on thinking beyond coding to business impact, initiative, communication, project ownership, and long-term mastery of tools and education, framed as lessons the author wishes he had learned earlier.

## Reading notes

- Dan Heller frames these principles as lessons for engineers that he wishes he had learned many years earlier, ideally at the start of his career.
- Engineers are encouraged to think in terms of business value, reasoning like a CEO about how their work helps the company succeed and making informed tradeoffs on quality, features, and speed.
- The job is presented as broader than writing code, emphasizing responsibility for making good decisions that further the company’s goals.
- Engineers should refuse to remain blocked, using persuasion, escalation, or technical creativity to keep work moving and ensure their efforts create value.
- Taking initiative is highlighted as critical, countering the belief that others are fully in control; engineers should own their team and company mission and act when they see needed work.
- Managers are described as depending on engineers’ creativity and intelligence rather than solving everything themselves.
- Strong, concise technical writing is described as a key skill for collaboration, persuasion, teaching, and informing different audiences.
- Written communication should account for the reader’s knowledge, aim for clarity and brevity, and typically include a short summary (tl;dr) near the top.
- Engineers are urged to own their project management by understanding dependencies, assigning ownership, and summarizing plans and status clearly.
- Proactive stakeholder communication on plans and progress, along with practicing how to run meetings, is tied to the ability to handle larger projects and prepare for leadership roles.
- Growth is framed as the engineer’s responsibility: pursuing mastery and making learning a daily habit, even in very small time slots.
- The thread mentions subscribing to mailing lists and seeking out papers and books as part of owning one’s education, though that specific list is cut off.
- Mastery of core tools such as editors, debuggers, compilers, IDEs, databases, network utilities, and Unix commands is portrayed as a major amplifier of development speed.
- When encountering new technologies or commands, engineers are urged to explore them more deeply than seems immediately necessary.
- Proactive, structured communication is linked to building confidence and goodwill with collaborators and fostering a culture of learning and camaraderie.
- Sharing knowledge and keeping stakeholders regularly updated on goals and progress are presented as ongoing responsibilities.
- Collaborating with others is described as a way to increase leverage and visibility, extending impact beyond one’s own code.
- The text connects advancing as an engineer and in one’s career with having influence that goes beyond individual technical contributions.
- Professionalism and reliability are defined in terms of punctuality, preparation, attention in meetings, and following through on commitments.
- Engineers are advised to communicate early when problems arise, maintain composure, and voice objections in a professional manner.
- The principles are sourced from "Building a Career in Software: A Comprehensive Guide to Success in the Software Industry."
