The article says the reasons for taking a leadership role shape how effective someone will be. Good motivations include wanting broader impact, learning new skills, helping others grow, improving the work environment, modeling the right behavior, influencing direction, and taking on harder problems.

It also warns against choosing leadership for money, power, fame, peer pressure, escape from individual contributor work, or pride. Those motives tend to produce control, resentment, weak trust, and mediocre teams.

## Reading notes

- Leadership is more effective when the motive is to increase team impact rather than personal output.
- Leaders need skills that differ from developer skills, including active listening, coaching, mentoring, feedback, and influence.
- A leader’s job includes creating conditions for others to grow and giving them room to try new work with support.
- Leaders can improve the work environment by removing friction and making team processes easier.
- Consistent behavior matters because what leaders say and do should match.
- Respect can give a leader early influence on team, department, or organizational direction.
- Leadership can be appealing because it brings more ambiguous and challenging problems.
- Seeking leadership for money can lead to doing only the minimum while avoiding the added accountability.
- Seeking leadership for power encourages control and weakens team commitment and initiative.
- Seeking leadership for fame damages trust when a leader takes credit for the team’s work.
- Peer pressure can push someone into a role they do not actually want.
- Using leadership to avoid detailed work makes it harder to judge quality and lead technical teams well.
- Pride in the title can lead to micromanagement and heavy-handed use of authority.
