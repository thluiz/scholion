The article argues that simplicity is often used as a vague design slogan, while real simplification depends on the decisions that shape a product. It defines simplicity as reducing a product to its purest form of purpose or value, and says that usability and simplicity are not the same goal.

## Reading notes

- Simplicity gets invoked in design conversations, but the article says confusion is often pushed out of the process without enough guidance.
- Making something obvious has a cost, because limited screen space, attention, and comprehension force tradeoffs.
- Design decisions also happen outside the interface, where other people can reshape a product in ways that reduce its focus.
- A simple product is reduced to its core purpose or value, not merely made easy to use.
- The article uses a knife as an example of a simple tool that can be effective at different levels of mastery.
- IA Writer is presented as a simple application because it is stripped to writing, with few settings and limited options.
- Giles Colborne’s four strategies for simplicity are remove, organize, hide, and displace.
- Remove means cutting anything nonessential, including content or labels.
- Organize means grouping interface elements into logical chunks based on mental models or familiar patterns.
- Hide means keeping the most important elements visible while making the rest accessible through navigation.
- Displace means moving some functions to another device or feature so one interface does not do everything.
- The article says simplification can also mean replacing special-purpose products with software on general-purpose devices, as in smartphones.
- It warns that this kind of consolidation still has costs, especially when many apps and novelty products are added.
- Apple is used as an example of a company whose culture treats simplicity as a core value.
- The article ends by saying true simplicity starts at conception and depends on the creators’ values, not just the final product.
