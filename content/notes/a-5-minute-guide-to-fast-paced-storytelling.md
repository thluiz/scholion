The page says pacing is not only about how much time a story covers, but about how fast that time feels to the reader. Fast-paced scenes use more action and detail to heighten drama, while slower scenes can move through less important events quickly.

It uses examples from Lee Child, Tuft and Needle, and Hiut Denim to show that good storytelling changes pace for a reason. A story should keep the details that serve its purpose and leave readers to imagine the rest.

## Reading notes

- Pacing is the reader’s experience of time, not just the story’s timeline.
- Fast-paced storytelling keeps readers engaged with vivid action and dramatic detail.
- Slow-paced storytelling can dwell on scenery, but it loses effect if the description is not compelling.
- Lee Child slows down a tense password scene by describing small actions in detail.
- A later password scene moves quickly because repeating the same level of detail would be boring.
- Good storytelling slows down at dramatic moments and skips boring events.
- The Tuft and Needle founding story uses pace to focus on the problem of buying a mattress.
- That story leaves out details that do not help explain the company’s purpose.
- The Hiut Denim story covers a long time span in a few lines and then slows down to show the mission.
- Good storytellers choose action, detail, and pace according to what they want the reader to feel and remember.
