The post argues that impostor syndrome has uses and can become a trigger for success. It frames it as a form of adversity that can be handled well or badly, then uses three career episodes to show different responses to doubt.

## Reading notes

- The author defines impostor syndrome as a persistent inability to believe that success is deserved or earned through one’s own efforts or skills.
- In the first example, a promotion to senior engineer brought isolation, self-doubt, production failures, and continued shame when the author tried to ignore the problem.
- In the second example, a move into management brought strong impostor feelings even though the author had years of programming, mentoring, and leadership experience.
- The response was to recognize the fear as a lie and over-prepare by reading management books and taking online courses.
- In the third example, the author felt impostor syndrome before a first conference talk and responded by writing daily, preparing the talk, and practicing the slide deck repeatedly.
- The result was a successful talk to 310 people and a stronger technical writing practice.
- The closing advice is to use specific doubts as something to act on, expect impostor syndrome to return, prepare enough to make the lie look absurd, and seek professional help if anxiety becomes severe.
