Daniel Na names a negotiation tactic after the rap battle scene that closes the movie 8 Mile. Eminem's character goes first against the reigning champion and lists his own weaknesses, his "white trash" background and hard life, before his opponent can use them against him. There's nothing left to attack. Na applies the same move to work: writing a strategy that draws pushback, working inside a codebase another team resents outsiders touching, or asking a busy team to collaborate on a priority they don't share.

He ties the tactic to two established ideas. Getting to Yes argues that durable negotiations are mutually beneficial, so winning a short-term exchange at the other side's expense damages the relationship long-term. The Steel Man Argument asks that you engage the strongest version of an opposing case rather than a weak one. Both point to the same question at work: what does the other party actually need, and how does anticipating it change your plan?

## Reading notes

- Names the tactic after the final rap battle in 8 Mile: Eminem's character goes first and lists his own "white trash" background before his opponent can use it against him, leaving the opponent with nothing to attack.
- Connects the tactic to Getting to Yes: durable negotiations are mutually beneficial, and winning an exchange at the other side's expense costs the long-term relationship.
- Draws a parallel to the Steel Man Argument: address the strongest version of the opposing case, not a weak caricature of it.
- Applies the method to three work scenarios: a strategy proposal with unpopular tradeoffs, working inside a codebase another team resents outsiders touching, and asking a busy team to collaborate on a priority they don't share.
- Each scenario gets the same fix: name the likely objection before it surfaces and remove it in advance. Documented and tested PRs ease into another team's codebase; minimizing a busy team's time commitment or tying the ask to a goal they already hold gets their collaboration.
- Closes on locus of control: a team that looks blocked by other people's opinions or priorities usually has more room to act than it assumes, and working through the method forces a list of real alternatives instead of stopping at frustration.
