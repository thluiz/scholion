The article argues that recessions and crises push organizations toward open source because it lowers costs, reduces vendor lock-in, and helps them keep performance while spending less. It frames the current moment as a possible third wave of migration, with applications, data science tools, and cloud-hosted workloads moving further into open source stacks.

## Reading notes

- Dries Buytaert’s point is that during downturns organizations try to lower costs, take control of their future, and do more with less, and open source helps them do that.
- The first major wave described here was UNIX to Linux migration, driven mainly by saving money on cheaper Intel hardware.
- Companies moved from Sun Solaris, HP-UX, and AIX to Linux because it was much less expensive and avoided vendor lock-in.
- The article says Sun Microsystems later declined in part because of UNIX to Linux migration and was eventually acquired by Oracle.
- The second wave widened from operating systems to the broader open source stack, starting with LAMP and then spreading to databases, middleware, storage, and networking.
- Open source databases such as MySQL, PostgreSQL, and MongoDB replaced proprietary options like Oracle and IBM DB2 in many places.
- Open source middleware such as Jetty and Tomcat gained ground against larger commercial vendors.
- The 2008 financial crisis accelerated migration to cloud services like AWS, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure, which also relied heavily on open source.
- The article says open source applications still face a weakness in the consistent availability of support and commercial assurances for independently maintained components.
- It notes that over 92% of application libraries contain open source components, yet many teams have not fully embraced open source for application development.
- Modern web applications built on React, Angular, or Vue are presented as likely candidates for migration.
- The article also points to SAS and MATLAB as expensive tools that organizations might replace with NumPy, pandas, and SciPy.
- It concludes that the third wave will need vendors that can provide commercial assurances similar to those once supplied by Red Hat or AWS.
