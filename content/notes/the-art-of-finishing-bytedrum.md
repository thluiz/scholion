The post describes the pull of unfinished projects and the frustration that comes from spending time on work that remains half done. It argues that endless beginnings create mental clutter, block learning that happens near the end of a project, and keep people from the satisfaction of shipping something complete.

It then proposes practical ways to finish more often: define done before starting, aim for an MVP, time-box work, practice finishing small tasks, separate ideas from implementation, celebrate completions, and use accountability to stay on track.

## Reading notes

- Unfinished projects feel full of possibility, which makes them easier to keep alive than to complete.
- Personal projects can drift without deadlines, and that freedom can turn into perfectionism and endless refinement.
- Never finishing leaves projects in the mind as open tabs, draining attention and creative energy.
- Finishing teaches perseverance, attention to detail, and the judgment needed to let go.
- Delivering a project brings feedback and professional credibility that starting alone cannot provide.
- The article recommends defining “done” early so scope does not keep expanding.
- It favors MVP thinking over perfection so a basic version can be released and improved later.
- Time-boxing projects is presented as a way to create urgency and stop feature creep.
- Practicing completion on small tasks is treated as a way to build the habit of finishing.
- New ideas during implementation should be recorded separately instead of immediately changing the current work.
- Celebrating completions and using accountability help reinforce the habit of shipping.
