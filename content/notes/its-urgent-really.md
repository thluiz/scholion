The post argues that email has been turned into a place for urgent work even though it was meant for asynchronous communication. It says this habit pulls attention away from other work and makes the inbox the center of the workday.

## Reading notes

- email became the place where professionals expect urgent matters to be handled, even though it was not designed for that
- when people stay in email all day, they do not make progress on other kinds of work
- some company cultures reward being in the know and make it hard to say “I don’t know. I’ll get back to you.”
- that culture leads to over-preparing for meetings, sending ASAP requests during meetings, and waiting for urgent messages from bosses
- when meetings get canceled, there is finally time to work
- fewer people getting work done leads to more meetings meant to check why work is not getting done
- meetings and email feed each other in a painful cycle
- the author says an email marked urgent would still require checking email and phone all day, including on a day off
- the sender had not given a timeframe, and the author did not know him
- email is chronological, has no intelligence, and does not prioritize for you
- reacting to email can create the feeling of being busy without actually doing meaningful work
- if you react to email, someone else sets your priorities
- real emergencies are unlikely to arrive through the inbox
- in tech work, very little is truly urgent
- ignoring imaginary emergencies and working on your own priorities helps people get work done without needing to explain themselves in meetings
