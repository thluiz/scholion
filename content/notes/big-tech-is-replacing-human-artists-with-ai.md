The text says AI-generated art and writing are moving quickly toward human-level output, while the cost of training large models will put the most capable systems under corporate control. It treats this as a coming shift in which Big Tech will own the main tools for creative work.

It also argues that AI creativity lacks consciousness and intentionality, so it represents a loss of meaning as well as a technical advance. The author connects that loss to what he calls extrinsic drift, then extends the concern to regulation, human dignity, and the way companies mediate access to these systems.

## Reading notes

- A painting made by AI serves as a reminder that human art can come under total corporate control.
- The scale of the models, with more parameters and more connections, is presented as the factor that drives AI progress.
- This movement favors Big Tech, because training large models costs millions and, later, possibly billions.
- The text states that GPT-3’s performance on short text already surpasses many people and can be measured by quantity and variety of output.
- AI is described as capable of generating large volumes of prose, poetry, and other formats in seconds.
- Even when the result requires attempts and editing, the author maintains that what matters for creation is the final output.
- The text cites a creativity test in which GPT-3 ranked between the 85th and 95th percentiles.
- The automation of creativity is presented as a process that has already begun in poetry and short prose and is expected to advance to other forms.
- The author links this trend to the concept of Semantic Apocalypse, understood as the emptying of meaning through productions without consciousness.
- The opposition between extrinsic perspective and intrinsic perspective organizes the critique, with art linked to consciousness and access to experience.
- AI is treated as an example of extrinsic drift, by replacing human explanations and productions with mechanisms and data.
- The text argues that the future of creative AI will be dominated by large corporations, which will control training, licenses, and access via oracles.
- The author proposes regulation to limit the scope of AI, citing human dignity and restrictions already applied in other areas.
- The comparison with the Aibo robot reinforces the idea that mechanical simulacra diminish living beings and hide corporate mediation.
- The conversation with AI about the dead fiancée is presented as stage necromancy, without digital afterlife and without the person’s real presence.
