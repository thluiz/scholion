The article quantifies the global predation impact of insectivorous birds using 103 published estimates of prey consumption across seven terrestrial biomes, scaled by biome area. The authors estimate that insectivorous birds have a standing biomass of about 3 million tons and consume roughly 400–500 million tons of arthropod prey annually, most likely near 400 million tons, equivalent to about 2.7×10^18 J per year or about 0.15% of global terrestrial net primary production. Forest birds account for over 70% of this consumption (around 300 million tons per year), with savannas/grasslands, croplands, deserts, and Arctic tundra contributing the remainder. The paper links these energetic estimates to experimental evidence that birds reduce herbivorous insects and provide economically relevant pest control, especially in forests and some tropical agroecosystems.

## Reading notes

- Study aims to estimate annual prey biomass consumed by the global population of insectivorous birds by biome and worldwide, and to estimate the standing biomass of insectivorous birds.
- "Insectivorous birds" are defined broadly as bird groups whose diets include a considerable proportion of arthropods (insects and spiders), also covering many omnivores such as starlings and thrushes.
- Insects are described as dominating terrestrial biota in numbers, biomass, and diversity, making them a major food base for terrestrial carnivores; social insects alone may exceed 700 million tons standing biomass globally.
- Previous global food-consumption estimates existed for seabirds and shorebirds, but the combined predation impact of terrestrial insectivorous birds had not been quantified.

- Standing biomass per biome is calculated from bird density (birds per hectare), mean body mass (kg per bird), and biome area (hectares), using published values for densities, areas, and representative species masses.
- Total standing biomass of the global terrestrial avifauna is estimated at 3.981×10^9 kg fresh weight (roughly 4 million tons), which is similar to an independent 5 million ton estimate obtained by other methods.
- Assuming about 90% of land bird individuals in temperate, boreal, and arctic zones and about 60% in the tropics are arthropod-eaters, the standing biomass of insectivorous birds is inferred to be on the order of 3 million tons.
- This bird biomass is small relative to other predatory taxa such as spiders (~25 million tons), ants (~280 million tons), and whales (16–103 million tons), which the authors relate to birds’ low production efficiency of about 1–2%.

- Annual prey consumption is derived from mean prey consumption rates (kg fresh weight per hectare per year) for each biome, averaged across 103 values and then extrapolated using biome area.
- Data sources include: direct prey-consumption estimates (26 cases); conversions from energy-demand estimates for bird communities (53 cases); and modeled food consumption for desert and Arctic tundra birds using metabolic equations, breeding-season lengths, and breeding densities.
- Conversions from energy demand to food mass assume approximately 70% water content of arthropod prey, energy density of animal matter of 22.5 kJ per gram dry weight, and assimilation efficiency of 75%.

- To isolate insectivorous birds from total land bird communities, the authors assume about 90% of individuals in temperate, boreal, and arctic regions and about 60% in tropical and non-tropical desert habitats are insectivores during the breeding season.
- They further assume breeding-season diets are composed of at least 75% arthropods in temperate forests and some temperate grasslands, about 95% arthropods in agricultural areas, and about 85% arthropods in deserts, and they multiply food consumption by these fractions to obtain arthropod intake.
- Arthropod consumption values for tropical biomes are treated as annual totals (breeding plus non-breeding seasons), whereas most temperate data reflect breeding-season values only.
- For temperate, sub-polar, and polar climates, the authors assume that non-breeding season arthropod consumption per hectare is about 5–10% of the breeding-season value, due to migration and shifts to plant-based diets when arthropods are scarce.

- Mediterranean shrublands are classified under tropical grasslands and savannas because of similar net primary production and bird densities; their total area is relatively small compared to global land area.
- Most underlying bird-density and prey-consumption studies were conducted in the late twentieth century; more recent bird population declines discussed in the literature are not incorporated into the global estimates.

- The 103 prey consumption estimates are assigned to seven terrestrial biome types: tropical forests; temperate and boreal forests; tropical grasslands and savannas plus Mediterranean shrubland; temperate grasslands (including meadows, pastures, old fields); cropland; deserts; and Arctic tundra.
- For each biome, the authors compute an average prey consumption rate (kg arthropods per hectare per year), then multiply by biome area to obtain a subtotal; summing subtotals yields a global annual prey-consumption estimate.

- Prey consumption rates per hectare vary significantly among biomes according to a Kruskal–Wallis one-way analysis of variance by ranks, with non-normal data distributions confirmed by normal probability plots.
- Dunn’s multiple comparison tests show tropical forests have higher prey consumption than all other biomes; temperate–boreal forests exceed tundra, deserts, and temperate grasslands but do not differ from tropical grasslands or croplands.
- Tropical grasslands and savannas show higher prey consumption than deserts and tundra; croplands show higher consumption than deserts; some other biome pairs do not differ significantly.
- Across biomes, annual prey consumption is positively correlated with net primary production using published NPP values.

- Summation of biome subtotals yields a global annual arthropod consumption estimate of 404,586×10^6 kg per year (about 404.6 million tons), corresponding to about 2.7×10^18 J per year, which is 0.15% of global terrestrial net primary production (1.782×10^21 J per year).
- This global estimate excludes arthropod intake at migration stopover sites, because quantitative assessments are lacking; based on the numbers of migrating birds and mixed diets at stopovers, the authors roughly estimate 3–5 million tons of arthropods consumed per year at stopovers, around 1% of total arthropod consumption by insectivorous birds.

- To explore uncertainty in non-breeding season insectivory in temperate and cold regions, two extreme scenarios are considered: a minimum with no arthropod consumption in the non-breeding season, and a maximum with non-breeding arthropod consumption at 50% of breeding-season levels.
- Under these assumptions, global annual prey consumption ranges from 396,041 million tons per year (minimum) to 472,145 million tons per year (maximum), suggesting a likely true range between 400 and 500 million tons per year, with values near the lower end favored by the authors.
- The authors compare their arthropod-only estimate for insectivorous birds to an earlier estimate of about 7.5×10^18 J per year for total energy consumption by all land birds, and attribute most of the difference to additional plant and other food sources considered in the earlier work, especially in the non-breeding season.

- The calculated consumption totals imply strong global predation pressure by insectivorous birds on arthropods, particularly in tropical and temperate/boreal forest ecosystems.
- This is supported by many exclosure experiments showing that birds substantially reduce herbivorous insect abundance and leaf damage in forests, grasslands, and croplands when allowed access.

- Diet studies indicate that insectivorous birds take a broad spectrum of arthropod taxa, with seven orders particularly frequent: Lepidoptera, Coleoptera, Orthoptera, Diptera, Hemiptera, Hymenoptera, and Araneae.
- In temperate forests and agricultural habitats, caterpillars and beetles are especially important prey, while grasshoppers are key prey in grasslands.
- Tropical forest and farmland birds often feed on beetles, ants, cockroaches, katydids, caterpillars, and spiders; desert birds frequently eat beetles, ants, and termites; savanna birds commonly exploit swarming termites; tundra birds mainly consume tipulid flies and spiders.

- Birds in forests account for about 75% of the annual prey consumption of insectivorous birds (around 300 million tons per year), reflecting both the large global forest area (about 41.6 million square kilometers) and high bird diversity and density in forests.
- Forest birds often feed heavily on leaf-eating caterpillars and beetles, especially during breeding, when nestling diets may contain 20–90% caterpillars.
- Studies on pests such as the eastern spruce budworm show that birds can destroy a large proportion of larvae and pupae at low pest densities, and woodpeckers are reported to suppress bark beetle populations in coniferous forests.
- Forest birds also frequently consume spiders, which offer high taurine content important for passerine development but themselves act as major insect predators, so this predation on spiders can reduce some indirect pest-control benefits.

- Birds in grasslands and savannas contribute about 15% of global annual prey biomass (around 60 million tons per year); these biomes cover about 45.4 million square kilometers, including 2.8 million square kilometers of Mediterranean shrublands.
- Prey biomass per hectare per year in grasslands is substantially lower than in forests, in line with lower bird densities, but some North American studies demonstrate that grassland birds can impose notable predation pressure on grasshopper populations.

- Bird communities in agricultural areas contribute roughly 7% of global arthropod consumption (around 28 million tons per year) across about 13.5 million square kilometers of cropland.
- Agricultural landscapes include crop fields and woody elements such as shelterbelts and tree-lined field roads, and birds associated with these features were included in cropland consumption estimates.
- Farmland birds may consume large numbers of lepidopteran and coleopteran pests, but strong suppression of crop pests by birds is infrequently documented in temperate agriculture, possibly because crop fields host relatively few birds.
- In contrast, tropical plantations show more frequent examples of effective avian pest control; coffee plantations in several Latin American and Caribbean countries provide a well-studied case where insectivorous bird communities reduce populations of coffee berry borer, with demonstrated ecological and economic benefits.

- Birds in desert and tundra biomes each account for less than 4% of global annual prey biomass, consistent with sparse, low-productivity vegetation and low bird densities in these regions.
- In desert and tundra habitats, birds feed mainly on non-pest arthropods, and are described as negligible from the standpoint of economic ornithology.

- The authors note that global energy consumption by insectivorous birds in the form of arthropod prey (about 2.7×10^18 J per year) is comparable to the 2011 energy use of a megacity such as New York (about 2.8×10^18 J per year).
- They argue that, to meet these energy demands, insectivorous birds capture very large numbers of potentially harmful herbivorous insects and other arthropods.
- On a global scale, only spiders and entomophagous insects appear to match insectivorous birds in their ability to suppress herbivorous insects across multiple biomes, while other vertebrate predators such as bats, primates, shrews, hedgehogs, frogs, salamanders, and lizards generally have lower global impacts.
- The estimated global predation impact of insectivorous birds (between 400 and 500 million tons per year) is presented as similar in magnitude to that of spiders (between 400 and 800 million tons per year).

- Statistical comparisons among biomes are carried out using Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by Dunn’s pairwise multiple comparisons with tie corrections, implemented in R with the PMCMRplus package.
- The study is positioned as a first global quantification of insectivorous bird predation and a counterpart to earlier global estimates for spiders, intended to clarify the extent to which major natural enemies suppress herbivorous insects at the planetary scale.
