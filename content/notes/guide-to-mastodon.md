## Reading notes

- Mastodon is presented as social networking software in which each site can talk to other Mastodon sites and to other fediverse services.
- The guide uses Twitter and email as points of comparison, but highlights that Mastodon does not work exactly like either one.
- In Mastodon, there are Home, Notifications, Local, and Federated timelines, and all are reverse-chronological, unless the server fails.
- The fediverse structure brings together servers with ActivityPub or OStatus, including software such as Pixelfed, Pleroma, Misskey, and WriteFreely.
- The choice of instance may depend on thematic focus, community size, and the speed of local timelines.
- The account can be redirected or migrated, but posts are not transferred to the new account.
- The username is treated as immutable; the only approximate way to change it is to migrate the account.
- Mentions of people from another instance need the full format username@instance.
- Rules vary according to the instance, and local moderators have the final say on what is allowed.
- The text describes the mute, block, and report options for dealing with unwanted users or rule breaking.
- The guide distinguishes post types between standalone posts, self-replies, and replies, and shows that the order of the chain changes the type of the reply.
- The visibility definitions include Public, Unlisted, Followers-Only, and Mentioned People Only.
- The text says that private messages and mention-only toots are not encrypted or secure.
- Following someone gives access to that person's posts in the Home timeline and to followers-only posts, and locked accounts allow manual approval of followers.
- Even with a locked account, public and unlisted posts continue appearing in the profile and, in the case of public posts, in open timelines.
- Every profile generates an RSS feed of public and unlisted posts, which can be read by anyone.
- Hashtags serve as metadata and allow searching and following specific topics, but the text recommends moderation in their use.
- CW means Content Warning and is used to hide text about sensitive topics or long posts.
- The eye icon hides images behind a layer of sensitive content.
- Images can receive a caption and alt text, and the text highlights the usefulness of this for accessibility and OCR.
- Custom emojis are defined by instance and can be inserted by name between two colons.
- Content search exists, but post search is disabled by default and is treated as a vector for harassment.
- The text also explains why Mastodon does not have quote-tweet, presenting this as an anti-harassment measure.
- Mastodon culture is described through customs such as 🍍, AWOO, #gameing, and CWs with song lyrics or memes.
- The guide recommends etiquette practices such as using alt text, marking sensitive content, avoiding commercial spam, using CW in cross-posts, and keeping a careful tone in conversations.
- The text mentions clients and apps such as Pinafore, Halcyon, Toot! and Tusky, as well as backup with Mastodon Archiver and keyboard navigation.
