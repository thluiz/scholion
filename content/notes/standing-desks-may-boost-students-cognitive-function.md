The article reports a pilot study on high school freshmen who used standing desks for one school year. Their neurocognitive test scores improved by 7 to 14 percent, and brain imaging showed increased activity in the left prefrontal cortex.

## Reading notes

- Standing desks are presented as a classroom change usually aimed at physical activity and health, but the study examines cognitive effects as well.
- The researchers followed 27 freshmen over one school year at a Texas high school that had standing-biased classrooms.
- The students could stand, sit on a stool, or switch positions during class.
- Five neurocognitive tests measured decision-making, reasoning, working memory, strategy adjustment, and cognitive flexibility.
- Researchers also used functional near infrared spectroscopy on 14 students during testing.
- Test scores improved from the start to the end of the school year.
- The imaging showed increased activity in the left prefrontal cortex.
- Because it was a pilot study, there was no sitting control group.
- The authors recommend larger studies with proper controls and suggest looking at grades and standardized test scores in future research.
