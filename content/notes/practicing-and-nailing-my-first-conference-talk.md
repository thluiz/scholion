The article focuses on how the author prepared for and delivered his first conference talk, with an emphasis on practice, pacing, and handling nerves. He describes how he refined the talk over four months, adjusted for the room and schedule, prepared for technical failure, and recovered after the presentation.

## Reading notes

- He practiced the talk 16 times over four months and used early run-throughs to find the core ideas, smooth slide flow, and decide when to add, remove, or reorder slides.
- Two experienced speakers from CodeMash answered his questions about nerves, humor, and starting the presentation, which helped confirm that he was on the right path.
- He spent extra time on the introduction because he wanted to get comfortable quickly and used repeated restarts whenever he stumbled on it.
- Later practices became more formal, with him standing and presenting to the TV, then to slides in his peripheral vision, so he could test how his knee and feet would hold up for a 60-minute talk.
- When he learned the talk would be in the first slot, in the largest room, and recorded by Pluralsight, he increased his preparation intensity and used a higher energy level in rehearsal.
- Julie Lerman’s public mention of his work helped reduce his impostor syndrome and made him feel that his ideas were worth sharing.
- During conference week he stopped changing slide order, made only small visual tweaks, and did one run-through each night to stay fresh.
- To prepare for stage fright, he agreed to a short lightning talk in the main auditorium so he could practice with lights, a raised platform, and a microphone.
- The lightning talk helped him realize that the room was just a room and the stage was just a stage, and that he could still speak well even with a small clicker mistake.
- Before the main talk, he made a backup of the slides, recorded timing for each slide, and built a cheat sheet listing the main slides and the times they would occur in case his machine or projection failed.
- He also noted the 20-minute and 10-minute warning points so the proctor’s signals would not rattle him.
- He arrived early, turned off WiFi and his phone, muted devices, checked the clicker, mouse, tablet, and laptop, and made sure the slide deck was ready to start.
- Right before speaking, he treated the room as a show-and-tell of his practice runs and felt encouraged to see support from people in his local .NET user group.
- The talk started well, but PowerPoint advanced slides twice because recorded timings were still enabled, so he stopped the presentation, cleared the timings, and continued.
- After that, occasional missed commands still happened, but he finished on time and received strong positive feedback in person and on Twitter.
- He received a visual note-taking souvenir from the conference and later needed time alone to unwind because giving the talk was draining for him as an introvert.
- He ended the day reflecting on the experience from a hot tub at the hotel water park and felt the first conference talk was unforgettable.
