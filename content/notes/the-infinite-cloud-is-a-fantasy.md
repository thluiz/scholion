The article argues that the cloud is a material infrastructure with environmental costs that marketing has obscured. It consumes electricity and freshwater, creates electronic waste and noise, and keeps expanding even as climate impacts intensify.

## Reading notes

- The cloud is presented as a metaphor that hides the complexity of digital infrastructure.
- For data storage companies, the cloud refers to hyperscale data centers run by a small group of firms.
- The cloud’s immaterial image is described as a marketing success and a form of obfuscation.
- The author links the cloud to warming, water depletion, electronic waste, and environmental toxicity.
- Residents in some communities resist new data centers because of pollution, power strain, land use, and weak job creation.
- Data centers can consume as much electricity as a small city and often draw power from coal-fired grids.
- Operators use diesel generators in hot standby to avoid outages.
- Cooling with freshwater is increasingly common, and some facilities consume millions of gallons per day.
- In drought-prone places such as Arizona, data centers compete with other water users in stressed watersheds.
- Decommissioned servers become electronic waste, and the recycling rate remains low.
- Informal salvage chains for e-waste can poison water, soil, and workers.
- Nearby residents may suffer from constant noise from generators and cooling equipment.
- The article says the climate window is closing while demand for ICTs keeps rising.
- Growth in cloud infrastructure is tied to more energy use, more land use, and more strain on power systems.
- The author argues that the cloud is already broken and that the problem is cultural, not just technical.
- One proposal is to break up Big Tech control and treat the cloud more like a public utility.
- Another is to end planned obsolescence by designing devices and servers for durability, repair, and modular upgrades.
- A third is to expand cold storage, which needs far less energy than hot storage.
- Cold storage is presented as a way to preserve data while reducing waste and easing pressure on servers.
- The conclusion says technology alone will not solve the problem without a cultural shift toward sustainability.
