Remote teams cannot rely on the casual, in-person questions that work in an office, so shared practices need to be written down. The article’s central claim is that checklists are a practical way to communicate team norms, especially when people work at different times and need clear procedures.

It draws on a handwashing study from The Checklist Manifesto to show that checklists can change behavior, not just repeat instructions. The same idea is then applied to software work through examples such as code review, testing, bug reports, component design, UI performance, continuous delivery, and CI/CD.

## Reading notes

- Remote teams need a different way to convey practices and procedures because people are not all available at the same time.
- Checklists are presented as the solution for communicating team norms on remote teams.
- The author says he has used checklists for years and started making them standard operating procedures after reading The Checklist Manifesto.
- A handwashing study showed a 35%–52% drop in disease incidence.
- The study’s impact came from the checklist instructions, not from the specific soap used.
- The checklist changed behavior by showing when and how to wash hands properly.
- The best checklists are short enough to memorize and contain only the key points.
- Long checklists reduce compliance because people begin to treat items as optional.
- The article gives examples of checklists for code review, code testing, test timing, unit tests, bug reports, components, UI performance, continuous delivery, and CI/CD.
- The CI/CD checklist includes peer review, automated tests, sandboxed builds, approval before merge, and automatic production deployment after checks pass.
