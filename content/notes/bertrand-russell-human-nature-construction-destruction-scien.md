Russell says science education is central to a future of happiness and democracy because applied science can reduce suffering, improve health, and help build a new world. He also insists that the humanities matter, since imagination keeps progress from becoming mechanical and trivial.

## Reading notes

- Science education is presented as necessary for building a world where people have a reasonable chance of happiness.
- Russell says applied science should be the chief ingredient of that education, with physics, physiology, and psychology as essential fields.
- He adds that the conquest of leisure and health is of little value if people do not remember how to use them.
- The humanities are important because they develop imagination, which lets people see what the world might be.
- He warns against cast-iron rules and crude utilitarianism, especially in a mechanistic civilization.
- Russell links science with democracy and says democracy is impossible without science.
- Education should produce confidence that knowledge is attainable, while recognizing that much accepted knowledge is mistaken and can be corrected.
- He describes this as a difficult state of mind that requires intellectual culture without emotional atrophy, which he calls the scientific temper.
- He rejects both dogma and skepticism as educational goals.
- He argues that life is governed not only by facts but also by hopes, and that truth is broader than matter-of-fact.
- Russell says children contain the raw material for both good citizenship and criminality, and that instinct is ethically neutral.
- He holds that environment shapes instinct toward good or evil.
- Construction and destruction both satisfy the will to power, but construction is harder and therefore more satisfying.
- He says many virtues begin with the joys of construction.
- Intelligent people should be encouraged to use imagination to find more productive ways of using existing social forces or creating new ones.
