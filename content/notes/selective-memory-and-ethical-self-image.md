The page argues that people may remember their own misdeeds less clearly than other kinds of personal events. It presents this as a possible way the brain reduces tension between unethical actions and moral values, which helps preserve a positive self-image.

## Reading notes

- A study based on nine experiments and 2,109 participants suggests that memories of one’s own unethical acts become fuzzier in the brain.
- The researchers link this selective blurring to avoiding dissonance between behavior and moral values.
- They say this may help people keep a positive moral self-image and avoid distress.
- The authors note that people can also justify their behavior or dehumanize victims, but forgetting may be the simplest strategy.
- In one experiment, participants rated memories of unethical deeds as less vivid than ethical, positive, negative, or neutral experiences.
- In another experiment, participants recalled their own misconduct less clearly, but remembered other people’s ethical and unethical acts with equal clarity.
