Time blocking divides the day into blocks reserved for specific tasks or groups of tasks. The guide pairs it with weekly review, task batching, and day theming, and suggests using a calendar plus a task manager to plan work, reduce context switching, and make time for deep work.

## Reading notes

- Time blocking asks you to plan the day in advance by assigning each block to one task or a specific group of tasks.
- A weekly review is presented as necessary to prioritize the task list and sketch the week’s time blocks.
- Task batching groups similar small tasks into shared blocks so the day is interrupted less often.
- Day theming assigns a whole day to one area of responsibility, which reduces context switching and decision fatigue.
- The method is said to support deep work by concentrating attention on one project or task at a time.
- It is also described as a way to handle shallow work more efficiently by setting limits on email, paperwork, and similar tasks.
- Blocking time makes commitments more concrete, which helps people see the cost of saying yes to new requests.
- The article says time limits can help counter perfectionism by making it easier to stop when a task is good enough.
- It also cites research that concrete plans improve follow-through on intentions and reduce procrastination.
- Even reactive jobs can use time blocking by reserving some control over the schedule and protecting a few small blocks for deeper work.
- Common mistakes include underestimating task duration, being too rigid, and overscheduling leisure time.
- The guide recommends combining time blocking with task batching in Todoist by assigning labels to categories of work and matching them to calendar blocks.
- It also suggests paper planning or shared-calendar blocks for deep work, then using Google Calendar integration for more granular task scheduling.
