The text challenges the belief that Americans historically ate a mostly plant-based diet and only later shifted toward heavy meat consumption. It argues that early U.S. diets were rich in red meat and relatively low in fruits and vegetables, that heart disease appears to have been rare during this meat-heavy era, and that influential claims about an earlier, healthier, plant-based past rely on flawed food-supply data and misleading aggregations of meat statistics.

## Reading notes

- Senator McGovern’s 1977 Dietary Goals portrayed recent U.S. dietary change as harmful and urged a return to a supposedly healthier, plant-based past.
- This nostalgic view leans heavily on USDA food “disappearance” data, which estimate supply rather than actual consumption and are acknowledged to be rough and incomplete.
- Early-1900s disappearance data counted only foods shipped across state lines, omitting locally produced and consumed items such as home-raised meat and eggs.
- With more than a quarter of workers in farming at that time, local food production likely made a large share of actual consumption invisible in the official numbers.
- Experts consider these early availability figures inadequate for serious use but still cite them because no better quantitative data are available, and there are no scientific data at all before 1900.
- In the absence of solid quantitative records, historical descriptions provide a picture of 18th- and 19th-century American eating habits.
- Accounts depict early American settlers as indifferent farmers who put little effort into crops because meat was plentiful and easy to obtain.
- Travelers and settlers described an abundance of wild game birds and animals, with flocks of birds so dense they darkened the sky and very fatty species like the now-extinct “doughbird.”
- Forests were full of animals such as bears prized for fat, raccoons, opossums, hares, and deer, while larger game like elk or bison often went unhunted due to the effort of transporting and preserving so much meat.
- One European visitor describing a Southern plantation listed various meats served, including multiple types of domestic and wild meat, and did not mention vegetables.
- Reports claim infants were fed beef even before getting teeth, indicating meat’s centrality even in early childhood diets.
- Anthony Trollope wrote that Americans ate roughly twice as much beef as Englishmen in 1861, and Charles Dickens described steak as indispensable to breakfast.
- Historical accounts assert that for roughly the first 250 years of U.S. history, even poor Americans could afford meat or fish at every meal.
- Observers thought the New World diet surpassed that of Europe largely because workers had easy access to abundant meat.
- Fictional but period-informed depictions, such as a frontier housewife worrying about an empty pork barrel, reflect how critical steady meat supply was considered for household security.
- Research summarized in Roger Horowitz’s Putting Meat on the American Table compiles scattered data to estimate actual meat intake.
- A 1909 survey of 8,000 urban Americans found that the poorest consumed about 136 pounds of meat per year, while the wealthiest ate more than 200 pounds.
- An 1851 New York Tribune food budget allocated about two pounds of meat per day for a family of five, suggesting substantial routine meat intake.
- Records indicate that enslaved people at the turn of the 18th century were allotted around 150 pounds of meat per year.
- Horowitz concludes that multiple sources together support an average 19th-century meat consumption of roughly 150–200 pounds per person annually.
- The article estimates this historic figure at about 175 pounds per person per year, contrasted with about 100 pounds per adult American today.
- Around half of today’s 100-pound meat intake is poultry, whereas historically chicken was mainly valued as an egg producer and eaten as a festive or special-occasion meat.
- Despite this long-term decline in red meat consumption, recent USDA reporting has described U.S. meat intake as being at record highs.
- Public and media discussions often link health problems to an assumed rise in meat consumption, based on these aggregated meat figures.
- The article argues these analyses are misleading because they combine red meat and poultry into a single meat category, hiding the sharp rise in chicken and the decline in red meat since the 1970s.
- When meat types are separated, the broader trend shows Americans now eat much less red meat than earlier generations.
- Historical evidence suggests early Americans ate relatively few vegetables, partly because leafy greens had short seasons and were seen as not worth extensive cultivation.
- Before large-scale global trade and supermarket distribution, having fruits and vegetables year-round in most of the U.S. was not feasible.
- Even during warmer months, many people avoided raw fruit and salad due to fears of cholera and spoilage.
- Canned vegetables became more available only with the Civil War, and even then mainly a few items such as corn, tomatoes, and peas.
- Historians Waverly Root and Richard de Rochemont concluded that it would be inaccurate to portray Americans of that period as heavy consumers of fruits or vegetables.
- A vegetarian movement took root in the United States by around 1870, but widespread suspicion of fresh produce lingered.
- Concerns about spoilage and disease linked to fresh foods began to ease only after World War I with the spread of home refrigeration.
- By modern dietary standards, which promote high intake of fruits and vegetables, the first 250 years of American eating patterns would likely be judged very poorly.
- Despite these meat-heavy and plant-poor diets, the text argues that heart disease appears to have been rare in the U.S. until the early 20th century.
- Although reliable cause-of-death statistics are limited, medical writings and clinical reports from the 1800s suggest that coronary disease was not commonly observed.
- Austin Flint, an influential 19th-century U.S. expert on heart disease, searched for cases across the country and reported encountering very few, even though he practiced in busy New York City.
- William Osler, later a founding professor at Johns Hopkins Hospital, also did not report coronary cases during his work at Montreal General Hospital in the 1870s and 1880s.
- The first clinical description of coronary thrombosis appeared only in 1912, indicating late formal recognition of this condition.
- An authoritative 1915 textbook on arterial diseases and angina pectoris made no mention of coronary thrombosis, underscoring its apparent rarity or lack of recognition at that time.
- Paul Dudley White, later known as President Eisenhower’s cardiologist, wrote before World War I that among 700 male patients at Massachusetts General Hospital, only four reported chest pain, despite many being older than 60.
- About 20 percent of the U.S. population was older than 50 in 1900, meaning there were around 10 million people in age groups typically susceptible to heart attacks.
- This age distribution challenges the idea that earlier generations simply died too young for heart disease to show up in large numbers.
- The article notes that the widely recognized heart disease “epidemic” began after a period when meat consumption had dropped significantly.
- Publication of Upton Sinclair’s The Jungle in 1906, exposing conditions in meatpacking, led U.S. meat sales to fall by about half and remain depressed for roughly two decades.
- Thus, the major rise in coronary disease followed a stretch of reduced meat eating rather than a surge in meat intake.
- Between 1909 and 1961, overall fat consumption in the U.S. did increase by about 12 percent during the era when heart attacks became more common.
- The article states this additional fat came mainly from newly available vegetable oils rather than from a higher intake of animal fat.
- Despite this historical and nutritional context, public authorities and many experts continue to assert that Americans once ate little meat and mostly plants.
- This enduring narrative underlies decades of official advice urging Americans to return to a presumed earlier, healthier, more plant-based diet.
