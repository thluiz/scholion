Alex & Books summarizes Ryan Holiday’s 38 rules for reading, based on his experience with more than 3,000 books. Holiday argues for reading constantly, favoring physical hardcovers, taking notes and keeping a commonplace book, prioritizing classics and wisdom, quitting bad books quickly, using history and literature to understand the present, and sharing good books with others.

He frames reading as a necessity and investment with high returns, encourages following footnotes and favorite authors to build expertise, and suggests tactics like rereading, embracing serendipity in bookstores, building an anti-library, and asking smart people which books changed their lives. Throughout, the focus is on deep understanding and practical application rather than skimming for the gist.

## Reading notes

- Ryan Holiday’s 38 rules stem from having read over 3,000 books, presented by Alex & Books as life-changing reading advice.
- Holiday urges reading constantly and carrying a book everywhere, using small pockets of time in places like award shows, hospitals, planes, and cars.
- He prefers physical books over other formats and specifically favors hardcovers over paperbacks.
- Although he sees pros in audiobooks if they get someone reading, he considers the physical form uniquely valuable and mentions Maryanne Wolf’s book "Proust and the Squid" on this topic.
- He recommends always having a pen while reading because he believes reading improves when you take notes.
- Holiday advises keeping a commonplace book, echoing Seneca’s idea of collecting useful, spirited sayings that can be applied directly in life.
- He suggests favoring older books, arguing that classics have endured for good reasons.
- He encourages “beating up” books through heavy use, seeing worn copies as evidence of genuine engagement, unlike pristine copies that people still call their favorites.
- Holiday recommends finding your next book through the footnotes or bibliography of the one you’re reading to trace subjects back to their core and build a knowledge base.
- When you find an author you love, he suggests reading all their work, citing how discovering another book by Cecil Woodham-Smith shaped a major portion of his own "Courage is Calling."
- He criticizes Sam Bankman-Fried’s claim that every book could be reduced to a short blog post, emphasizing that reading aims at deep understanding, not just getting the gist.
- Holiday advises buying any book that catches your interest without fixating on price, viewing reading as a necessity and a potentially life-changing investment.
- To illustrate reading as investment, he retells Warren Buffett’s story of buying Benjamin Graham’s "The Intelligent Investor" in his youth and later calling it the foundation of his multibillion-dollar fortune.
- He shares his own example of buying a cheap paperback of "Meditations" in 2006, crediting it with teaching him about life, writing, management, and the subject he now writes about.
- Holiday recommends rereading books, referencing the Stoic idea that you never step in the same river twice: the books stay the same but the reader changes.
- He dismisses speed reading as a scam, saying the real solution is simply spending a lot of time reading.
- He argues that if a book is bad, you should stop reading it, claiming that good readers often abandon many books because life is too short for unenjoyable reading.
- One rule he cites is "one hundred pages minus your age": if you’re 30 and not captivated by page 70, you stop, and as you age, your tolerance for bad books decreases.
- Holiday values serendipity in bookstores, saying many of his favorite books came from random grabs and describing bookstores as discovery engines better than algorithms.
- He suggests building an "anti-library" of unread books to humble you, highlight how much there is to learn, and serve as a resource for future deep dives.
- Quoting Emerson, he notes the habit of asking highly intelligent or successful people which books changed their lives, then reading those books himself.
- He cites Emerson’s three reading rules: only read books at least a year old, only read famed books, and only read what you like.
- To exit reading slumps, Holiday rereads favorite novels like "What Makes Sammy Run?", "The Great Gatsby", "Ask the Dust", and "The Moviegoer."
- He describes reading "Ask the Dust" and "Hunger" because Neil Strauss had praised them, suggesting that when people rave about a book or say it changed their life, you should take that seriously.
- Holiday admits he sometimes avoids very popular books out of snobbery but later regrets it because many bestsellers are good, concluding that book snobbery is unhelpful.
- He challenges claims of lacking time to read by pointing to smartphone screen time and calendars as evidence of available time.
- For understanding current events, he recommends books over breaking news, especially history, psychology, and biographies that offer information with a long half-life.
- He gives examples: "The Great Influenza" for understanding COVID, "It Can’t Happen Here" for modern threats to democracy, and "First Principles" for American politics.
- Holiday often looks up plots on Wikipedia in advance, especially for difficult works like Shakespeare or Aeschylus, privileging understanding why events happen over preserving surprise.
- He argues that readers wrongly skip prefaces and forewords, which can provide valuable context, influence, and memorable details about a work.
- Drawing on Marcus Aurelius’s lesson from Rusticus, he warns against settling for the gist and explains that he reads introductions, reviews, articles, watches videos, and consults other books to truly grasp what he’s reading.
- Holiday says intelligent readers ask themselves what they plan to do with the information they consume.
- He quotes Harry Truman: not all readers are leaders, but all leaders are readers, stating that the point of reading is personal improvement and practical use, not intellectual showmanship.
- He promotes his "Read to Lead" course as a way to expand how readers apply what they read, noting that over 10,000 people have taken it and calling it his most popular course.
- Holiday encourages reading widely, including authors you disagree with, aligning with the Stoic view that wisdom should be sought regardless of its source.
- He criticizes pretentiousness in reading, recounting Epictetus’s remark that if the dense writer Chryssipus had been better, a student would have less to brag about.
- Holiday urges readers to seek wisdom rather than isolated facts, aiming to build a body of insight that can guide real-life decisions.
- Drawing again on Seneca, he notes that people often obsess over details and pointless debates about authorship or chronology and miss a work’s core message.
- His final rule is to recommend and pass along good books to others.
