The author explains how new projects and other decisions are “green lit” at Basecamp through a simple rule: if a decision doesn’t seem likely to get you fired, you should go ahead and make it. Basecamp avoids formal decision-making structures like budgeting processes, program reviews, and long-term detailed plans, relying instead on a shared vision and strong trust in employees to move the company and product in the right direction.

Decision authority is largely implicit and varies by role and tenure, but the guiding rule is that if you don’t fear a disastrous outcome, you can decide. The author outlines three typical outcomes: most decisions help the company, some are “wrong” but harmless and informative, and a few cause problems that are still manageable because the company rarely faces single decisions that could destroy it. This setup depends on mutual trust between leadership and staff and aligns with David Marquet’s “Intent-Based Leadership,” where people state their intent instead of asking permission.

Over nearly seven years at Basecamp, the author has asked Jason and David for explicit permission only a handful of times, mostly for input on large or unusual moves, such as initial paid advertising experiments, a major infrastructure hardware spend, starting an internship program, and hiring a teammate. What was often sought was advice rather than formal approval. In contrast, most actions, including committing to substantial recurring cloud costs, long-running projects, internal systems, and product features, were undertaken without prior permission, sometimes preceded by a shared plan or followed by an announcement.

The author argues that companies that tightly restrict decision authority spend energy on who may decide instead of on doing the work, and they risk losing strong employees. An anecdote about a designer needing five levels of approval to buy a mouse at a previous job illustrates this contrast. The author notes that this trust-based, autonomy-focused approach has worked personally so far, including the choice to publish the post without asking for permission.

- Instead of formal processes, there is a high-level vision for the company and product and strong trust that people will direct their time and energy toward that vision.
- The absence of formal structures means individuals must decide how to act on their ideas on their own.

- Decisions are grouped into three broad outcomes: right decisions that benefit the company, wrong decisions that cause no harm but offer learning, and wrong decisions that cause problems.
- The author says most decisions at Basecamp fall into the beneficial category, attributing this to hiring intelligent people who make those decisions.
- Even “wrong” decisions without negative impact are seen as acceptable when they provide useful experience or lessons.
- When a wrong decision does cause harm, the company can still recover because its scale makes truly catastrophic, company-ending decisions rare.
- Basecamp’s approach to decision authority relies on trust: leadership trusts employees to be smart, capable, and aligned with the interests of the company, coworkers, and customers.
- In return, employees trust that if they act in good faith, they will be safe even when some decisions turn out poorly.
