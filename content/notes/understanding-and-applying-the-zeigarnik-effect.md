Bart Krawczyk walks through the Zeigarnik effect: people remember unfinished tasks more readily than finished ones, because an open task keeps generating cognitive tension until it closes. Bluma Zeigarnik first observed the pattern in the 1900s. In product design, showing someone how close they are to done raises the odds they push to finish.

The piece covers progress bars, checklists, and loyalty cards as ways product teams already trigger the effect, and warns that piling on too many open tasks at once can overwhelm users instead of pulling them toward completion.

## Reading notes

- Progress indicators are the most direct application: a progress bar or step counter raises completion odds both within a single lesson (Duolingo) and across a whole course or checkout flow.
- Checklists left after signup work the same way. LinkedIn's profile-completion reminder is the example cited: users keep coming back to clear the list even though ignoring it costs nothing concrete.
- Initial nudges get users to start a multi-step flow and then leave them alone; once a task is half-done, most people finish it later rather than abandon it mid-way.
- Gamification (levels, badges, milestones) draws on the same effect, and works best paired with a visible progress indicator so users see exactly how close a badge is to unlocking.
- Incomplete-task reminders (cart abandonment emails, a to-do app nudging about unfinished items) bring back churned users by reactivating that same tension.
- A coffee-loyalty-card experiment is cited as the "initial endowment" hack: cardholders who started with a "welcome bonus" of three pre-filled stamps out of ten returned more often than cardholders who needed all eight stamps from zero, even though both groups needed seven more stamps to redeem.
- Closing caution: overloading users with too many simultaneous open tasks backfires. The tactic only works when the progress being tracked leads somewhere the user actually cares about. A leveling system falls flat in a single-player product but works in community-based ones.
