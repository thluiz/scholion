Peter Clayborne argues that anarchy is not a promise of perfection, but a way to expand the parts of life that already feel free, mutual, and alive. He ties that hope to criticism of wealth, punishment, war, and rigid assumptions about how large societies must be organized.

## Reading notes

- He uses Richard Cory to show that material wealth can coexist with deep social misery.
- He says anarchy hopes people do not have to choose between bread and love.
- He rejects utopia as impossible, but says conflict, suffering, and death do not have to be self-imposed.
- He argues that the best parts of life already look anarchic, and could be expanded.
- He opposes sex offender registries and says the evidence for their effectiveness is limited and mixed.
- He says public notification can worsen recidivism and make reintegration harder.
- He highlights the harm registries do to families and the criminalization of children.
- He disputes Dunbar’s number as a firm limit on social organization.
- He says peaceful communities differ more by relationship quality than by size or isolation.
- He imagines an anarchist society as overlapping and interconnected layers, not a single fixed unit.
- He says borders, invasion, and occupation would become unthinkable in a world whose communities are deeply intertwined.
- He compares freer human societies to forests rather than machines.
- He ends by framing anarchy as a response to change, not as a way to preserve the present.
