Seth Godin argues that school was built for the industrial age, when society needed compliant workers, standardized testing, and mass production. In a connected economy, those goals no longer match what students need. He says school should be rethought around leadership, passion, judgment, creativity, and the ability to connect information, people, and ideas.

He uses examples from Harlem Village Academies, Khan Academy, FIRST, and other schools and programs to show that motivated students and trusted teachers can do far more than a system built on fear and compliance. The manifesto repeatedly asks what school is for and argues that the answer must shift from producing obedient workers to helping students dream, learn, and make things happen.

## Reading notes

- School was designed for industrial-era needs such as obedience, compliance, and mass processing.
- The connected economy rewards leadership, initiative, judgment, and passion more than standardized performance.
- The manifesto argues that changing school means changing what schools are asked to produce.
- Standardized testing and multiple-choice sorting are presented as tools for industrial sorting, not for real learning.
- Fear is described as the main mechanism used to keep large school systems orderly, but it also destroys passion.
- The text says attitudes such as courage, creativity, commitment, and willpower can be taught and unlearned.
- Schools should teach students to connect dots, not just collect them.
- The Harlem Village Academy is presented as an example of strict but respectful schooling with engaged teachers and students.
- Digital tools, online courses, and connection make access to information abundant, so teaching should focus less on delivery of facts and more on motivation and guidance.
- Teachers matter most as people who transfer emotion, create trust, and coach students toward learning.
- The manifesto argues that school should cultivate dreams instead of narrowing them to safe, low-level ambitions.
- College is criticized as a famous-brand sorting system that often measures status more than education.
- The future of school is framed as making students able to lead, solve problems, and keep learning in a changing world.
