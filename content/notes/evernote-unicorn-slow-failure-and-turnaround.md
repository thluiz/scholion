Evernote, founded in 2004 and once a high-flying unicorn, shifted from a simple note-taking app to an overextended lifestyle brand with socks, notebooks, side apps and conferences, before growth slowed and leadership churn, layoffs and office closures set in. The company’s long struggle, now under C.E.O. Ian Small, undercuts Silicon Valley’s “fail fast” mantra, showing how heavily funded start-ups can linger in a drawn-out rehabilitation instead of collapsing quickly.

Evernote chased a “Nike for your mind” vision and three-comma valuations, adding products and perks while amassing more than 150 million registered users and hundreds of millions in venture capital. As hype faded, attempts to pivot into business software ran into product gaps and fragmented apps, leading to weak enterprise sales and employee malaise, even as rumors of a big-tech acquisition failed to materialize. After a wave of executive departures and “death spiral” headlines in 2018, Small publicly acknowledged Evernote’s buggy, unstable foundation and set expectations for a year of fixes, while aiming for positive cash flow around $100 million in annual revenue.
