The study argues that there is a measurable sweet spot for learning. In simple tasks with clear correct and incorrect answers, the best learning rate appears when failure happens about 15% of the time, or when responses are correct 85% of the time.

The authors tested this idea with machine-learning experiments and found the same pattern in previous animal-learning studies. They suggest it may also apply to perceptual learning in humans, but they limit the claim to simple tasks and do not extend it to general school grades.

## Reading notes

- Learning is strongest when a task is challenging enough to leave room for improvement, but not so hard that progress stops.
- In the experiments, computers learned fastest when they reached 85% accuracy on two-choice tasks.
- The 85% rule also matched patterns found in earlier animal-learning studies.
- The authors think the idea may fit perceptual learning, such as learning to distinguish tumors from non-tumors in images.
- The study does not claim that students should aim for a B average, because it only tested simple tasks with clear right and wrong answers.
- The authors say the work may help education research on more complex forms of learning.
