The author describes looking for a new job after six years working remotely and valuing that flexibility, especially with family responsibilities. In several talks with interested companies, he often heard that remote work from Berlin might be allowed "for the right candidate." He argues this phrase is effectively a hidden "no" and should be treated as a major warning sign.

He explains that "for the right candidate" can mean a company is reluctant to support remote work, possibly driven by desperation or cost concerns, rather than a deliberate choice to become a distributed team. Without a conscious decision and cultural and workflow changes to support remote employees, remote hires risk becoming second‑class workers and the odd ones out in a colocated setup. He adds that these "right candidates" are often held to higher standards because the company broke its own rules to hire them, which can foster resentment among colleagues who are denied remote options and create a false hierarchy where the remote person must outperform onsite peers. He concludes that when a role says "remote considered for the right candidate," it usually means accepting avoidable difficulties to work there, and candidates should decide with clear eyes whether that tradeoff is worth it.

- If leadership does not fully commit to remote work, the author believes any remote arrangement is likely to fail over time.
- The "right candidate" often ends up held to a higher performance standard because hiring them required breaking normal company rules.
- This dynamic can create resentment among existing team members, especially those who want to work remotely but are not allowed to.
- It can also create a false hierarchy where the "right candidate" is expected to produce about twice as much as colocated coworkers just to be seen as contributing equally.

- He acknowledges that in some circumstances the opportunity might still justify that extra pain, but urges candidates to be clear on the tradeoffs they are accepting.
