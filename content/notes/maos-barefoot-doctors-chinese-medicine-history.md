The article traces how Chinese alternative medicine entered Western pop culture by focusing on Mao Zedong's barefoot doctor program. In mid‑20th‑century China, modern, science‑based medicine was concentrated in cities, while rural populations relied on traditional practices. Mao’s government created barefoot doctors by training rural farmers with limited medical instruction and sending them back to their villages, where they used basic first aid, hygiene, referrals, and, when available, modern drugs. Because resources were scarce, they also relied heavily on herbal and traditional remedies, systematized in _A Barefoot Doctor's Manual_. When this manual reached the West, especially in a cut-down edition that removed conventional treatments, it gave a distorted impression that Chinese medicine consisted mainly of traditional therapies. The article argues that educated Chinese doctors used modern medicine, that acupuncture in China was mostly an adjunct for pain relief, and that both promoters and skeptics mischaracterize Mao’s use of alternative medicine.

## Reading notes

- Promoters claim Chinese alternative medicine reflects ancient wisdom widely used in China, while skeptics say it was used only in rural areas due to lack of conventional care and low cost.
- The article sets its context in revolutionary mid‑20th‑century China during the Great Leap Forward and Cultural Revolution.
- Most Chinese in that era lived isolated from modern science and technology, with worldviews shaped by local traditions.
- Rural populations generally did not see formal medical care and relied on traditional treatments based on long‑standing, non‑scientific beliefs.
- In contrast, hospitals in major cities in industrialized regions, including Chinese cities, practiced cutting‑edge medicine.
- Chinese oncologists were using chemotherapy for cancer, similar to practices in other industrialized countries.
- Patients in severe pain in Chinese hospitals received opiates as part of conventional pain management.
- By 1949, the Chinese Academy of Sciences was already a leading global research institution in life sciences and medicine.
- Acupuncture was in relatively wide use in Chinese hospitals but only for pain relief, not as a primary disease treatment.
- According to the article, in China acupuncture was always combined with conventional painkillers when available and treated as an ornamental adjunct.
- Urban residents, less than 2% of the national population by 1964, received nearly all of China's healthcare budget and benefits of modern medicine.
- The vast majority living in remote rural areas received little of this healthcare despite having similar needs.
- Mao’s government tried from 1949 to persuade doctors to relocate from cities to the countryside, but these efforts largely failed.
- Traveling medical teams would visit rural provinces briefly, then return to city hospitals where they could earn a better income.
- Schistosomiasis became a prominent symbol of the lack of rural healthcare, causing organ damage and notable abdominal swelling.
- Mao’s solution, published in _Red Flag_ in 1968, was to train farmers and send them back to serve as medical workers in their own villages.
- Within a few years, about 150,000 doctors and 350,000 paramedics were deployed, totaling half a million barefoot health workers.
- Barefoot doctor candidates had to be high school graduates and typically received three to six months of training at nearby hospitals.
- Their compensation included work points, the communist system for measuring family productivity and entitlement to extra grain or goods.
- Barefoot doctors were required to spend no more than half their time on medical work and continue agricultural labor to maintain farm output.
- Some barefoot doctors later attended medical school and became licensed physicians.
- Professor Chen Zhu, later China’s Minister of Health and Vice President of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, began as a barefoot doctor in Jiangxi Province.
- By 1970, China had a large corps of paramedics positioned in rural areas but lacked the instruments, supplies, and drugs to fully equip them.
- Treatments existed for schistosomiasis, but barefoot doctors had no funding to provide the necessary drugs.
- To combat schistosomiasis without drugs, barefoot doctors mobilized labor to clear ponds and streams and eliminate snails spreading the parasites.
- This environmental control strategy significantly reduced schistosomiasis, from about ten million cases a year to just over two million within fifteen years, nearly eliminating it in some regions.
- Barefoot doctors were trained in first aid for injuries and emergency medical situations.
- Their training included pre‑ and post‑natal care and basic hygiene practices such as handwashing before meals.
- They were taught to recognize serious conditions requiring medical treatment and refer those patients to hospitals.
- For less severe illnesses, wellness concerns, and simple treatable conditions, barefoot doctors could prescribe medications when available.
- Medication was often scarce and too expensive for peasants, making widespread pharmaceutical treatment impractical.
- Mao lacked the resources to open and stock 150,000 new pharmacies to support the barefoot doctors.
- The Revolutionary Health Committee of Hunan Province created _A Barefoot Doctor's Manual_ to serve as a comprehensive field guide.
- The manual covers basic anatomy, contraception, hygiene, diagnosis, and instructions for treating a wide range of expected illnesses.
- Anticipating drug shortages, the manual devotes much of its content to medicinal herbs, including identification, collection, preparation, and believed indications.
- When the manual reached Western audiences, it helped transform Chinese alternative medicine from a curiosity into a popular cultural trend.
- The manual did not depict practices in urban hospitals or routine work of fully licensed doctors, focusing instead on minimally equipped rural medics.
- Western readers thus received a skewed picture, seeing what the least resourced Chinese medics used in the worst circumstances.
- The manual generally advocates using conventional treatments when possible and traditional therapies when modern options are unavailable.
- For Japanese encephalitis, the original manual suggests acupuncture, mud packs, toad‑based compresses, herbal teas, and also intravenous conventional drugs.
- According to the article, nearly every disease entry includes recommended conventional treatments plus fallback traditional methods.
- The author examined English translations of _A Barefoot Doctor's Manual_ and noted large variations in page count, from 372 to 960 pages.
- A widely published US edition by The Running Press is titled _A Barefoot Doctor's Manual: A Concise Edition of the Classic Work of Eastern Herbal Medicine._
- An earlier, longer Running Press edition carried the subtitle _The American Translation of the Official Chinese Paramedical Manual_ and is now out of print.
- Snippets of the full‑length edition on Google Books show the complete conventional treatment for Japanese encephalitis.
- In the "concise" edition checked by the author, all references to conventional medicine for that disease had been removed.
- The article states that about 600 pages of conventional medical information were cut from the book for Western readers.
- The remaining concise edition functions mainly as a catalog of traditional Chinese treatments, without indicating that barefoot doctors also used modern medicine.
- The editing of the concise edition is characterized as intentional shaping to promote alternative medicine under the image of ancient Chinese wisdom.
- The article concludes that, in the era of science‑based medicine, knowledgeable Chinese doctors did not primarily rely on alternative therapies.
- It argues that the claim that alternative medicine is validated by Chinese usage misrepresents actual Chinese medical practice.
- The article also challenges the skeptical view that Mao pushed alternative medicine solely for cost reasons.
- It presents the barefoot doctor initiative as a genuine effort to extend the best available care, resorting to alternative therapies only when modern treatments were lacking.
