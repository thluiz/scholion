The article says time-blocking can replace a to-do list by turning the day into scheduled blocks of time. It presents this as a way to see when plans are unrealistic, stay focused, and use time more efficiently.

It also says time-blocking can improve psychological well-being. Research cited in the piece suggests many to-do list items are never finished, while putting tasks into a specific date, time, and duration can reduce the stress of unfinished work.

## Reading notes

- Time-blocking organizes the day into time slots, with each period devoted to one task or several tasks.
- This approach makes it easier to see when you are being unrealistic about your time.
- Cal Newport says it helps you schedule work when it makes the most sense, batch small tasks, and handle hard work during longer stretches.
- It also gives more accurate feedback about free time and how long recurring tasks actually take.
- Kevin Kruse says organizing time instead of tasks adds discipline and order.
- The article cites research from I Done This saying 41% of to-do list items are never completed.
- Kruse says high performers he interviewed talked about calendars and organization, not to-do lists.
- The Zeigarnik effect is used to explain why unfinished tasks weigh on people.
- Putting tasks into a specific date, time, and duration can reduce stress and help people sleep more soundly.
