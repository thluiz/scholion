A study from the Norwegian National Centre of Ageing and Health, with Columbia Public Health and the Butler Columbia Aging Center, links moderate to high physical activity at work across the life course with greater risk of cognitive impairment in later life. The authors compare occupational activity from ages 33 to 65 with outcomes after age 70 and report higher risk among people in physically demanding jobs.

## Reading notes

- The study uses a life-course perspective on occupational physical activity, following work histories from ages 33 to 65 and relating them to cognitive health after 70.
- It includes 7,005 participants from the HUNT4 70+ Study, with 902 dementia diagnoses and 2,407 cases of mild cognitive impairment.
- People in physically demanding jobs in the later part of working life had a 15.5% risk of dementia or mild cognitive impairment, compared with 9% among people in jobs with low physical demands.
- The authors present this as part of the physical activity paradox, where leisure-time physical activity is linked to better cognitive outcomes while work-related physical activity is linked to worse ones.
- The paper says earlier studies often used a single occupation measure and were usually self-reported, while this study uses occupational trajectories across working life.
- The authors say the findings point to the need for preventive measures and follow-up for people with high lifetime occupational physical activity.
