The page explains how understanding the intent behind common interview questions helps candidates give stronger answers and reduce anxiety. It walks through frequent questions, showing what interviewers look for and giving concrete tactics, like asking for scope, doing company research, giving salary ranges, staying positive about past employers, demonstrating self-awareness and growth, and using specific examples to show problem-solving, communication, and time management.

## Reading notes

- The authors frame interviewing as stressful for many people and argue that familiarity with common question types makes it easier to craft effective responses.
- They surveyed Hired candidates mid-interview to identify which questions felt most challenging and to surface why employers ask them.
- Practicing with mock interviews or self-run drills is presented as the best way to apply the guide.
- For “Tell me about yourself,” they say candidates often ramble because they ignore the question’s intent.
- They suggest first asking the interviewer where to start so the scope is clear, which helps keep the answer focused.
- Once scope is set, candidates should share specific past experiences that connect directly to the role and company needs.
- They give an example of tailoring one’s story to a ruby development role by focusing on how and where the candidate learned and advanced in ruby.
- The pro tip for this question is to keep answers as relevant as possible to the role to show potential value.
- For “Why are you interested in this opportunity?” the key signal is whether the candidate has researched the company and role.
- Interviewers also evaluate passion for the company, mission, industry, and product, and will often pass if that passion is missing.
- They recommend using sites like Crunchbase, Mattermark, and Hired’s Company Directory to learn about the company.
- A pro tip is to research who is interviewing you, including their career path and current role, to help build rapport and shape answers.
- On “What are your salary expectations?” they note the topic can arise early or later in the process, so candidates should be ready.
- They advise considering the salary on one’s Hired profile, current pay, cost of living, relocation, and commute when forming a range.
- The recommended approach is to be honest while sharing a comfortable range to leave room for negotiation.
- Their pro tip is to avoid giving a single firm number so as not to scare employers off or undersell oneself before knowing the role’s scope.
- For “Why are you looking to leave your current company?” they warn that hiring managers dislike hearing negativity about current employers.
- Complaints about managers, compensation, or work are discouraged because they suggest the candidate will later complain about the new employer too.
- They recommend focusing on what initially attracted you to your current role, what has changed with a positive framing, and your future ambitions.
- In discussing “biggest strength and area of growth,” they emphasize the question has strong weight in evaluation.
- One goal of this question is to see whether candidates are reflective and self-aware about their strengths, weaknesses, and growth areas.
- A second goal is to see if candidates proactively work on development instead of being complacent about skill gaps.
- A third goal is to assess culture fit and compatibility with the management structure, such as communication style alignment with a potential manager.
- They give an example where weak communication in both candidate and manager could signal a poor match even if it is not a core duty.
- The pro tip is to name 2–3 concrete ways you are improving growth areas and explain your action plan in depth to show dedication to development.
- For “Tell me about a time your work responsibilities got a little overwhelming,” they say it is a chance to show prioritization and time management.
- Candidates should choose a specific scenario with a heavy workload and explain how they ranked tasks and decided priorities.
- They advise being specific about the setting, role, and actions taken to handle competing responsibilities.
- The example answer describes a Senior Software Engineer who paused to assess task priority, timelines, and daily needs, then built and worked through a ranked list, seeking help when needed.
- In “Give me an example of a time where you had a difference of opinion with a team member,” they suggest briefly describing the project, then focusing on the conflict handling.
- Interviewers use this to gauge how candidates behave with colleagues and how they approach workplace problems.
- An effective answer values listening to different opinions and emphasizes collaborative resolution and reaching the best outcome together.
- For “Tell me about a challenge you faced recently in your role,” they say hiring managers are assessing problem-solving and ability to handle adversity.
- They propose a three-part structure: explain what went wrong with context, describe what you did to address it, and share the resolution.
- Candidates are encouraged to spend more time on their reasoning and actions than on the initial problem description.
- They recommend selecting an example that highlights problem-solving or shows a lesson learned for future situations.
- The sample answer shows a team facing multiple issues before a project start, where the candidate encourages alternatives, takes action, keeps schedule realistic, and reaches a successful result.
- For “Where do you want to be in five years?” they frame the question as about long-term fit and mutual investment.
- Candidates should set realistic goals that align with their experience while still showing ambition and a thought-out direction.
- The answer should reflect how the position fits with one’s goals and growth possibilities inside the company.
- They add that hiring managers listen for signs that the candidate will stay and contribute, not just quickly move up.
- The pro tip warns that simply saying you want your manager’s job in five years is weak; they advise a more thoughtful, role-anchored response.
- Their example answer highlights enthusiasm for developing deep expertise in a sector and gradually taking on more managerial and project leadership responsibilities.
- For “Tell me about a time you needed information from someone who wasn’t responsive,” they say the question targets communication skills and handling colleague-related obstacles.
- Candidates should explain why the information mattered and walk through the steps they took to obtain it.
- Interviewers want to see empathy for the other person’s situation combined with assertive, clear communication to still reach the goal.
- The pro tip is to specify 2–3 steps taken, such as follow-up tactics or channel changes.
- Their example scenario involves working with an external partner who was slow to reply, where the candidate empathizes with their workload but recognizes the impact on planning.
- In that example, the candidate uses reminders for follow-ups and sets phone meetings to gather more information at once, which improves deadline performance for both sides.
