A digital garden is presented as a living network of connected ideas rather than a file cabinet or a fixed index. The page argues that a bit of chaos helps new ideas emerge, because it makes unexpected connections more likely and supports a more organic form of thinking and note-taking.

It also describes a personal workflow built around low-friction capture, linking by concept, simple note names, and using search as an entry point. Seeds become saplings, and saplings become fruits when notes develop into shareable writing, projects, and other outputs.

## Reading notes

- A digital garden is framed as a messy, interconnected space that encourages association over strict order.
- Chaos and entropy are treated as useful because they help separate ideas meet and produce new possibilities.
- The author prefers a garden-like system over Zettelkasten-style or folder-based organization because upfront classification creates friction.
- Thinking is described as non-linear and non-hierarchical, so the ideal tool should let insights emerge from chaos.
- Writing is a form of knowledge distillation that clarifies thought and creates a shareable representation of ideas.
- Public writing is valuable because it invites feedback and strengthens half-formed thoughts.
- Notes should link by concept rather than exact match, and names should stay simple.
- Search matters because people often do not know the exact term they need and must enter through a node before recalling related ideas.
- The author divides knowledge flow into seeds, saplings, and fruits: capture, individual notes, and derivative output.
- The goal of the system is to support experimentation, learning, sharing, and public growth of ideas.
