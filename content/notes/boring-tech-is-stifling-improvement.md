The post uses the I-95 collapse repair as an example of professionals choosing a better material and bypassing a slow supply chain to reopen the road quickly. It then applies that contrast to software, where the author argues that “boring technology” is useful but can be pushed too far.

- The I-95 repair used foamed glass gravel as infill, showing that the people in charge found a better way to do the work and deliberately avoided the slow supply chain.
- The author links this example to software and says the “boring technology movement” borrows from the 2015 “Choose Boring Technology” essay and its deck.
- The manifesto’s ideas about an innovation token, operability, and unfamiliarity are presented as useful.
- The author argues that too many different tools can be a problem, but a one-size-fits-all single tool can be extreme in the other direction.
- Monocultures can create a single point of failure, vendor lock-in, and missed savings from competition among vendors.
- Overreliance on boring tech can drain enjoyment from the work and turn away people who are excited about computing.
- It can also chill innovation by discouraging deviation from existing practice.
- It can produce stagnation by making teams overlook better ways of doing things and by privileging the familiar over progress.
- The author gives examples of incremental improvement such as introducing Kotlin into a Java shop and rewriting stored procedures in PL/Python.
- The conclusion is that the original manifesto has value, but teams should not over-index on the current state and lose chances to improve.
