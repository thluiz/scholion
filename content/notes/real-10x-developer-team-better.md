The article argues that the 10x developer myth misses what actually drives engineering outcomes. Team success depends more on the quality of social learning communities than on any one person, so leaders should value people who learn and help others learn too.

It presents communities of practice as a way to break down silos, support cross-functional collaboration, build trust, and preserve reusable knowledge. It also links learning at work to hiring, retention, and performance, and says managers should model learning, make time for it, share mistakes, and provide resources.

## Reading notes

- The article rejects the idea that one highly productive engineer determines whether a technology project succeeds or fails.
- It says organizations should be stronger than any single person.
- It recommends building communities of practice around products, tools, and technologies.
- Communities of practice are described as self-directed groups that learn through regular interaction around a shared interest.
- The article says these communities break down silos, encourage collaboration, build trust, and accelerate innovation.
- It says communities of practice matter especially in new fields like GenAI.
- They help colleagues share experiences, combine expertise, solve problems, and refine best practices.
- Fostering community learning is presented as part of the core job, not extra work.
- The article says access to learning resources, experts, and vetted knowledge helps developers self-serve and ramp up quickly.
- It cites survey data showing that learning opportunities affect happiness at work and retention.
- It recommends that managers model learning, protect time for learning, share mistakes, and provide learning resources.
- It concludes that expecting individuals to be 10x developers leads to burnout and shifts unsustainable responsibility onto them.
