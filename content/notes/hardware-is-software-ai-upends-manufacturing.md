Boom’s CEO Blake Scholl argues that most hardware engineering is actually software-like calculation work, currently done with ad‑hoc spreadsheet “baby software”. At Boom they aim to formalize these calculations as real software and algorithms for designing hardware, using AI so that software engineers define architectures while hardware experts supply physics. This already lets tiny teams design turbomachinery and aircraft structures that previously required far more engineers.

The article extends this logic to examples like NASA using AI to design cheaper, lighter, faster-to-design parts, and Casey Handmer using Claude to generate a working radar PCB and code. It suggests GPU design and software moats like NVIDIA’s CUDA can be recreated by strong models, pushing R&D toward automation. Over time, AI is expected to strip out much of the cost tied to design, documentation, certification, and other white‑collar work, while also producing cheaper, better parts.

Using solar panels and SpaceX rockets as analogies, the author introduces the “idiot index”: when final prices far exceed raw material costs, better intelligence can slash waste. As AI accelerates learning curves, raw materials become a larger share of product cost. Blue‑collar work is also targeted: the text argues that more motions will be automated through improved AI‑designed machinery and humanoid robots, which industry expects to transform physical work in the next few years.

The piece quantifies human costs at roughly a quarter of manufacturing budgets, with parts and materials over 60%. If AI cuts about 20–25% of human costs at each value‑chain step and also reduces material usage, the author estimates direct company savings near 37%. Propagated through suppliers, total value‑chain costs could fall around 74%, leaving raw materials, energy, and residual human oversight as the main expenses.

As humans are replaced by capital—AI inference and robots—employment shifts toward AI and robot makers, but likely not enough to offset job losses in manufacturing. The article lists consequences: AI first hits management and R&D, then blue‑collar work as humanoids scale; competition drives lower costs into lower prices and higher quality; demand for manufactured goods rises, yet job destruction in manufacturing remains severe; raw materials gain relative importance, making mining strategically central in an AI world.

Finally, the author sketches two schools of thought on timing. One expects slow change due to politics, incumbents, physical testing, and robot ramp‑up, implying a 5–20 year horizon for dramatic shifts, with startups using AI to outcompete legacy manufacturers. The other expects AI‑driven organizations, including tech labs and private equity, to rapidly take over or reform manufacturing firms and even automate physical testing, compressing the transition into 1–4 years.
