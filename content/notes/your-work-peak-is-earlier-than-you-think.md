Arthur C. Brooks reflects on an encounter with an aging hero on a plane who felt useless despite public admiration, which triggers his own fear of professional decline. Drawing on happiness research, creativity studies, and philosophy, he argues that high achievement does not protect against later-life misery, that many cognitively demanding careers peak surprisingly early, and that clinging to fading status deepens suffering. He contrasts Darwin’s despondency with Bach’s reinvention as a teacher, ties this to the shift from fluid to crystallized intelligence, and brings in Hindu ashramas and Buddhist practices to propose a different path. His roadmap centers on leaving peak roles on one’s own terms, serving others through teaching and mentorship, deepening spiritual life, and prioritizing relationships over résumé virtues.

## Reading notes

- On a late-night flight, Brooks overhears an elderly, world-famous man tell his wife he wishes he were dead, then watch him light up when recognized by others, highlighting a clash between public reverence and private despair.
- The incident troubles Brooks, then 51 and professionally successful, and pushes him to ask whether he can avoid future misery when his own career slows.
- He decides to study professional decline as a social scientist, seeking ways to turn it from a source of dread into a chance for progress.
- Happiness research summarized by Jonathan Rauch suggests well-being typically dips through the 30s and 40s, bottoms out in the early 50s, then rises again into the 70s, though some—especially men—see sharp drops and higher depression and suicide rates after 75.
- Studies of older adults show that feeling useless is strongly associated with greater disability and higher mortality, pointing to perceived irrelevance as a key driver of late-life unhappiness.
- Research on gifted individuals finds that being labeled highly intelligent early in life can correlate with worse psychological well-being around age 80, possibly due to unrealistic expectations centered on intellect.
- Evidence from former elite athletes indicates that the end of a high-performing sports career often brings low control, depression, addiction, or worse, underscoring the pain of losing a core source of identity.
- Brooks introduces the "Principle of Psychoprofessional Gravitation": the suffering of professional oblivion increases with prior prestige and emotional investment in that status.
- He illustrates this principle with Charles Darwin, who achieved great early fame but later stalled, missed Mendel’s genetics work, felt left behind, and lamented lacking the heart for long investigations.
- Brooks recounts his own early dream of becoming a great French-horn player, initial professional success, then unexplained technical decline in his 20s despite intense practice and top teachers.
- A humiliating fall off the Carnegie Hall stage and years of deteriorating performance in Barcelona and Florida lead him, after earning degrees via distance learning, to abandon music at 31 for a doctorate in public policy.
- Even after building a new academic career, he continues to dream about performing, showing how deeply early vocational decline can sting, though giving up young allowed him to redirect his life.
- He notes that while early decline is expected in physical fields like sports, many in knowledge work assume stable quality until very old age, with some planning to "push it until the wheels came off."
- Citing Dean Keith Simonton, he reports that success and productivity in creative careers tend to rise for about 20 years after career start, peaking around 50 for someone who begins at 30, then declining afterward.
- Benjamin Jones’s work on scientists and inventors shows that major breakthroughs most often occur in the late 30s, with innovation probabilities rising in the 20s–30s and then falling through later decades.
- Data on Nobel laureates and major inventors indicates that the chance of producing a major innovation at 70 is roughly as low as at 20.
- Simonton’s analysis of literary careers suggests poets tend to peak in their early 40s, while novelists more often hit the top of bestseller lists in their 40s and 50s, with a steep drop after 70.
- Entrepreneurial success tends to come earlier still, with many billion-dollar start-up founders in their 20s and 30s and most under 50, implying earlier peaks in that domain.
- Broader labor research, including work from Boston College, shows age-related decline in varied occupations such as policing and nursing, not just in elite or creative fields.
- Performance data on Major League Baseball umpires and mandatory retirement rules for air traffic controllers illustrate how error-prone older professionals can become in roles demanding rapid, precise decisions.
- Brooks concludes that in jobs relying on processing speed and analytic capability, noticeable decline often comes sooner than people believe.
- He contrasts the careers of Bach and Darwin to show different responses to midlife fade: both were prodigies with lasting fame, but they handled later stagnation differently.
- Bach’s baroque style was eclipsed by newer classical trends, including those championed by his son C.P.E. Bach, whose prestige rose as his father’s music went out of fashion.
- Instead of growing bitter, Bach shifted his focus from innovation to instruction, spending his final decade on "The Art of Fugue" as a teaching work for students and future generations.
- Bach’s later life emphasizes being a teacher and family man, and he dies respected and fulfilled, serving as a model for constructive adaptation to decline.
- Brooks uses Raymond Cattell’s distinction between fluid and crystallized intelligence: fluid involves problem-solving and novel reasoning, peaks relatively early, and then declines; crystallized is accumulated knowledge and its use, growing into later life.
- Fields that depend heavily on fluid intelligence, like tech entrepreneurship or high-innovation roles, tend to peak earlier, while those that draw more on accumulated knowledge, like history, tend to peak later.
- Simonton’s work indicates that poets reach half their lifetime output by around 40, whereas historians reach that point closer to 60, illustrating the different timing of peak productivity.
- Brooks suggests a practical strategy: shift one’s career emphasis over time away from innovation and toward capacities that remain strong or even improve, such as teaching and synthesizing knowledge.
- Evidence from higher education shows that older professors in knowledge-heavy fields often receive the best student evaluations, highlighting teaching as an ability that can improve or remain strong very late.
- Many professors plan to retire well after the national average retirement age, some into their 70s and 80s, reflecting the longevity of teaching-based roles.
- Administrators worry about reduced late-career research output and blocked slots for younger scholars, which Brooks frames as an opportunity to reweight older faculty toward teaching and younger ones toward research.
- In his think-tank experience, Brooks observes that the most original insights usually come from scholars in their 30s and early 40s, while the clearest explainers and synthesizers tend to be in their mid-60s and beyond.
- He argues that older professionals in any field can look for ways to share knowledge meaningfully, aligning with the natural advantages of crystallized intelligence.
- Brooks criticizes the pursuit of ever-greater wealth as a way to measure self-worth, noting that many rich people stay on a "hamster wheel" in hopes that more money will finally make them feel successful and ready to die.
- He notes that Eastern philosophies warn that attachment to acquisition fosters vanity and obscures one’s essential nature, undermining the search for happiness.
- As he ages, he sees that more books or achievements will not notably increase his satisfaction and may only delay accepting the end of certain pursuits.
- He proposes treating life as a block of marble to carve by removing unnecessary obligations, possessions, and relationships, creating a "reverse bucket list" to reveal a truer self.
- Seeking clarification of that "self" leads him to Palakkad in South India to meet guru Sri Nochur Venkataraman (Acharya), who is focused on enlightenment rather than Western self-help quests.
- Brooks presents Acharya with the problem of accomplished people who suffer as they lose hard-won abilities, asking if this suffering is unavoidable or if there is an escape.
- Acharya explains the Hindu four stages of life (ashramas): Brahmacharya (youthful learning), Grihastha (career, wealth, family), Vanaprastha (retiring into a more spiritual and service-oriented focus), and Sannyasa (full dedication to enlightenment).
- He warns that many people get stuck in Grihastha, overly attached to rewards such as money, power, sex, and prestige, trying to extend that phase indefinitely.
- Vanaprastha, typically starting around 50, involves deliberately shifting attention away from professional ambition toward spirituality, service, and wisdom, even while many still work.
- Sannyasa traditionally could mean leaving family, taking vows, and spending remaining years in study and prayer, but the broader point is to prioritize transcendent aims over worldly success in old age.
- Reflecting on the man on the plane, Acharya says he failed to leave Grihastha and stayed addicted to worldly rewards, letting his worth rest on past achievements and derivative recognition.
- According to Acharya’s view, by neglecting Vanaprastha the man also missed the bliss associated with Sannyasa, illustrating the cost of clinging to professional identity.
- Brooks applies this framework to high-achieving lawyers, executives, entrepreneurs, and leaders who live by their wits and revere fluid intelligence, warning that they need to scale back worldly ambition earlier than feels natural.
- He connects this to David Brooks’s distinction between résumé virtues (career achievements, comparative status) and eulogy virtues (ethical and spiritual qualities people will remember at one’s funeral).
- Brooks suggests that midlife and beyond are best spent pursuing the eulogy virtues that matter deeply, not just stacking more résumé items.
- He suspects that his own fear of professional decline is rooted in an underlying fear of death, which manifests as denial of any weakening of his résumé virtues.
- This denial, he argues, distracts from focusing on eulogy virtues that actually bring him joy, making it harder to adjust to change.
- To counter fear of death, he cites Buddhist corpse meditation practices and Montaigne’s advice to familiarize oneself with death so it loses its strangeness.
- Psychological research comparing imagined versus actual last words suggests that people imagining imminent death are far more negative than those truly near death, implying that abstract fear can be worse than confronting reality.
- Brooks concludes that routinely contemplating mortality, rather than numbing oneself with pursuits of success, can lessen fear, highlight life’s temporariness, and make each day feel more meaningful.
- He embraces the idea that decline is unavoidable and earlier than expected, but maintains that misery is not, provided one accepts the rhythm of changing abilities and shifts focus to higher priorities.
- Seeking specific guidance, he develops four commitments: Jump, Serve, Worship, and Connect, each representing a concrete adaptation strategy.
- Under "Jump," he argues that trying to sustain peak performance indefinitely by leaning on fading fluid intelligence is futile, and that one should savor achievements but step away on one’s own terms.
- Acting on this, he resigns as president of the American Enterprise Institute around the time of writing, even before noticing performance decline, citing the job’s reliance on fluid intelligence and his desire for more spiritual time.
- He notes that long-serving executives can harm institutions by staying too long, so leaving also serves the organization he loves.
- He acknowledges that walking away can feel like a partial death, likening it, via Tibetan Buddhism’s concept of bardo, to stepping toward a precipice between death and rebirth.
- Recognizing his own privilege in being able to resign, he stresses that even those who must keep working can gradually detach from external rewards like power, fame, status, and money while remaining employed.
- Under "Serve," Brooks calls for shifting from self-focused professional ambition to activities oriented toward other people, a move he admits is difficult given his egotism but sees as necessary because selfishness carries heavy costs.
- He argues that service aligns with later-life strengths such as teaching and mentorship, and says he is reorienting his career toward university teaching, hoping his most productive years will come in this phase.
- Under "Worship," he clarifies he is not pitting work against spirituality but urging people to cultivate their spiritual side; in his case, he plans to spend more of his remaining years in the practice of Roman Catholicism.
- He suggests that if one loosens attachment to worldly outcomes and focuses work on enriching and teaching others, work itself can become a spiritual or transcendental practice.
- He cites Bach’s statement that music should ultimately honor God and refresh the soul, offering this as a model for any vocation aimed at inner renewal rather than ego gratification.
- Brooks describes how Bach signed his manuscripts "Soli Deo gloria" but left his final fugue from The Art of Fugue unfinished, with C.P.E. Bach noting that the composer died at that point, symbolizing a life where work and prayer intertwined to the end.
- Under "Connect," he emphasizes that abundant research links happiness across the lifespan to the strength and number of relationships, more than to continuing professional dominance.
- He recommends deliberately demoting work to make room for deeper ties with family and friends as a way to buffer the distress of professional decline.
- He notes that focusing more on relationships does not preclude continued success, citing a Psalmic image of a flourishing tree and then reinterpreting the aspen tree as a metaphor for shared roots beneath apparent individualism.
- Aspen groves, which are vast single organisms connected underground, illustrate his point that even seemingly solitary high achievers thrive and wither as part of larger relational networks.
- He concludes that if he strengthens bonds of love and connection, his own fading will be compensated by growth and flowering in others who share those roots.
- Revisiting the plane hero, Brooks explains that initial flashes of pity were really projections of his own fear, and that as his understanding of decline and adaptation has grown, his fear has decreased.
- He now feels gratitude toward the man on the plane for providing the impetus to examine these issues and hopes that the man can eventually find the peace and joy Brooks is learning to seek.
