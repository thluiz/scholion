The post says Lean Startup began as a disciplined method for learning faster through small experiments, but its language was flattened into slogans like “ship fast.” In that shift, many teams treated MVP as permission to launch broken products and treated pivoting as a way to justify aimless changes.

It argues that the original approach depends on testing the riskiest assumption, gathering trustworthy user data, and using speed as learning speed rather than shipping speed. The post frames Lean Startup as a craft built on rigor and humility, not a shortcut for sloppy execution.

## Fichamento

- Lean Startup started as a way to reduce waste by testing the riskiest assumption with small, rigorous experiments.
- The MVP was meant to be the simplest version of an idea that could validate a core hypothesis, not a sloppy prototype.
- Over time, the method was reduced to slogans like “ship fast,” and some founders used “Lean” to justify weak products and rushed launches.
- The pivot was originally tied to validated learning, but it is now often treated as a cover for struggling or unfocused changes.
- The text describes a pattern in which many MVPs became Minimum Viable Disasters, with buggy products and poor user experiences.
- It says customers should remain learning partners in a scientific process, not guinea pigs for broken products.
- The distortion is linked to cultural copying, investor pressure, and confusion between learning speed and shipping speed.
- To recover the method’s intent, the post asks founders to identify the riskiest assumption, design the simplest test, check whether user data is trustworthy, and ask whether they are actually learning.
- The post closes by presenting Lean Startup as a disciplined craft that requires rigor, humility, and respect for users.
