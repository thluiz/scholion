The thread argues that asking for a candidate's current salary in interviews is a critical and morally questionable practice because it weakens the candidate's negotiation position. It explains how companies set salary ranges based on supply, demand, and internal constraints, and how knowing a candidate’s present pay helps them keep offers low even when the role might justify much higher compensation. Instead of answering, candidates are urged to share their salary expectations as a range, framed confidently and respectfully, and to use a counter-question about confidential customer pricing if pressed.

## Reading notes

- The author introduces the interview question "What is your current salary?" as common, critical, and morally questionable, and sets out to explain how to handle it.
- Before tackling the question, he outlines how the value of work is determined in a simplified way to show why honest disclosure puts candidates at a disadvantage.
- He states that the value of work is individual and depends partly on supply and demand, particularly in local markets.
- Even employees doing the same job at the same company are unlikely to be paid exactly the same, underscoring salary variability.
- Companies differ in revenue, operating costs, and leadership, yet all aim to minimize costs and maximize profits within some limits.
- When hiring, companies create a budget that reflects what they are willing to pay for a role, based on experience, supply and demand, and other factors.
- There is a lower bound below which candidates will not be interested, and an upper bound above which the company feels it gives away money it could spend elsewhere.
- Firms try to find a salary “sweet spot” that attracts enough candidates to fill one or multiple positions, and multiple hires for similar roles may still earn different amounts.
- Although a company has a salary range in mind, it will negotiate based on interview performance, prior experience, and the candidate’s own expectations.
- If a candidate asks for less than what the company had budgeted, this is considered a win for the company.
- Companies often avoid stating their salary range publicly because it would weaken their negotiating position.
- Knowing this, the author labels the current-salary question as a trick: if someone earns $100k, the company can resist paying $150k for a similar job.
- Revealing current salary weakens a candidate’s position in all subsequent negotiations with that employer.
- Recruiters often justify the question by saying they need to see if the candidate fits the company’s salary expectations, but the author calls this a poor excuse since they could ask for expectations instead.
- Once the company knows your current pay, they can frame a large raise (like 50%) as excessive and unjustified, shifting focus away from whether you were underpaid.
- The author rejects the idea that even a 20% raise is automatically a big win, emphasizing that the value of work is subjective and company-dependent.
- The same work can produce different profits at different companies depending on marketing, sales, customer base size, and customers’ willingness to pay.
- If one company can sell your work for more, it can also afford to pay you more for essentially the same contribution.
- He states that employees deserve a share of the value they create, which is why they show up to work.
- As a result, answering the current-salary question honestly is not a great option; instead, candidates should avoid it and state salary expectations.
- The author advises neither disclosing the current salary nor lying about it.
- He warns that if a lie about previous salary becomes known to a manager or HR, it can make future raise negotiations harder.
- Both company and candidate aim to find a common base for negotiation; if expectations do not align, the role is not a fit.
- He argues that walking away to find another job is better than underselling oneself.
- From his experience, anyone who revealed their current salary found it much harder to negotiate a fair offer afterward.
- Therefore, he recommends politely declining to answer and instead offering salary expectations.
- He provides an example answer that begins by saying current salary is not relevant, while acknowledging the company’s need for a number to align expectations.
- In the example, the candidate states a target range (e.g., $140k–$170k) and notes that it can be refined once expectations and capabilities are clearer after interviews.
- The answer expresses confidence that the candidate is worth that range and promises not to disappoint in upcoming interview rounds.
- It ends by checking if the proposed range is acceptable to the company and whether expectations align.
- The author praises this answer because it declines to discuss current pay yet clearly states expectations and respects the company’s need to assess affordability.
- Giving a range instead of a single number signals willingness to negotiate depending on how well role expectations match the candidate.
- The formulation also demonstrates self-confidence and belief in being worth the requested compensation.
- The answer suggests that the candidate has thought carefully about their expectations, which supports their confidence.
- It offers the company an exit if they cannot work with the proposed range, allowing them to decline early.
- For situations where candidates worry they cannot refuse, or if recruiters insist on knowing current salary, he proposes a counter-question.
- He suggests asking how much the company charges a large customer on a custom plan, which they will likely refuse to disclose as internal information.
- The follow-up is to ask whether they would share that custom-pricing information with another customer.
- He asserts they would never do that because custom plans are negotiated and revealing such data could show they try to charge some customers more.
- The analogy is that your current salary is like the last customer’s deal, and the company is now asking for information they themselves would never share in a similar negotiation.
- This counter-question is meant to signal that the candidate recognizes the trap and chooses not to take it.
- In later replies, the author says he is only sharing his experience and is happy if it helps even one person.
- He clarifies that current salary has nothing to do with the value an employee will create at a new company and mainly enables companies to buy employees cheaply while profiting from their work.
- He notes that the question is still being asked and that he would like to see it disappear because it harms candidates in the long run.
- He agrees with a commenter that this practice is shady and disproportionately affects underrepresented groups and minorities.
