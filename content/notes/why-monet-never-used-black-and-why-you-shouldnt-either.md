Monet and other Impressionists treated color as a relationship between hues, viewer, and medium. The post argues that black is often a weaker choice for contrast because hue shifts, temperature, and context create richer depth and more lifelike results.

## Reading notes

- Monet avoided black for nearly his entire painting career, and the post uses that choice to argue for more dynamic color use in design.
- Impressionists explored the limits of paint, brushes, and canvas, and their medium shaped the blurrier look of their work.
- They placed colors next to each other to create the illusion of a desired color, much like dithering in a limited palette.
- Pointillism is presented as an extension of that approach, using dots of color to produce a different overall color effect.
- The post says the Impressionists were studying the space between subject and viewer, including how light, water, and shadow are perceived.
- Color theory is used to explain warm and cool hues, complementary colors, tints, and shades.
- Warm hues tend to advance toward the viewer, while cool hues recede.
- Tints tend to pop forward, while shades tend to recede.
- Context changes the effect of the same color, so a tint or shade does not behave the same way in every setting.
- Hue relationships can overpower simple tint or shade differences.
- Black contrast can produce vibration at the edges of a shape, while a dark hue with a related color can create a more natural sense of depth.
- In typography, the post recommends using color relationships to build hierarchy instead of defaulting to black on white.
- It gives examples where a warm dark base color and related secondary colors create hierarchy with less harsh contrast.
- For interface graphics, it recommends using richer color relationships in highlights, shadows, and text to make buttons feel more lively.
