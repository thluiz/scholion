Kaplan-Moss argues that the split between “great” and “terrible” developers is false and destructive. He says the myth of the exceptional programmer pushes people into unhealthy work habits, discourages careers in technology, and reinforces narrow hiring patterns.

He also argues that average developers are the norm, not the exception, and that teams need a lower entry bar. The article connects this to Microsoft, which made useful work possible for average programmers, and to a broader slowdown in the idea that developers must be exceptional to matter.

## Reading notes

- The article rejects the “rock star” versus weak developer split as false.
- Labeling developers as only “great” or “terrible” leaves no middle ground.
- That pressure can drive punishing work hours and discourage people from entering tech.
- Kaplan-Moss says most developers are average, and that is normal.
- The “10X” myth can push recruiters toward white males who “look like a programmer.”
- The myth helps keep diversity and honest developers out.
- The article argues for a lower bar that treats average skill as valuable.
- It says the expectation of constant passion and nonstop study creates an unhealthy monoculture.
- Microsoft is presented as an example of making programming and administration accessible to average developers.
- The piece links the myth to the full-stack developer trend and to excessive fragmentation in technology.
