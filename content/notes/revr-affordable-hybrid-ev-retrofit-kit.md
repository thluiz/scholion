Australian design student Alexander Burton created REVR, a prototype kit to cheaply convert petrol or diesel cars into hybrid electric vehicles, winning Australia’s national James Dyson Award. Instead of removing the existing engine and hardware, the system adds an axial flux motor at the rear wheels plus a battery and controller in the spare wheel well or boot, aiming to work with most cars for around AU$5,000.

Burton was motivated by the high cost of conventional conversions, his family’s reliable 2001 Toyota, and the desire to cut emissions from fuel use and new car manufacturing. REVR targets a small battery providing about 100 kilometres of electric range, which he argues covers typical daily driving and yields major emission cuts with minimal vehicle changes. The design, developed using FEMM and MOTORXP, places the rotor between the disc brakes and uses existing mounting points for the stator, with pedal-position sensing for acceleration and regenerative braking so the hydraulic system remains untouched. He plans to use his prize money to build a working prototype, hopes to eventually retrofit a million cars, and criticises the automotive industry for preferring planned obsolescence over repair and retrofitting.

## Reading notes

- REVR (Rapid Electric Vehicle Retrofits) is a prototype kit to convert combustion cars to hybrid electric at lower cost.
- Burton estimates current conversion services average around AU$50,000 and are mostly used for valuable classic cars.
- Typical conversions remove the internal combustion engine, gearbox, hydraulic brakes and replace them with batteries and electric motors.
- REVR leaves these components in place and adds a flat axial flux motor between the rear wheels and disc brakes.
- The battery and controller are intended to fit in the car’s spare wheel well or boot.
- Additional off-the-shelf systems for brake and steering boosting, e-heating and air conditioning go under the hood.
- Burton believes this approach can bring the kit price down to roughly AU$5,000 and suit almost any car.
- He studies industrial design and sustainable systems engineering at RMIT University and developed REVR largely outside coursework.
- The idea originated when he and his father considered converting their 2001 Toyota, which they saw as reliable but expensive to electrify.
- He aims to offer an affordable option that reduces emissions from fuel combustion and from manufacturing new electric vehicles.
- Burton notes studies estimating manufacturing emissions for new electric cars can exceed those of traditional vehicles.
- REVR is designed to extend the usable life of existing cars by several years.
- The kit converts vehicles to hybrids, not full EVs, with a small battery target of 100 kilometres electric range.
- He argues this limited range delivers strong emission reductions with few modifications to the car.
- Burton cites Australian drivers averaging 35 kilometres a day, mainly commuting, so a modest battery can cover typical use.
- He calculates that a 5 kWh battery would cover this distance, and that the wheel well can fit about three times that capacity.
- Motor design relied on FEMM and MOTORXP modelling tools.
- In the design, the rotor sits between the vehicle’s disc brakes.
- The stator is attached using existing mounting points on the brake hub.
- A sensor reading accelerator pedal position controls both acceleration and braking, similar to existing hybrids.
- This setup avoids modifying the hydraulic braking system, which Burton says should not be disrupted.
- The concept won the national James Dyson Award for exceptional student design in Australia.
- The international Dyson Award winner from 30 participating countries was due to be announced on October 18.
- Burton plans to spend his AU$8,800 prize money on a small CNC machine and specialised materials to build a working prototype.
- He previously produced a non-working prototype in RMIT’s workshop.
- His long-term “stretch goal” is to retrofit one million cars using REVR.
- Burton is open to partnering with automotive industry players but criticises their lack of investment in retrofitting.
- He likens this to resistance to repairability and says industry prefers planned obsolescence.
- He argues retrofitting undermines profit margins by extending product lifetimes.
- As a result, he sees existing retrofitting companies as mostly focused on classic cars due to high costs.
- The article notes earlier James Dyson Award winners such as an infection-sensing wound dressing and a fish-waste-based bioplastic.
