Elle Griffin describes leaving Substack for a custom-built platform focused on long-form reading. She argues that Substack has shifted from a quiet reading environment to a noisy social media-style app dominated by algorithmic feeds, short posts, and discovery features that weaken the connection between writers and readers. Her metrics show subscriber counts rising while the proportion of subscribers reading each essay falls, with many app users missing posts unless they arrive by email. She frames this as a broader problem with attention, arguing that reading and social media scrolling require incompatible modes of focus and should not share the same space.

She decided to build her own platform after meeting writers who run custom sites that function more like traditional email newsletters and comment spaces. Using Claude to help with development, she created a site optimized for reading, long attention spans, and deep discussion, adding features Substack does not offer: print pamphlet subscriptions without mandatory email, sliding-scale and series-specific subscriptions, a Commons discussion area with sentence-level comments and “laurels,” and a membership program that rewards spending, comments, and referrals with perks such as merchandise, comment prominence, permanent access to paid posts, and even future board seats and profit sharing. She also replaced Google Docs with an integrated writing and research backend.

Griffin details the risks of leaving Substack, especially losing subscribers during email domain migration. She treats Substack as a social media outpost, continuing to post there while routing all email through elysian.press and redirecting URLs from her former custom domain on Substack. To reduce spam filtering, she ran a staged “warming” campaign: first notifying readers on Substack that she was leaving, then emailing small cohorts from the new domain and asking them to reply, so providers would classify the messages as legitimate. She used ZeroBounce to audit 6,865 inactive addresses, removed 1,261 defunct or high-risk emails, and placed 5,604 non-responsive but valid addresses into the final wave with a targeted message explaining the move and inviting them to remain subscribed.

The warming campaign produced a 43% overall open rate, over 1,000 replies, only six spam reports, and modest list loss, leaving her with a baseline of 21,793 subscribers. She describes the emotional impact of receiving many affirming emails from long-time readers. Looking ahead, she sees AI tools like Claude enabling writers to build their own software rather than depending on large tech platforms, and she wants to cultivate an analog-leaning, reader-centric culture centered on pamphlets, books, and thoughtful comment sections. She credits Substack with briefly proving that people will pay for reading, but argues that its evolution toward mass-market social media leaves room for smaller projects that serve readers exclusively and allow writers to truly control their work and attention.

## Reading notes

- Griffin left Substack for a custom platform because she believes it has become a social media app that distracts users from long-form reading.
- She contrasts Substack’s early period, when the app centered on essays and comments, with the current noisy interface filled with short posts, notifications, and algorithmic recommendations.
- Her husband’s experience of subscribing to only two newsletters and still not seeing their posts in the app illustrates how the feed obscures subscribed content.
- She argues the world spent decades building social media and instead needs an “anti-social media platform” that supports quiet, focused reading and life offline.
- Substack’s shift toward discovery and algorithmic feeds led her to stop reading in the app, then to feel it was no longer the right place to write either.
- Her subscriber count grew from 8,300 to 22,700 in three years, yet average readers per essay grew far less, so a smaller fraction of subscribers read each post.
- She notes that Substack “followers” tend to engage mainly with social posts, with only a small portion reading any given essay.
- Many subscribers with email turned off were expected to read in the app, but her tests show they largely do not open essays there.
- Comparing email sends to app posts, she finds that sending essays as emails reaches thousands more people and generates significantly more opens than publishing them only in the app.
- This leads her to conclude that many subscribers only see her work in their inbox and miss it when it appears primarily in the app feed.
- She questions the value of Substack’s widely touted subscription numbers if a large share of those subscribers do not actually read.
- Griffin argues that Substack no longer delivers on its early promise of letting writers “own their audience,” as the platform now optimizes for social posting and network effects.
- She attributes these changes to Substack’s need to serve investors and a mass market, which pushes it toward a pure social media model.
- She claims most people are scrollers rather than readers, and that Substack now caters to scrollers by design.
- Because of this environment, she feels writers focused on reading now need almost double the number of free subscribers to achieve the same readership as before.
- She insists that long-form writing and social media scrolling depend on incompatible forms of attention and should not coexist within one platform.
- Referring to her earlier essay about fixing the phone, she links addictive phone features like infinite scroll and notifications to anti-social behavior and calls for media platforms that avoid these mechanisms.
- She defines the alternative as dedicated reading platforms that prioritize essays, deep focus, and meaningful discussion.
- A backpacking trip with successful writers who use custom platforms showed her that she did not have to accept whatever tech companies provided.
- Those writers described systems where people subscribe after enjoying an essay, receive posts directly in their inbox in chronological order, and discuss via comments rather than social feeds.
- As Claude’s capabilities improved, she used it first to build a personal reading app and pamphlet shop, then to create her own integrated writing and reading platform.
- She designed the new site to optimize for reading, extended attention, and member discourse, modeling it on the idea of an online Enlightenment-era literary salon.
- Griffin likens leaving Substack to replacing a dead fridge, saying she would not have changed systems while Substack still worked for her, but saw an opportunity once it no longer fit.
- The new platform replicates Substack’s core functions of newsletters, website, and paid subscriptions while enabling extra features she wanted.
- She now offers an analog print pamphlet subscription that does not require an email subscription.
- Subscription options include a sliding scale and series-specific plans, allowing readers to commit to a single series rather than an entire year.
- The site hosts the Commons, a separate discussion space for subscribers, and introduces sentence-level commenting plus “laurels” that readers can award to highlight strong contributions.
- She frames the project as a social club, with a membership program that grants points for spending, commenting, and referrals, multiplied by subscriber tenure.
- Membership tiers provide benefits such as merchandise, boosted visibility in comment threads, permanent access to paid posts, and eventual board seats and profit sharing.
- She notes that her business currently reinvests all revenue, so profit sharing is a speculative, long-term reward.
- On the backend, she built tools that let her replace Google Docs with a more organized environment for writing and research.
- Griffin acknowledges the risk of leaving Substack’s discovery ecosystem, since many recent subscribers found her there and some readers still prefer the app.
- She describes the new platform as a personal passion project oriented around writing, thinking, and printing pamphlets rather than a mass-market news operation.
- To manage the transition, she chose to keep using Substack purely as a social media channel, posting notes that link back to her work.
- She exports her Substack content daily into the new site so new Substack signups will still join her list, but all emails now originate from elysian.press.
- She removed the custom domain from Substack and restored it to her own site, redirecting all former Substack URLs at that domain to equivalent pages on elysian.press.
- A key technical risk is that switching sending domains causes email providers to flag initial messages as spam, potentially cutting off many subscribers.
- She cites other writers’ migrations, such as Molly White, Today in Tabs, and Garbage Day, to show how domain setup issues and spam filters can reduce open rates and shrink lists.
- To avoid similar problems, she built a “warming” campaign so spam filters would learn to trust her new domain before she resumed full essay sends.
- The plan began with a notice on Substack about her departure and instructions to check spam folders for messages from the new address.
- She then sent her first emails from the new platform to small groups, asking recipients to reply, which signals engagement to spam filters.
- Claude helped determine the order of outreach, prioritizing the most active and engaged subscribers.
- She identified a segment of 6,865 Substack subscribers who had not opened emails or clicked links in at least a year and treated them as a special case.
- Concerned that emailing so many inactive addresses from a new domain could trigger spam flags, she decided to verify them using ZeroBounce.
- ZeroBounce reported that 5,604 of the inactive addresses were valid, including standard inboxes, read-it-later and newsletter app addresses, privacy aliases, role-based accounts, and partially verified domains.
- She retained those 5,604 addresses and scheduled them for the last wave of her warming campaign with a tailored message referencing their Substack signup.
- ZeroBounce also identified 1,261 addresses as problematic due to non-existent mailboxes, full inboxes, dead domains, servers that reject mail, obvious typos, spam-flag histories, temporary inboxes, spam traps, and other toxic markers.
- She removed these 1,261 addresses from her list before migrating, shrinking the risk surface when sending from the new domain.
- Griffin shares a chart of the warming waves and summarizes that the results were very positive.
- Across all waves, she achieved an overall open rate of 43%, over 1,000 replies, only six spam complaints, and modest attrition from unsubscribes and bounces.
- After the cleanup and warming process, she begins life on the new platform with 21,793 subscribers, which she describes as a healthy starting point.
- She expresses strong gratitude to readers who replied, clicked links, and pulled messages out of spam during the warming phase.
- The volume of kind responses, including notes from long-time readers and poems inspired by her work, reassured her that her writing is resonating.
- Griffin is enthusiastic about the creative possibilities of having a custom platform where she and her community can shape the experience.
- She argues that Claude opens a new era in which writers and other creative people can build their own software worlds instead of deferring to developers’ choices.
- Personally, she remains committed to writing and reading, to printed pamphlets and books, and to cultivating long attention spans.
- She imagines a community where readers sit quietly to read and think, and then engage one another through substantial comment threads.
- Griffin describes the current moment as a kind of renaissance that Substack initially sparked by proving that there is still a paying audience for reading.
- She believes Substack’s evolution toward broader social media leaves room for smaller efforts that design specifically for readers and analog sensibilities.
- She sees an opportunity to experiment with media formats that diverge from conventional platforms and wants to explore these directions with her audience.
- She insists that writers should indeed reclaim their attention and ownership of their work but says that no platform will fully deliver this without writers building it themselves.
- She closes by thanking readers for their support of The Elysian and noting that she will soon answer questions from an AMA prompted during the warming campaign.
