Monica Lent shares how she is preparing to launch her newsletter on Product Hunt by redesigning the landing page to better convert visitors into email subscribers. She moves from a mostly long-form, personal intro toward a clearer, more structured page with early signup forms, explicit audience targeting, social proof, and examples of content, while keeping the long-form “why” section that subscribers already responded well to.

## Reading notes

- She wants to get better at "building in public" and is preparing her first Product Hunt launch, starting by adapting her homepage based on lessons from launching on Twitter.
- The product is her newsletter, so the page’s main goal is collecting email addresses; the original version had a bold headline followed by long-form text, which some people liked and cited as their reason for subscribing.
- In the original layout visitors had to scroll a lot before seeing the first email form; for Product Hunt visitors who already saw a description, she adds an upfront, very clear explanation of what the newsletter helps users do and who it is for.
- She places a compact, inline signup form near the top so people can quickly decide if it’s for them and opt in; she adds subtext about "Join over 2,000 developers" to signal legitimacy.
- Before returning to the long-form explanation, she inserts supportive tweets from existing subscribers as social proof, noting that public, unprompted tweets seem more genuine and were not available when she first launched.
- The long-form section explaining the "Why" remains unchanged from the live homepage because readers told her they liked it and subscribed because of it.
- Further down the page, she changes the layout so that after the long-form copy there is another chance to subscribe followed by a "Why it matters" section with testimonials, instead of just an "About" section.
- For testimonials, she asks people matching different subscriber personas, such as @swyx for a career-growth-for-devs angle and @dqmonn as a SaaS founder and indie hacker.
- She wants readers who keep scrolling to decide that people like them subscribe to this kind of newsletter, and also to feel reassured that it is legitimate and not a scam.
- Both old and new versions include a photo of her and a short background section that aim to build credibility and make the page feel more human.
- The new version adds sample content, including examples from blog posts, interviews, and specific topics subscribers can expect, which is meant for visitors who want a deeper look even though she does not publish email archives.
- The page ends with a final subscription form and footer links; she keeps navigation in the footer to reduce distraction, whereas previously the site had no navigation because it was a one-page site.
- She summarizes that these are the changes she plans before launching on Product Hunt the following week and invites feedback, especially from people with Product Hunt experience, while sharing before/after screenshots of the page.
- She notes that the new landing page is not live yet and encourages others that if they want to do a similar launch, now is a good time to start.
- In replies, she thanks someone for sharing the project on Twitter and compliments another person’s complex build, showing ongoing engagement with her audience.
- Later she mentions adding a banner on a few pages to link to Product Hunt and is unsure whether it should be sticky, since she finds most sticky elements annoying.
- Close to launch she lists her prep steps: schedule the Product Hunt launch at 12AM PST, schedule an email announcement, deploy the site with the banner, mention the launch in a tweet, and add a milestone on Indie Hackers, and she asks if she is missing anything.
