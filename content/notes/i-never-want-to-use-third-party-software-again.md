The essay argues that software is moving toward hyperpersonalization. The author shows this through their own tools, from an agent app built for eight terminals to a Duolingo-like app for their kids, and says the point is both workflow fit and self-expression.

## Reading notes

- The author says software used to feel shared and uniform, but AI is changing that.
- Personalization already shapes names, entertainment, and recommendations.
- Most software still keeps personalization in settings menus, which limits what users can change.
- The author built a mobile app for managing AI agents and then a desktop version tailored to their own workflow.
- The app uses Claude and Codex together, keeps eight agents in a grid, and removes features the author does not use.
- The author describes this as a feedback loop where they can change friction points immediately.
- The essay links this feeling to the IKEA effect, where people value what they assemble themselves.
- The author also built a math and reading app for their children using stories tied to their real lives and interests.
- The kids could give feedback on difficulty and request specific story content.
- The author says personalized lessons helped students in a cited study solve algebra problems faster and more accurately.
- The essay gives two reasons hyperpersonalization will matter: workflow utility and self-expression.
- It frames software in levels, from specific use cases to platform builders to anything builders.
- The author recommends making interfaces malleable, letting preferences go beyond preset options, letting feedback change the product, and treating seams between tools as part of the experience.
- The closing claim is that software will increasingly be remixed to fit individual people.
